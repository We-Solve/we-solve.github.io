Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

Polynomial time means a bound polynomial in the finite input length; for an integer input, its binary length is proportional to its logarithm. A root modulo p of a fixed polynomial f is an element $a\in\mathbb F_p$ with $f(a)=0$. The sample polynomial is $x^3-2$. Since f is fixed, polynomial time is measured in the binary length of the prime p; only roots that exist can be output.

Target and attempted exponent route

Question 18 asks for a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm, for a fixed polynomial such as $X^3-2$, finding its roots modulo a varying prime $p$. Here polynomial time means polynomial in $\log p$ with the polynomial fixed. We prove unconditional elementary cases and a complete remaining root-extraction algorithm conditional on a supplied cubic nonresidue. We do not supply that nonresidue deterministically in the stated time for every prime, and do not infer such a construction from the source's complex-multiplication remark.
The finite-field group fact used below.
The multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic. A short proof is useful here. Let $e$ be the least common multiple of the orders of its elements. For each prime power dividing $e$ to its exact exponent, some element order contains that prime power; taking an appropriate power of the element gives precisely that order. Products of these elements have order the product of their pairwise coprime orders: if such a product raised to $k$ is one, isolating a factor and raising to the product of the other orders shows its order divides $k$ by coprimality. Thus an element of order $e$ exists. All group elements are roots of $X^e-1$, so their number is at most $e$; the element's distinct powers give the opposite inequality. It therefore generates the entire group. The root bound used here follows by repeatedly dividing a polynomial by $X-a$ for a root.
Unconditional branches.
For $p=2$ the only root is zero; for $p=3$ it is two, by direct evaluation of the field elements. If $p\equiv2\pmod3$, set $e=(2p-1)/3$. Then $3e=2(p-1)+1$, so $(2^e)^3=2$ by the order-$p-1$ multiplicative group identity. The cube map is bijective because multiplication by three permutes exponents modulo $p-1$. Thus this gives all roots, namely the unique one, using binary exponentiation in polynomial time.

For $p\equiv1\pmod3$, writing a generator as $w$ shows that $a\ne0$ is a cube exactly when $a^{(p-1)/3}=1$: both conditions say that its exponent relative to $w$ is divisible by three. This tests whether $2$ has any roots with polynomially many bit operations. If $p-1=3m$ with $3\nmid m$ and $2$ passes the test, choose $e$ inverse to three modulo $m$ (if $m=1$ set $e=0$). Then $2^m=1$ and $(2^e)^3=2$, so one root is found. Finding all three additionally needs a nontrivial cube root of unity; the one-root calculation alone has not constructed it.

A complete conditional algorithm for the other cases.
Suppose $p-1=3^s m$, $s\ge1$, $3\nmid m$, and a noncube $z\in\mathbb F_p^*$ is supplied. Set $c=z^m$. Relative to a generator, the exponent of $z$ is not divisible by three, so $c$ has exact order $3^s$. Assume $a=2$ passes the cube test. Choose $e$ with $3e\equiv1\pmod m$, using $e=0$ when $m=1$, and put $b=a^e$, $u=a/b^3$. The exponent $1-3e$ is a multiple of $m$, so $u$ belongs to the cyclic subgroup generated by $c$. Moreover $a$ and $b^3$ are cubes, so $u$ is a cube. In this subgroup that says $u=c^{3j}$ for a unique $j$ modulo $3^{s-1}$: if $c=w^{mk}$ with $3\nmid k$, then $c^j$ is a cube precisely when $3\mid j$.

This discrete logarithm in a power-of-three group can be computed digit by digit. Put $h=c^3$, of order $3^{s-1}$. If $s=1$, then $u=1$ and set $j=0$. Otherwise start $J=0$ and, for $k=0,\ldots,s-2$, form \[ R=u h^{-J},\qquad D=R^{3^{s-2-k}},\qquad \omega=h^{3^{s-2}}. \] Choose the unique $d_k\in\{0,1,2\}$ for which $D=\omega^{d_k}$, and replace $J$ by $J+d_k3^k$. To verify this procedure, write the true $j$ in base three. If its first $k$ digits agree with $J$, then $j-J=d_k3^k+3^{k+1}v$. Consequently $R^{3^{s-2-k}}=h^{d_k3^{s-2}}$, since the remaining exponent is divisible by $3^{s-1}$. The three powers of $\omega$ are distinct because its order is three. This proves the inductive digit step, and at the end $u=h^J$. Therefore \[ x=b c^J\quad\hbox{satisfies}\quad x^3=b^3u=a. \] All roots are $x,x\xi,x\xi^2$, where $\xi=c^{3^{s-1}}$ has order three. They are distinct and exhaust the roots because a cubic has at most three roots. There are $s\le\log_3(p-1)$ digit steps, each using modular powers with $O(\log p)$-bit exponents and arithmetic, so the conditional algorithm is polynomial in $\log p$.

First missing unconditional step and audit

The primary Question 18 was inspected. The proof locates the precise obstruction in this route: an unconditional deterministic polynomial-time construction of a cubic nonresidue, or another mechanism avoiding it. Trying $z=1,2,3,\ldots$ and testing each eventually succeeds, but no polynomial-in-$\log p$ bound for this search is proved here. Existence follows from cyclicity, since two thirds of nonzero elements are noncubes; that density does not bound their first position in a deterministic ordered search. Random sampling is not the requested deterministic algorithm. The audit distinguishes finding one root from all roots and handles $m=1$, $s=1$ and the primes two and three separately. The complete argument is algebraic; finite exact checks described below test implementation but not the unresolved bound. Exact Python integer arithmetic checked every supplied noncube for each prime below 500 congruent to one modulo three for which 2 is a cube. There were 2,664 input pairs across 15 primes. In every case all three outputs agreed with direct enumeration of $x^3=2$ in the field. The recorded prime list and case count are in the accompanying audit.
