Identifiability does not supply a uniform sample bound

The primary report restores the model absent from the short extract: $x=W^Th+\sigma\xi$, with $h\in\{0,1\}^d$, $d\le m$ and $W$ of row rank $d$. Its tensor method has exponential dependence on $d$ because it enumerates tensor eigenvectors. We test whether identifiability alone can justify a sample bound polynomial in $d$. Take the noiseless submodel $m=d\ge1$, $\sigma=0$, and independent hidden bits, with $\Pr(h_1=1)=p=2^{-d}$ and all other bits unbiased. Compare $W_0=I_d$ and $W_1=\operatorname{diag}(2,1,\ldots,1)$. Both have full rank and their observed distributions are distinct, hence the two-point parameter family is identifiable.
Proposition: The total variation distance between the distributions of $N$ independent observations under these two parameters is $1-(1-p)^N\le Np$. Any estimator that, under both parameters, estimates the first diagonal entry with absolute error less than $1/2$ with probability at least $2/3$ therefore needs $N\ge2^d/3$.
Proof: When every sampled first hidden bit is zero, the observation laws agree exactly; this event has probability $(1-p)^N$ in both worlds. On the complementary event, the first observed coordinate takes a nonzero value at least once. That value is 1 in the first world and 2 in the second, so the two complementary supports are disjoint. This decomposition gives the exact total variation distance. The bound by $Np$ follows from the union bound over the rare-bit events. The success intervals for the two first diagonal entries are disjoint. Turn the estimator into a test by accepting the first world whenever its estimate lies within $1/2$ of 1. This event has probability at least $2/3$ in the first world and at most $1/3$ in the second, so total variation is at least $1/3$. Combining inequalities yields $Np\ge1/3$ and the stated sample lower bound. ∎

Verification and remaining gaps

For small $d,N$, every sequence of first-bit observations was enumerated to obtain $1-(1-2^{-d})^N$ exactly, and the bound was checked. This rules out an inference from bare identifiability to a uniform polynomial-in-$d$ sample guarantee. It does not disprove the source's computational target: an algorithm polynomial in the number of supplied samples and in $d$ could still be consistent for every fixed identifiable model as sample size tends to infinity. Quantitative nondegeneracy may also exclude this rare-component family. No such algorithm or computational lower bound is obtained.
