Research attempt: Common model distributions prevent uniform tree selection

Choose a finite-state general Markov model on any tree, and let every edge transition matrix have all rows equal to the same strictly positive probability vector $\pi$. Each child is then drawn from $\pi$ independently of its parent. Multiplying the conditional probabilities and summing hidden variables shows the leaf distribution is $\prod_{i\in\text{leaves}}\pi(x_i)$, independently of the tree. This holds for each of the three distinct four-leaf binary topologies, even though all transition entries are positive.

Given any selector based on $N$ independent sites, its output has the same law under these three parameterized trees. The probabilities of outputting the three labels sum to at most one, so at least one tree has success probability at most $1/3$. No finite sample size, or even access to the exact leaf distribution, identifies the originating topology at these parameters. This is an identifiability obstruction, not a computational hardness result.

A quantitative version explains why nearby regimes need a separation margin. For two distributions $p,q$ on a finite alphabet, define $d_{TV}(p,q)=\frac12\sum_x|p(x)-q(x)|$. Expanding a product difference one factor at a time and summing absolute values gives $d_{TV}(p^{\otimes N},q^{\otimes N})\le N d_{TV}(p,q)$. For any decision event $A$, the sum of its two classification errors is $p^{\otimes N}(A^c)+q^{\otimes N}(A) \ge1-d_{TV}(p^{\otimes N},q^{\otimes N})$. The event bound follows because the sum of positive entries of $p-q$ equals its total variation. Thus arbitrarily close competing distributions preclude a uniform finite-sample guarantee at fixed $N$.

For mutation models nested by parameter restriction, data generated at their intersection similarly do not identify an originating model label. A criterion such as the smallest adequate model is a separate convention. The source's "best" and "most accurate" cannot be replaced by unique label recovery without such choices.
