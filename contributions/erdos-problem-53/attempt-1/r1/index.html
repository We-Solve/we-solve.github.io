Erdős Problem #53: a rank reduction for sums and products of distinct elements

Independent attempt, 23 September 2026.

1) Statement and known answer

For a finite set $A$ of integers, let $S(A)$ and $P(A)$ be the sets of sums and products of nonempty subsets of $A$. The question asks whether, for each fixed $k$, all sufficiently large $A$ satisfy $|S(A)\cup P(A)|\ge|A|^k$. The current editorial attributes the affirmative answer to Chang. This note establishes elementary lower bounds, solves a high-multiplicative-rank subcase, and identifies the low-rank part not covered by those arguments. No novelty is claimed.

2) A quadratic supply of distinct subset sums

If $0<b_1<\cdots<b_r$ are distinct positive integers, their subset sums, including the empty sum, number at least $1+r(r+1)/2$.

Proof is by induction. Let $T=b_1+\cdots+b_{r-1}$ and let $S_{r-1}$ be the previous subset sums. They all lie in $[0,T]$. For each of the $r$ distinct choices \[ x\in\{0,b_1,\ldots,b_{r-1}\}, \] the number $T-x$ is a previous subset sum, and \[ b_r+T-x>T \] because $x<b_r$. Thus adding $b_r$ supplies at least $r$ distinct new sums above $T$. Starting with the single empty sum proves the claim. Excluding that sum leaves at least $r(r+1)/2$ nonempty sums.

If $|A|=N$, discard zero if present. At least $r=\lceil(N-1)/2\rceil$ remaining elements have the same sign. Apply the preceding lemma directly to positive elements, or negate a negative subset. All its resulting sums are still sums of distinct members of $A$, up to that sign reversal. Consequently \[ |S(A)|\ge\frac{r(r+1)}2\ge\frac{N^2-1}{8} \] when $N$ is odd, and the stated $r$ formula gives a slightly stronger bound when $N$ is even. In particular $|S(A)|\gg N^2$. This alone does not meet the requested arbitrary power, nor even the exact coefficient-one bound $N^2$.

3) Independent prime valuations give exponentially many products

For a nonzero integer $a$ with $|a|>1$, write its prime-valuation vector as \[ v(a)=(v_p(|a|))_p, \] a finitely supported integer vector. Let $r_\times$ be the dimension over $\mathbb Q$ of the span of these vectors as $a$ ranges over $A$. Choose $r_\times$ elements whose vectors are linearly independent.

Their distinct subsets have distinct absolute products. Indeed, an equality of two absolute products gives \[ \sum_i\epsilon_i v(a_i)=0,\qquad\epsilon_i\in\{-1,0,1\}, \] and independence forces every $\epsilon_i=0$. Distinct absolute products also give distinct signed products, even if some selected elements are negative. Since every selected absolute value exceeds one, no nonempty product has absolute value one. Hence \[ |P(A)|\ge2^{r_\times}-1.\tag{1} \] For example, the problem's conclusion follows immediately whenever $r_\times\ge k\log_2N+1$. The extra one accommodates exclusion of the empty product; no empty-subset convention is being used to reach the target.

A useful directly checkable sufficient condition is an ordering $a_1,\ldots,a_r$ of nonzero elements of $A$, with primes $p_i$ such that $p_i\mid a_i$ and $p_i\nmid a_j$ for $j<i$. The valuation matrix with these primes as rows is triangular with nonzero diagonal, so these elements are multiplicatively independent and $1$ applies. Nonzero elements are required because zero has no finite prime-valuation vector.

4) What the low-rank reduction leaves

Any counterexample to the target inequality must have \[ 2^{r_\times}-1<N^k, \] and therefore $r_\times<\log_2(N^k+1)$. This is a real reduction, but not a proof: a rank bound of order $\log N$ still permits complicated exponent vectors and does not imply a sufficiently large set of subset sums by the elementary lemma above.

Low rank itself need not be an obstruction. If $N\ge2$ and $A=\{1,b,b^2,\ldots,b^{N-1}\}$ with integer $b\ge2$, its multiplicative rank is one, yet all $2^N$ subset sums are different. For an alleged equality, look at the highest power whose inclusion differs: it exceeds the sum of all smaller powers, which is $(b^j-1)/(b-1)<b^j$. Thus $|S(A)|=2^N-1$ with nonempty subsets. Meanwhile its subset products have only $N(N-1)/2+1$ values, because the exponents obtained as subset sums of $0,1,\ldots,N-1$ fill exactly the interval from zero to $N(N-1)/2$. This example illustrates the needed interaction of addition and multiplication rather than proving it for general low-rank sets.

5) Outcome and references

ATTEMPT UNRESOLVED for general $A$. The quadratic sum bound, exponential rank bound, and reduction to logarithmic multiplicative rank are proved. The principal low-rank additive argument needed to reproduce Chang's full theorem is not supplied.
