Zeta verification: bounded rational transcripts and a complete zero-dimensional certificate

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

For a variety $V$ over $\mathbb F_q$, its zeta function is $Z(V,T)=\exp(\sum_{r\ge1}\#V(\mathbb F_{q^r})T^r/r)$. The decision task is whether a supplied function equals this zeta function. Membership in NP requires a finite certificate verifiable in time polynomial in the encoded input length.

A certificate route and its assumptions

The original Question 15 asks about NP verification of a candidate zeta function for a variety over a finite field. An NP claim requires an encoding and certificate length polynomial in that encoding. The argument below gives a rational-function reduction and a concrete certificate for squarefree univariate zero-dimensional schemes. It does not provide a certificate for arbitrary varieties.
How many point counts determine a bounded rational function.
Suppose $Z_i=P_i/Q_i$ with $P_i(0)=Q_i(0)=1$, $\deg P_i\le a$, $\deg Q_i\le b$, for $i=1,2$. If their Taylor coefficients agree through degree $a+b$, then they are equal. Indeed $P_1Q_2-P_2Q_1$ has degree at most $a+b$. Multiplying $Z_1-Z_2$ by the series units $Q_1Q_2$ shows that this polynomial has all coefficients through that degree zero, so it is the zero polynomial. Conversely equality gives agreement.

For $Z(T)=\exp(\sum_{i\ge1}N_iT^i/i)=\sum_{k\ge0}z_kT^k$ over $\mathbb Q[[T]]$, differentiation yields \[ kz_k=\sum_{i=1}^k N_i z_{k-i},\qquad z_0=1. \] The formal chain rule proves the identity after equating the coefficient of $T^{k-1}$; division by $k$ recursively determines the coefficients. Thus counts through $a+b$ determine $Z$ if the stated numerator and denominator bounds are independently justified. They do not prove those bounds or certify the counts themselves.

An explicit certificate in a nontrivial special case.
Let $f\in\mathbb F_q[X]$ be monic, squarefree and of degree $D$. Take $V$ to be its zero set as a zero-dimensional scheme. A certificate is a list of distinct monic irreducible polynomials $f_j$ with $f=\prod_j f_j$. If $d_j=\deg f_j$, the claimed answer is \[ Z(V,T)=\prod_j(1-T^{d_j})^{-1}. \] Here is both its proof and a polynomial verification procedure. For a monic polynomial $h$ of degree $d\ge1$, check \[ h\mid X^{q^d}-X, \quad \gcd(h,X^{q^{d/r}}-X)=1 \quad\hbox{for each prime }r\mid d. \] These conditions characterize irreducibility. To prove this, the roots of $X^{q^k}-X$ form the $q^k$-element field in an algebraic closure: the polynomial has derivative $-1$ and degree $q^k$; its roots are closed under field operations by the Frobenius identities. A root of an irreducible degree-$e$ polynomial generates a field with $q^e$ elements, since the quotient has a basis of $e$ powers of the root. Its nonzero group has order $q^e-1$, so Lagrange's theorem shows that its Frobenius orbit is finite. For a root $\alpha$ its orbit under $x\mapsto x^q$ has length $e$ equal to its minimal-polynomial degree. The orbit product has coefficients fixed by Frobenius, hence in $\mathbb F_q$, while every polynomial over $\mathbb F_q$ vanishing at $\alpha$ vanishes on that orbit. The degree equality follows. Such a root lies in the $q^k$-element field exactly when its orbit length divides $k$.

The divisibility test therefore makes $h$ squarefree and forces every irreducible factor degree $e$ to divide $d$. If $e<d$, some prime $r$ divides $d/e$, so $e\mid d/r$ and that factor contradicts the gcd test. All factors must have degree $d$, leaving just one because $h$ itself has degree $d$. Conversely an irreducible $h$ of degree $d$ satisfies all tests by the orbit characterization. Degree one has no prime divisors to test and works by the same argument.

After verifying all factors, distinctness, and their product, a degree-$d_j$ factor contributes $d_j$ points over $\mathbb F_{q^k}$ if $d_j\mid k$, and zero otherwise, again by the orbit description. Its contribution to the exponent defining $Z$ is $\sum_{m\ge1}d_jT^{md_j}/(md_j)=\sum_{m\ge1}T^{md_j}/m$. Exponentiating gives $(1-T^{d_j})^{-1}$. This last formal identity follows by differentiation and constant term one, or by the geometric series. Multiplying the contributions proves the formula.

All tests have polynomial cost in $D$ and $\log q$ under an explicit finite-field representation with polynomial-cost field arithmetic. Repeated modular powering computes $X^{q^k}\bmod h$ using $O(k\log q)$ polynomial multiplications by binary exponentiation; $k\le D$. Euclidean gcd and dense polynomial products have polynomial cost. The prime divisors of $d\le D$ can be found by trial division through $D$, also polynomial in this parameter. The certificate lists at most $D$ total factor degrees. A candidate rational function can be compared with the displayed product by cross multiplication in polynomial time in its input length and $D$.

Where the general NP argument fails

For an affine variety in $n$ coordinates, $N_k\le q^{kn}$, so the integers $N_1,\ldots,N_B$ need at most $O(nB^2\log q+B)$ bits. Short numerical answers are not automatically short proofs. Listing every point to certify a count can already require $q$ entries for the affine line over $\mathbb F_q$, exponential in $\log q$. This only refutes that listing strategy; it does not refute the existence of other NP certificates. Our squarefree-factor certificate avoids enumeration in dimension zero, but no counterpart is supplied for general varieties or for certifying general cohomological degree bounds polynomial in the proposed input size.
