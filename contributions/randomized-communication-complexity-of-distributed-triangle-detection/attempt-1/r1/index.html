Vertex-owned triangle detection: aggregation collapse and sampling costs

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

A triangle is three distinct vertices with all three joining edges. In the described vertex-partition model each participant knows incident edges, so an edge has two informed endpoint participants.

Communication complexity measures the number of transmitted bits needed for parties holding separate inputs to compute a joint function. Randomized protocols allow random choices, with their error probability specified by the model.

In the LOCAL model vertices communicate synchronously along graph edges; messages and local computation are unrestricted and complexity is the number of rounds. After $t$ rounds a vertex can depend only on its radius-$t$ neighborhood and any supplied identifiers or randomness.

Verbatim supplied catalogue question

What is the randomized communication complexity of detecting a triangle when each graph vertex is assigned to a player who receives all incident edges, so each edge is known to its two endpoint players?

Source communication model

Oshman's report, printed pages 3078--3080, discusses both point-to-point and shared-blackboard communication before asking the vertex-owned triangle question. We retain that distinction. Each labeled vertex knows its incident edges in a finite simple undirected graph. The elementary upper bounds below are for a shared blackboard with total transmitted bits charged once. They are not round bounds for CONGEST or LOCAL. The cited 2025 primary abstract concerns CONGEST rounds and therefore cannot simply be substituted for the unspecified total-communication target in the catalogue.

A baseline protocol and the exact failure of two-party grouping

In fixed vertex order, vertex $i$ writes the bits for all pairs $\{i,j\}$ with $j>i$. The blackboard then contains the whole graph using exactly $\binom n2$ edge bits, without an extra sender label because the schedule is fixed. Every player can enumerate vertex triples and decide triangle existence. This deterministic protocol has no error.

For a lower-bound attempt, partition the vertices into two sets $A,B$ and give Alice all incident-edge inputs of vertices in $A$, and Bob all those in $B$. Each now knows every edge touching their own set; in particular, both know every crossing edge. Every triangle has at least two vertices in one of the two sets. If it has two in $A$, all its three edges touch $A$, so Alice can recognize it from her view. The analogous assertion holds for Bob and $B$. Alice locally tests whether a triangle has at least two vertices in $A$ and sends one bit; Bob does the corresponding test for $B$ and sends one bit. The OR of these two bits is exactly the answer. Empty parts cause no difficulty. Thus this aggregated two-party problem has a deterministic two-bit protocol for every partition.

This proves the obstruction behind a direct cut-based communication reduction: that aggregation hands each party enough overlapping edge information to destroy triangle hardness. It does not give a two-bit protocol to the original $n$ separate players, who do not individually possess the aggregated views. The duplication of crossing-edge knowledge is essential and cannot be replaced silently by an edge-disjoint input model.

A public-random wedge-sampling attempt

Let $d_v$ be each degree and let $W=\sum_v\binom{d_v}{2}$. Vertices first broadcast their degrees, using $O(n\log(n+1))$ bits. If $W=0$ there is no triangle. Otherwise public randomness chooses a vertex with probability $\binom{d_v}{2}/W$ and an index uniformly among its unordered neighbor pairs. That vertex broadcasts the two selected neighbor labels, using $O(\log(n+1))$ bits; one designated endpoint broadcasts whether their edge exists. The selected object is a uniformly random wedge, meaning an unordered pair of incident edges with its center specified.

If the graph has $T$ triangles, exactly $3T$ wedges are closed. Indeed each triangle contributes one centered wedge at each of its three vertices, and every closed wedge determines its triangle uniquely. Thus each independent trial detects a triangle with probability $3T/W$. There are no false positives. After $t$ trials the failure probability is $(1-3T/W)^t\le\exp(-3Tt/W)$; the same elementary inequality $1-u\le e^{-u}$ proves the bound. Subject to a lower bound on $T/W$ this is a useful protocol, but no such bound is in the source question.

In fact take the disjoint union of a complete bipartite graph $K_{a,b}$ and a triangle, with $a,b\ge1$. The bipartite component has no triangle because every three vertices include two in the same independent part. It has \[ a\binom b2+b\binom a2=\frac{ab(a+b-2)}2 \] wedges. The triangle adds three, so $T=1$ and $W=ab(a+b-2)/2+3$. For balanced $a,b$ growing with total vertex count, $W$ is of order $n^3$. By the union bound, $t$ trials have success probability at most $3t/W$, so success at least $2/3$ requires $t\ge2W/9$. Uniform wedge sampling therefore needs cubic many trials on this family and does not improve the quadratic full-graph communication bound. This is a failure of this sampler, not a randomized communication lower bound against arbitrary protocols.
