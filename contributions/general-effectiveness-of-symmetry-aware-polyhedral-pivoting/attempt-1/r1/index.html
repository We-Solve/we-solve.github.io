Research attempt: Exact quotient-graph enumeration and its unpriced operations

A correct orbit traversal under explicit access assumptions
Let $\Gamma=(V,E)$ be a finite connected undirected graph, and let a finite group $G$ act by graph automorphisms. Assume exact procedures to enumerate neighbors and to assign each vertex a canonical representative of its $G$-orbit. Start with the canonical representative of any vertex. Whenever an unprocessed representative $r$ is removed from a queue, enumerate every neighbor $u$ of $r$, canonicalize $u$, and enqueue its representative if unseen. Store all seen representatives.

This visits every orbit. For any edge $v\sim w$, choose $g$ sending $v$ to its representative $r$. Then $r\sim gw$, and $gw$ lies in the orbit of $w$. Consequently all quotient-graph neighbors of an orbit appear when its representative is processed. The quotient graph is connected because a path between original vertices maps to a walk between their orbits. Induction along such a walk proves every orbit is eventually discovered. Finiteness gives termination; at most one representative is processed per orbit. Loops and multiple quotient edges merely produce already-seen representatives.

With an explicitly listed group, a canonical representative can be defined as the lexicographically least exact encoding among $\{gv:g\in G\}$. This is correct and finite, but needs up to $|G|$ action/encoding comparisons. If $R$ is the chosen representative set, the algorithm makes exactly $\sum_{r\in R}\deg(r)$ neighbor-canonicalization calls, in addition to initialization. No polynomial output-sensitive bound follows until costs of these calls and the representation of $G$ are specified.

An example of a real saving and a reason it is not automatic
For the graph of the $n$-cube with bit-flip adjacency and coordinate-permutation group, the vertex orbits are exactly Hamming weights $0,1,\ldots,n$. A canonical representative is a string of that many ones followed by zeros, obtainable by counting bits. Neighbors change weight by one. One can therefore enumerate the $n+1$ orbits by a path, although the original graph has $2^n$ vertices. Even an implementation enumerating all $n$ neighbors of each representative uses only $n(n+1)$ neighbor calls; enumerating all $n!$ group permutations for every canonicalization would squander this saving. The useful ingredient is the inexpensive canonicalization invariant, not only the existence of a large group.

For the trivial group there is no orbit compression at all. For degenerate polyhedra, multiple bases can represent one geometric vertex, and a basis pivot graph can be much larger than the vertex graph. The graph algorithm counts whichever objects its input vertices encode; changing to geometric vertices needs a proof that adjacency and traversal remain complete. Likewise a perturbation that destroys the original automorphisms invalidates the quotient action used above, even if it makes ordinary pivots easier.

Verification and adversarial audit

Scope and remaining gaps
