Bounded subdeterminants: rational vertices and two exponential enumeration traps

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

An integer program optimizes a linear objective over $\{x\in\mathbb Z^n:Ax\leq b\}$ with finitely encoded integer constraint data. A square subdeterminant is the determinant of any square submatrix of $A$. The parameter $\Delta$ bounds their absolute values and is fixed independently of the dimensions. Polynomial time means polynomial in the total binary input length, including $b$ and the objective.

Statement recorded in the supplied source.
For a fixed integer $\Delta\geq3$, can integer programs whose constraint matrices have every square subdeterminant bounded in absolute value by $\Delta$ be solved in polynomial time? [S2]

Verbatim supplied catalogue question

For a fixed integer $\Delta\geq3$, can integer programs whose constraint matrices have every square subdeterminant bounded in absolute value by $\Delta$ be solved in polynomial time?

Attempted finite-denominator algorithm

Every square minor is bounded by a fixed $\Delta$, so in particular each entry of $A$ is bounded. It is tempting to enumerate the few possible fractional vertex types or search a bounded integer box. We derive the genuine denominator statement and then show why neither enumeration argument has a polynomial input-length bound.
Vertex denominators are bounded.
At a vertex $x$ of a full-dimensional polyhedron $Ax\le b$, the active row vectors span $\mathbb R^n$. Otherwise choose nonzero $h$ orthogonal to all active rows. Because there are finitely many inactive inequalities, both $x+th$ and $x-th$ remain feasible for sufficiently small $t>0$: choose $t$ below every positive slack divided by the corresponding nonzero directional absolute value. Then $x$ is their midpoint and is not a vertex, a contradiction. Choose $n$ independent active rows, forming an integer nonsingular matrix $B$. The equality $Bx=b_B$ gives $x=B^{-1}b_B=\operatorname{adj}(B)b_B/\det B$. The adjugate entries and $b_B$ are integral. Thus each coordinate denominator divides the nonzero integer $\det B$, whose absolute value is at most $\Delta$. In particular all vertices lie in $D^{-1}\mathbb Z^n$ for $D=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,\Delta)$. This does not imply a bounded number of vertices or nearby lattice points, or that coordinatewise rounding preserves inequalities.
Fractional-vertex rounding fails already at determinant three.
Minimize $x+y$ subject to \[ 2x+y\ge1,\qquad x+2y\ge1,\qquad0\le x,y\le1. \] The linear optimum is $(1/3,1/3)$ with value $2/3$: adding the first two inequalities gives $x+y\ge2/3$, and that point attains it. The constraint rows are $(-2,-1),(-1,-2),(1,0),(0,1),(-1,0),(0,-1)$. All one-by-one minors have absolute value at most two; the two special rows have determinant three, a special row and a coordinate row give absolute determinant at most two, and two coordinate rows give at most one. Hence every square minor is bounded by three. Rounding both coordinates down gives $(0,0)$, which violates both covering constraints. Rounding both up gives $(1,1)$ of value two, whereas $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ are feasible integer points of value one and no integer value below one is feasible. Even this small denominator does not make either uniform rounding rule an exact integer optimizer.
Enumerating all vertices or all box points is not enough.
The cube $[0,1]^n$ has exactly $2^n$ vertices: a point with a coordinate strictly between zero and one is a midpoint of a small coordinate perturbation, whereas a zero-one point cannot be a nontrivial convex combination of other cube points, coordinate by coordinate. Its matrix consists of rows $\pm e_i$ and all minors belong to $\{0,1,-1\}$: a square submatrix with a zero row or repeated nonzero column position has determinant zero; otherwise it is a signed permutation matrix. Thus even the stronger determinant-one promise permits exponentially many vertices. Listing all LP bases or vertices is not polynomial.

Likewise the one-dimensional interval $0\le x\le B$ has a determinant-one constraint matrix and $B+1$ integer points. If $B$ is binary encoded, listing them is exponential in $\log B$. These simple instances are themselves easy to optimize; they disprove only the proposed enumeration bounds, not polynomial solvability of the promised class. A residue-class list of size $D^n$ also has no polynomial bound at fixed $D>1$. We do not claim that all those residue patterns can independently occur under the full minor promise; using this loose list already loses the desired running time.
