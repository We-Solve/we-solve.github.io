Research-attempt provenance

Definitions and mathematical statement

Let $S\subset\mathbb R^3$ be a finite set with no four coplanar points. A tetrahedralization of $S$ is a face-to-face subdivision of $\operatorname{conv}(S)$ into nondegenerate tetrahedra whose vertex set is exactly $S$. In particular, every input point is used and no Steiner vertices are added.

A $2\leftrightarrow3$ bistellar flip replaces two tetrahedra sharing a triangular face by three sharing an edge, or reverses this operation. The five involved vertices must form a convex triangular bipyramid, and both local subdivisions must have the same support and boundary. Only replacements that remain geometric tetrahedralizations are legal.

Form a graph $\mathcal F(S)$ with one node per tetrahedralization and an edge for each legal flip. The question is whether $\mathcal F(S)$ is connected for every such $S$. Equivalently, can any two tetrahedralizations of the same point set be related by a finite sequence of these moves?

The point coordinates stay fixed throughout. Vertex-inserting $1\to4$ and vertex-deleting $4\to1$ moves are excluded. Connectivity of abstract triangulations, or connectivity restricted to a special class such as regular triangulations, does not settle the general geometric statement.

Short English statement

Can any two tetrahedralizations of the same points in three-dimensional space be connected by legal two-to-three and three-to-two flips, without moving, inserting, or deleting vertices?

Research attempt: A nonflippable locally non-Delaunay face

A natural route to connectivity is to fix a preferred triangulation and repair every locally bad face. The following five-point calculation shows why the elementary two-to-three face repair is not always available. Set \[ A=(0,0,0),\ B=(1,0,0),\ C=(0,1,0),\ D=(1/2,1/2,1),\ E=(7/10,7/10,-1/10). \] The tetrahedra $ABCD$ and $ABCE$ meet in $ABC$ with tips on opposite sides. The segment $DE$ meets $z=0$ at $(15/22,15/22,0)$, outside $ABC$ since its first two coordinates sum to $15/11>1$. Hence their union is not convex, and replacing this pair by the three tetrahedra around $DE$ is not a legal two-to-three move. To justify nonconvexity directly, this point lies on the segment between two points of the union; the union meets $z=0$ exactly in $ABC$.

Nevertheless $ABC$ fails the local empty-sphere test. The sphere through $A,B,C,D$ has center $(1/2,1/2,1/4)$ and squared radius $9/16$. The squared distance of $E$ to its center is $2(1/5)^2+(-7/20)^2=81/400<9/16$. Thus a local non-Delaunay face need not be repairable by flipping that face.

This example occurs inside a full tetrahedralization, not just an isolated nonconvex pair. The unique affine dependence can be written \[ \tfrac12 B+\tfrac12 C=\tfrac4{15}A+\tfrac1{15}D+\tfrac23E. \] All coefficients are positive. In particular the five points are in convex position, and every four are affinely independent: direct determinants for $ABCD,ABCE,ABDE,ACDE,BCDE$ are nonzero (the first four are immediate, and the dependence with all five coefficients nonzero then implies the fifth). The three tetrahedra \[ ABCD,\quad ABCE,\quad BCDE \] triangulate their convex hull around edge $BC$. For a detailed coverage check, start from any convex combination of the five points and add a multiple of the displayed affine dependence until one of the coefficients of $A,D,E$ first becomes zero. The resulting nonnegative combination lies in one of these three tetrahedra. Two different stopping choices can coincide only when at least two of those coefficients vanish, which is a shared face or lower-dimensional face; uniqueness of the one-dimensional affine dependence proves disjoint interiors.

There is a legal three-to-two flip around $BC$, yielding $ABDE$ and $ACDE$. Therefore this is not a disconnected flip graph or an isolated node. It refutes only the proposed face-by-face greedy argument and exhibits exactly the extra edge move that the argument must consider. A global descent proof would still need to guarantee an available improving move at every undesired triangulation.

Scope and remaining gaps

No global potential argument guaranteeing escape from every tetrahedralization has been proved, and no counterexample to connectivity has been constructed. The first gap is an improving legal move or a different connecting mechanism when an offending face itself is nonflippable.
