Random MAX-3SAT: an explicit high-density bound and the fixed-density gap

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

$n$ is the number of Boolean variables and $m$ the number of random clauses. $A(F)$ counts the clauses satisfied by the algorithm and $\operatorname{OPT}(F)$ the maximum satisfiable count; expectations are over the instance distribution and any algorithmic randomness.

Polynomial time means a number of elementary steps bounded by a polynomial in the encoded input length. A decision problem is in $\mathrm{NP}$ if yes-instances have polynomial-length certificates verifiable in polynomial time; NP-hardness is under polynomial-time reductions.

A $k$-CNF formula is a conjunction of clauses, each a disjunction of at most $k$ literals; a literal is a variable or its negation. Satisfiability asks whether a Boolean assignment makes every clause true. MAX-SAT instead maximizes the number or weight of satisfied clauses.

Verbatim supplied catalogue question

For fixed clause size $r$ and density $m/n$, design a polynomial-time algorithm for random MAX $R$-3SAT instances $F$ that returns an assignment with expected value $\mathbb{E}[A(F)]\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{OPT}(F)]$ for every $\varepsilon>0$.

Distribution and source scope

The primary discussion distinguishes approximating the optimum value from finding an assignment with a ratio guarantee. The cleaned question asks the stronger assignment guarantee in expectation. Its clause-size symbol $r$ is not a new parameter silently substituted for the source's three-literal clauses. We analyze the explicit model in which each clause independently chooses three uniform literals with replacement from the $2n$ literals. This makes precise one reading of the source's random choice; no claim is made for an unspecified different distribution.
A fixed assignment and a bound on the expected optimum.
Every fixed assignment makes exactly $n$ of the $2n$ literals false. All three draws are false with probability $1/8$, so the number $X_a$ of clauses satisfied by assignment $a$ has expectation $qm$, where $q=7/8$. Different clauses are independent. We claim \[ qm\le\mathbb E\operatorname{OPT}(F) \le qm+\sqrt{mn\log2/2}. \] The lower bound follows by using any fixed assignment. For the upper bound, if $B$ is a Bernoulli variable, the function $h(t)=\log\mathbb E e^{t(B-\mathbb EB)}$ has $h(0)=h'(0)=0$. Direct differentiation shows that $h''(t)$ is the variance of a Bernoulli variable with its exponentially tilted success probability, and is at most $1/4$, since $u(1-u)\le1/4$. Twice integrating gives $h(t)\le t^2/8$ for $t\ge0$. Independence now gives $\mathbb E e^{t(X_a-qm)}\le e^{mt^2/8}$. There are $2^n$ assignments, so \[ \mathbb E e^{t(\operatorname{OPT}-qm)} \le\sum_a\mathbb E e^{t(X_a-qm)}\le2^n e^{mt^2/8}. \] The first inequality holds because a maximum of nonnegative numbers is bounded by their sum. Jensen's inequality for the exponential gives $t(\mathbb E\operatorname{OPT}-qm)\le n\log2+mt^2/8$. Choosing $t=\sqrt{8n\log2/m}$ proves the claim for $m,n>0$; $m=0$ is immediate. Jensen here also follows from the supporting-line inequality $e^x\ge e^u(1+x-u)$ with $u=\mathbb EX$.
What this actually proves for an algorithm.
The deterministic algorithm returning the all-false assignment has expected score $qm$, though its score on an individual formula need not be at least $qm$. If $m=cn$, the preceding estimate yields \[ \frac{\mathbb EA(F)}{\mathbb E\operatorname{OPT}(F)} \ge\frac{q}{q+\sqrt{\log2/(2c)}} \ge1-\frac{1}{q}\sqrt{\frac{\log2}{2c}}. \] The last inequality is $1/(1+x)\ge1-x$ for $x\ge0$, obtained by multiplying by $1+x$. Thus a density $c\ge\log2/(2q^2\varepsilon^2)$ suffices for the requested expectation ratio in this specified model. This is an elementary high-density observation, not a scheme for every fixed density and arbitrarily small $\varepsilon$.
The failed extension to a fixed sparse density.
For fixed $c$, our error bound divided by $m$ is a positive constant, not a quantity tending to zero with $n$. The union over $2^n$ assignments contributes the $n\log2$ term that survives after optimization. A concentration statement for $\operatorname{OPT}/n$ around its unknown expectation cannot remove this term or compute that expectation: it locates the random value around an unspecified center. This is the exact point where combining a trivial assignment with concentration fails to prove a PTAS at every fixed density. No lower bound on all algorithms is inferred from this failure.
