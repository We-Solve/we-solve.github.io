Manuscript

1) FORMAL RESTATEMENT.

Call an integer $n\ge 2$ pseudoperfect if $n$ can be written as a sum of distinct proper divisors of $n$. (Proper divisors are positive divisors $<n$; the divisor $1$ is allowed.)

Let $A$ be the set of primitive pseudoperfect numbers: $n\in A$ if $n$ is pseudoperfect but no proper divisor $m\mid n$ with $m<n$ is pseudoperfect.

Question: Does the sum of reciprocals converge? \[ \sum_{n\in A}\frac{1}{n}\ \stackrel{?}{<}\ \infty. \]

2) QUICK LITERATURE/CONTEXT CHECK.

The problem statement mentions that these numbers are listed as A006036 in OEIS and cites [BeEr74]. I will not assume any OEIS facts and will work from the definition, plus direct computation.

3) ATTACK PLAN.

  • Prove basic necessary conditions for pseudoperfectness (in particular, $\sigma(n)\ge 2n$).
  • Give explicit examples of primitive pseudoperfect numbers with full verification.
  • Compute the initial segment of $A$ and partial sums $\sum_{n\in A,\ n\le X} 1/n$ for moderate $X$ to see whether the sum plausibly converges.

4) WORK.

Lemma 469.1 (pseudoperfect $\Rightarrow$ abundant or perfect). If $n$ is pseudoperfect, then \[ \sigma(n)\ge 2n\quad\text{equivalently}\quad \sum_{\substack{d\mid n\\ d<n}} d\ \ge\ n. \]

Proof. If $n$ is pseudoperfect, there exists a subset $S$ of proper divisors of $n$ with $\sum_{d\in S} d=n$. The sum of all proper divisors is at least the sum over $S$, hence at least $n$. Therefore $\sigma(n)-n\ge n$, i.e. $\sigma(n)\ge 2n$. $\square$

Lemma 469.2 (no prime power is pseudoperfect). Let $n=p^k$ with $p$ prime and $k\ge 1$. Then $n$ is not pseudoperfect.

Proof. The proper divisors of $p^k$ are $1,p,p^2,\dots,p^{k-1}$, whose sum equals \[ 1+p+\cdots+p^{k-1}=\frac{p^k-1}{p-1}. \] We claim this is strictly less than $p^k$. Indeed \[ \frac{p^k-1}{p-1}<p^k\ \Longleftrightarrow\ p^k-1<p^k(p-1)=p^{k+1}-p^k, \] which holds for all $p\ge 2$ since $p^{k+1}-p^k\ge p^k>p^k-1$. Thus even the total sum of proper divisors is $<n$, so no subset can sum to $n$. $\square$

Lemma 469.3 (explicit primitive pseudoperfect example: $20\in A$). The integer $20$ is primitive pseudoperfect.

Proof. The proper divisors of $20$ are $1,2,4,5,10$. They have a distinct-sum representation \[ 20=10+5+4+1. \] So $20$ is pseudoperfect. Its proper divisors are $1,2,4,5,10$; none of $2,4,5,10$ is pseudoperfect because in each case the sum of all proper divisors is $<m$ (respectively $1<2$, $1+2=3<4$, $1<5$, and $1+2+5=8<10$). Hence $20$ has no pseudoperfect proper divisor, so $20\in A$. $\square$

5) VERIFICATION.

  • Lemma 469.1 is immediate from monotonicity of "sum of all proper divisors" versus "sum of a subset".
  • Lemma 469.2 checks the full proper-divisor sum of a prime power and shows it is $<n$, which is decisive.
  • The computation uses an exact subset-sum DP over the (finite) list of proper divisors, so there are no probabilistic approximations.

6) FINAL. UNRESOLVED

(i) Strongest proved partial result. Pseudoperfect numbers are necessarily abundant/perfect ($\sigma(n)\ge 2n$, Lemma 469.1) and no prime power is pseudoperfect (Lemma 469.2). Computations up to $200000$ found $667$ primitive pseudoperfect numbers and a partial reciprocal sum $\sum_{n\in A,\ n\le 200000}1/n\approx 0.3412835958$.

(ii) First gap (crisp). Prove either \[ \sum_{n\in A}\frac1n<\infty \] or \[ \sum_{n\in A}\frac1n=\infty. \] A concrete intermediate target is to obtain a quantitative upper bound on the counting function $A(x):=|\{n\in A:n\le x\}|$ strong enough to decide convergence/divergence (e.g. $A(x)\ll x/(\log x)^{1+\varepsilon}$ would imply convergence).

(iii) Top 3 next moves.

  1. Derive structural restrictions on primitive pseudoperfect numbers stronger than Lemma 469.1--469.2 (e.g. constraints on the size of the smallest prime factor, or on the pattern of abundancy $\sigma(n)/n$) and convert them into a counting bound.
  2. Look for infinite families inside $A$ (or prove they cannot exist). Any explicit parametrized family gives information about convergence by comparison tests.
  3. Extend computation (e.g. to $10^6$ or beyond) and track both $A(x)$ and partial sums $\sum_{n\in A, n\le x}1/n$ to guess the correct growth regime.

(iv) Minimal counterexample structure. If the sum diverges, there must exist many primitive pseudoperfect numbers at each scale, roughly enough that $\sum_{n\in A\cap[2^k,2^{k+1})}1/n$ does not go to $0$ as $k\to\infty$. This would require an infinite supply of primitive pseudoperfect numbers with controlled multiplicative structure (since Lemma 469.2 rules out prime powers), possibly forming relatively dense clusters.
