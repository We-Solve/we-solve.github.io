Statement recorded in the supplied source.
A.7 Mitchell, Ian

The solution to the Hamilton-Jacobi(-Bellman)(-Isaacs) partial differential equation (HJ PDE) provides the optimal value function (eg cost to go) for bounded input optimal control or zero-sum differential game problems respectively. These equations arise in many disparate fields, and my personal interest lies in how they can be used to approximate the reachable sets of continuous dynamic systems in order to verify and/or synthesize provably safe control policies. For these purposes, it may be possible to relax the goal of a convergent approximation of the solution of the PDE. Instead, we seek guaranteed under or over approximations of the solutions; for example, an underapproximation of the solution might overapproximate the unsafe reachable set, which might thereby generate false negative verifications but no false positives. With or without such relaxations, is it possible to take advantage of structure in the system dynamics to reduce the typically exponential growth in computational cost with system dimension? For example, is it possible to take advantage of weak or linear coupling between certain components to reduce the degrees of freedom needed by an approximation, perhaps through partial parameterization? Or another approach: Can sampling methods be applied to produce quantifiable probabilistic verifications? In addition to these questions related to the application area of verification, I am inter-ested in the class of algorithms designed to approximate solutions of HJ PDEs - often broadly called "level set methods." This interest includes both the time-dependent and stationary (or static) versions of the equation, as well as issues related to the use of such numerical approximations in the control of robotic systems. Research attempt: Small coupling can accumulate over unstable horizons

Choose coupled and reference dynamics on the same state space with the same admissible control signals and initial state: $\dot x=f(x,u)+r(x,u)$ and $\dot y=f(y,u)$. Assume both solutions exist on the common time interval being compared. Assume $f$ is uniformly $L$-Lipschitz in state and $\|r\|\le\epsilon$. For the error norm $e$, its upper right derivative satisfies $D^+e\le Le+\epsilon$. Multiplying the scalar comparison equation by $e^{-Lt}$ and integrating gives \[ e(t)\le\epsilon(e^{Lt}-1)/L\quad(L>0),\qquad e(t)\le\epsilon t\quad(L=0). \] One can avoid differentiability of the norm at zero by replacing it with $\sqrt{\|x-y\|^2+\eta^2}$ and letting $\eta\downarrow0$ in the same integral inequality. Matching each admissible signal in both systems gives both directions of the corresponding reachable-set distance bound.

This tube is sharp even in dimension one. Take $f(x)=Lx$, $r=\epsilon$, and both initial states zero. Then $y=0$ and $x=\epsilon(e^{Lt}-1)/L$ exactly. At $T_\epsilon=L^{-1}\log(1/\epsilon)$ for $0<\epsilon<1$, the error tends to $1/L$ as $\epsilon\to0$. Thus "weak coupling" alone does not justify a small-error reduction uniformly over growing horizons or unstable dynamics.

For safety, if a reference reachable set is farther than the displayed radius from the unsafe set, the coupled set is disjoint from it by the triangle inequality. Without that margin, containment in a small tube is inconclusive. Sampling gives a different guarantee: if independent tested inputs follow a fixed law and the unsafe-input probability is $p$, the probability of seeing no failure in $N$ tests is $(1-p)^N\le e^{-Np}$. This bounds a probability under that law, not adversarial safety. An unsafe input in a probability-zero set is never sampled, although it remains an admissible adversarial input. Neither the trajectory tube nor the sampling calculation addresses the cost of representing a high-dimensional set.
