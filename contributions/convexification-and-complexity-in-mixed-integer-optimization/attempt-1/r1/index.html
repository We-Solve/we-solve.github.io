Ordinary convex hull need not be a closed polyhedron

With the usual convention that a polyhedron is a finite intersection of closed affine half-spaces, take $P=[0,1]\subset\mathbb R$ and remove the polyhedron $Q=\{0\}$. Then $P\setminus Q=(0,1]$ is already convex and is not closed, so its convex hull is not a polyhedron. A finite intersection of closed sets is closed, proving the last implication. This is a counterexample to that literal component, not a resolution of all the other questions in the bundle. It also shows why replacing the convex hull by the closed convex hull would change the statement.
The closed-hull version, with proof.
For any polyhedron $P$ minus finitely many polyhedra $Q_1,\ldots,Q_r$, the closed convex hull is polyhedral (possibly empty). Write each $Q_i$ by finitely many weak linear inequalities. A point outside $Q_i$ violates at least one of them strictly. Distributing these finite choices expresses $P\setminus\bigcup_iQ_i$ as a finite union of cells, each given by weak linear inequalities for $P$ and selected strict linear inequalities. If one $Q_i$ is the whole space the difference is empty; discard empty cells. For a nonempty cell, its closure is obtained by changing its strict inequalities to weak ones. One inclusion follows by continuity. For the other, let $p$ be a fixed point satisfying all strict inequalities and let $x$ satisfy their weak versions together with the constraints of $P$. For $0<t\le1$, $(1-t)x+tp$ satisfies all required strict inequalities and approaches $x$. Thus the closure of each nonempty cell is a polyhedron $R_i$.

It remains to prove that the closed convex hull of a finite union of nonempty polyhedra is polyhedral. Write $R_i=\{x:A_ix\le b_i\}$ and introduce \[ \lambda_i\ge0,\quad \sum_i\lambda_i=1,\quad A_i v_i\le\lambda_i b_i,\quad x=\sum_i v_i. \] The projection $H$ is a polyhedron. Indeed, eliminating a variable from a finite weak linear system preserves that form: constraints with positive and negative coefficients yield finitely many comparisons of its lower and upper bounds; constraints with zero coefficient remain; if only one sign is present, a sufficiently large value in the appropriate direction meets those bounds. Repeated elimination proves the projection assertion and hence closedness. Every convex combination of points in the $R_i$ has this lift, so its closure is contained in $H$.

Conversely take a feasible lift and choose any $p_i\in R_i$. For $\eta>0$, replace \[ \lambda_i\quad\hbox{by}\quad \lambda_i^\eta=\frac{\lambda_i+\eta}{1+r'\eta},\qquad v_i\quad\hbox{by}\quad v_i^\eta=\frac{v_i+\eta p_i}{1+r'\eta}, \] where $r'$ is the number of remaining nonempty polyhedra. All new weights are positive and sum to one, and $A_i v_i^\eta\le\lambda_i^\eta b_i$. Thus $v_i^\eta/\lambda_i^\eta\in R_i$, and $x^\eta=\sum_i v_i^\eta$ is a convex combination approaching $x$. This proves $H$ is exactly the closed convex hull. Finally, replacing the finite cells by their closures does not change their closed convex hull: each closure is contained in any closed convex set containing the cells, and the reverse containment is immediate. The stated closed-hull result follows.

An exact oracle-model obstruction

Let the feasible integer set be $\{0,\ldots,N-1\}$, encoded by binary bounds, and impose no structure on an evaluation-oracle objective $f$. A deterministic algorithm that computes the exact minimum value must query all $N$ points in the worst case. Run it against the all-zero function. If it stops without querying $a$, change only $f(a)$ to $-1$. The query transcript and output are identical, but the minimum value changes from zero to $-1$. Hence one of the two outputs must be wrong. This also shows exponential dependence on the bit length $\log N$ can be unavoidable for this unrestricted model.

For finding a minimizer with randomization, consider the family $f_a$ with unique minimum $-1$ at $a$ and zero elsewhere, with $a$ uniform. For a deterministic procedure making at most $q$ queries, follow its all-zero transcript. It discovers the marked point only for the at most $q$ indices queried on that transcript; if discovery never occurs, its final output can be the right answer for at most one further index. Its success averaged over $a$ is at most $(q+1)/N$. Averaging this inequality over random seeds proves the same bound for every randomized procedure, and therefore some input has success no greater than this average. A uniform success guarantee of $2/3$ requires $q\ge2N/3-1$. This argument assumes neither convexity nor Lipschitz control; it is not a lower bound for promised convex oracle problems. Such promises are precisely what the broad practical question would need to add.

Adversarial audit and gaps
