Gauge Fixing for Quantum Vertex Algebra Deformations

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
$\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$; the standard number fields have their usual meanings. All additional conventions are stated locally.
Definitions and conventions.
A vertex algebra packages fields and their operator products; a quantum vertex algebra has a deformed version of these algebraic structures. Its deformation complex controls infinitesimal changes and their compatibility equations. In ordinary Calabi--Yau Kodaira--Spencer theory, a $(0,1)$-form $A$ valued in the holomorphic tangent bundle satisfies $\bar\partial A+\tfrac12[A,A]=0$. Tian gauge is the gauge choice enabling the corresponding perturbative action treatment. The report seeks an analogue for its coupled quantum-vertex-algebra deformation equations.
Source scope and qualifications.
The required gauge and physical interpretation are two separate targets. The report's proposed fivebrane interpretation is suggestive context, not an established identification or a definition that may be assumed.

A finite-dimensional gauge slice.
Let $V^0\xrightarrow{d_0}V^1\xrightarrow{d_1}V^2$ be a finite-dimensional Hilbert complex, so $d_1d_0=0$. Infinitesimal deformations obey $d_1a=0$, with gauge changes $a\mapsto a+d_0u$. Orthogonal projection onto $\operatorname{im}d_0$ decomposes $a=d_0u+h$, where $d_0^*h=0$. Since $d_1d_0=0$, also $d_1h=0$. Thus every infinitesimal gauge class has a unique representative in \[ \mathcal H^1=\ker d_1\cap\ker d_0^*. \] Uniqueness follows because an element simultaneously in $\operatorname{im}d_0$ and its orthogonal complement is zero.
Second-order obstruction survives gauge fixing.
Suppose an antisymmetric graded bracket defines a Maurer--Cartan equation $da+\tfrac12[a,a]=0$. For $a(t)=th+t^2b+O(t^3)$ with $dh=0$, the next equation is $db=-\tfrac12[h,h]$. Therefore the degree-two cohomology class of $[h,h]$ must vanish. This requirement is not removed by imposing a linear slice. For an explicit failure take $V^1=\mathbb C h$, $V^2=\mathbb C z$, zero differential and $[h,h]=z$, with all other brackets zero. The graded Jacobi identity holds because $z$ is central, but no nonzero first-order direction extends to second order.
