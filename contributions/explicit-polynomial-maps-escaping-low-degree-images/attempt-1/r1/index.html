Elusive-map attempts: affine escape, a quadratic cover, and ineffective elimination

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

The image of a map $f$ is $f(\mathbb F^n)$. The requested noncontainment says that some point in this image is absent from the comparison map's image. $n^{\omega(1)}$ denotes growth faster than every fixed power of $n$.

Polynomial time means a number of elementary steps bounded by a polynomial in the encoded input length. A decision problem is in $\mathrm{NP}$ if yes-instances have polynomial-length certificates verifiable in polynomial time; NP-hardness is under polynomial-time reductions.

Statement recorded in the supplied source.

First, for functions $s=s(n)$, $m=m(n)$, and $r=r(n)$ satisfying $n^{\omega(1)}\leq s<m=n^r$, with $r$ relatively small (for example, $r=\log\log n$), construct an explicit polynomial map $f:\mathbb{F}^n\to\mathbb{F}^m$ of degree at most $2^n$ whose image is not contained in the image of any polynomial map $\Gamma:\mathbb{F}^s\to\mathbb{F}^m$ of degree at most $r$. Verbatim supplied catalogue question

Let $\mathbb{F}$ be a field. First, for functions $s=s(n)$, $m=m(n)$, and $r=r(n)$ satisfying $n^{\omega(1)}\leq s<m=n^r$, with $r$ relatively small (for example, $r=\log\log n$), construct an explicit polynomial map $f:\mathbb{F}^n\to\mathbb{F}^m$ of degree at most $2^n$ whose image is not contained in the image of any polynomial map $\Gamma:\mathbb{F}^s\to\mathbb{F}^m$ of degree at most $r$. Second, for integers $s<m=n^{r+1}$ and $2\leq r\leq O(1)$, given a map $\Gamma:\mathbb{F}^s\to\mathbb{F}^m$ of degree at most $2r-1$, design a polynomial-time algorithm that outputs an explicit map $f:\mathbb{F}^n\to\mathbb{F}^m$ of degree $\operatorname{poly}(n)$ whose image is not contained in the image of $\Gamma$.

Attempt and results

The source has two distinct tasks: one map must escape every competitor in the first, whereas the second permits dependence on the supplied competitor. We test an explicit moment-curve candidate for the first and an elimination route for the second. The field and output representation matter throughout.
Affine competitors can be escaped explicitly.
Over an infinite field, $f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=(x_1,x_1^2,\ldots,x_1^m)$ has image in no proper affine subspace of $\mathbb F^m$. Otherwise there would be a nonzero affine functional $a_0+\sum_{j=1}^m a_j y_j$ vanishing on that image. The resulting polynomial $a_0+\sum_j a_j t^j$ vanishes at every field element; a nonzero degree-at-most-$m$ polynomial has at most $m$ roots, by repeated division at distinct roots, so all coefficients must vanish, a contradiction. The same proof works over a finite field with more than $m$ elements. Hence this map escapes every affine image of dimension $s<m$. Its degree is $m$; whether it meets a particular degree budget must be checked separately rather than assumed from this restricted statement.
The same candidate is covered by a quadratic map.
Let $h=\lceil\sqrt m\rceil$, $l=\lfloor m/h\rfloor$. Use variables $u_0,\ldots,u_{h-1},v_0,\ldots,v_l$, a total of $s_0=h+l+1$. For $1\le j\le m$, write $j=hq+r$ with $0\le r<h$ and define $\Gamma_j(u,v)=u_rv_q$. These are degree-two polynomials. Setting $u_r=t^r$ and $v_q=t^{hq}$ gives $\Gamma_j=t^j$ for every coordinate, including the cases $r=0$ or $q=0$, where the assigned variable value is one. Thus the moment curve is contained in the image of this quadratic map. Whenever $s_0\le s<m$, unused domain variables extend it to the allowed competitor dimension. In particular large allowed $s$, such as $m-1$ once $m$ is sufficiently large, defeat the candidate despite its full affine span. This is a counterexample to extending the affine-span argument to higher degree; it is not a counterexample to the existence of other elusive maps.
Nonconstructive elimination supplies a point, at a potentially huge cost.
Let $\Gamma:\mathbb F^s\to\mathbb F^m$, $s<m$, have coordinate degree at most $D$, where we choose the degree upper bound $D\ge1$ (also for constant maps). Substitution $Q\mapsto Q\circ\Gamma$ is a linear map from target polynomials of total degree at most $e$ to source polynomials of total degree at most $De$. Their dimensions are $\binom{m+e}{m}$ and $\binom{s+De}{s}$, respectively, by counting monomials. For sufficiently large $e$ the first is greater: as polynomials in $e$, their leading terms are $e^m/m!$ and $D^s e^s/s!$, and $m>s$. Thus a nonzero polynomial $Q$ of degree at most $e$ satisfies $Q\circ\Gamma=0$ identically. A nullspace calculation on the expanded substitution matrix finds such $Q$, with no promise that its size is polynomial in the original input length. Over an infinite field, choose a set $T$ of $e+1$ distinct field elements. A nonzero polynomial of degree at most $e$ in each variable cannot vanish on all of $T^m$. This follows by induction on the number of variables: view it as a polynomial in the last variable; vanishing on all $e+1$ choices forces each coefficient polynomial to vanish on the remaining grid, and induction then forces every coefficient to be zero. Enumerating that grid therefore finds $a$ with $Q(a)\ne0$. The constant map $f\equiv a$ escapes the supplied $\Gamma$ and has degree zero. The expansion and grid may both be exponential or worse in the relevant parameters; this existence argument does not produce the requested polynomial-time algorithm. It also depends on $\Gamma$, so it cannot answer the first universal escape task by exchanging the quantifiers. Over a small finite field, a nonzero formal $Q$ might vanish everywhere, as $z^{|\mathbb F|}-z$ does. Cardinality still ensures some point is outside $\Gamma(\mathbb F^s)$, but it does not make this formal-polynomial test find one.
