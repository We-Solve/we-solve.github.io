4) WORK Lemma 1 (Three explicit forbidden identities). For every integer $a\ge 1$ the following hold: \[\frac1{2a}=\frac1{3a}+\frac1{6a},\qquad \frac1{3a}=\frac1{4a}+\frac1{12a},\qquad \frac1{4a}=\frac1{6a}+\frac1{12a}.\]

Proof. These are direct common-denominator calculations: \[\frac1{3a}+\frac1{6a}=\frac{2+1}{6a}=\frac1{2a},\quad \frac1{4a}+\frac1{12a}=\frac{3+1}{12a}=\frac1{3a},\quad \frac1{6a}+\frac1{12a}=\frac{2+1}{12a}=\frac1{4a}.\square\]

Lemma 2 (Forced omissions inside $S_a$). Fix $N$ and let $A\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}$ contain no solution to $(\dagger)$. Let $a$ be such that $12a\le N$, so $S_a=\{2a,3a,4a,6a,12a\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}$. Then $|A\cap S_a|\le 3$ (equivalently, $A$ omits at least two elements of $S_a$).

If instead $6a\le N<12a$ so $S_a\cap[1,N]=\{2a,3a,4a,6a\}$, then $|A\cap S_a|\le 3$ (i.e. $A$ omits at least one element of this 4-set).

Proof. First case ($12a\le N$): by Lemma 1, each of the triples \[\{2a,3a,6a\},\ \{3a,4a,12a\},\ \{4a,6a,12a\}\] supports a forbidden equality of the form $1/x=1/y+1/z$ with distinct elements. Thus $A$ cannot contain any of these triples. We check that every 4-subset of $S_a$ contains at least one of the listed triples: removing $12a$ leaves $\{2a,3a,4a,6a\}\supset\{2a,3a,6a\}$; removing $6a$ leaves $\{2a,3a,4a,12a\}\supset\{3a,4a,12a\}$; removing $4a$ leaves $\{2a,3a,6a,12a\}\supset\{2a,3a,6a\}$; removing $3a$ leaves $\{2a,4a,6a,12a\}\supset\{4a,6a,12a\}$; removing $2a$ leaves $\{3a,4a,6a,12a\}\supset\{3a,4a,12a\}$. Hence $A$ cannot contain 4 elements of $S_a$, so $|A\cap S_a|\le 3$.

Second case ($6a\le N<12a$): the available triple $\{2a,3a,6a\}$ is still contained in $S_a\cap[1,N]$ and again gives a forbidden equality by Lemma 1. Therefore $A$ cannot contain all of $\{2a,3a,6a\}$, so among the four elements $\{2a,3a,4a,6a\}$ it must omit at least one. $\square$

Lemma 3 (Disjointness of the family $S_a$ for special $a$). Let $\mathcal{A}$ be the set of integers of the form $a=8^b9^cd$ with $b,c\ge 0$ and $(d,6)=1$. For $a\in\mathcal{A}$ define \[S_a=\{2a,3a,4a,6a,12a\}.\] Then the sets $\{S_a:a\in\mathcal{A}\}$ are pairwise disjoint.

Proof. Write any integer $n\ge 1$ uniquely as $n=2^{u}3^{v}d$ with $u,v\ge 0$ and $(d,6)=1$. If $n\in S_a$ for some $a\in\mathcal{A}$, then $n=t a$ where $t\in\{2,3,4,6,12\}$ and $t=2^{\alpha}3^{\beta}$ with $(\alpha,\beta)\in\{(1,0),(0,1),(2,0),(1,1),(2,1)\}$. Since $a$ has $v_2(a)\equiv 0\pmod 3$ and $v_3(a)\equiv 0\pmod 2$, we have \[u\equiv \alpha\pmod 3,\qquad v\equiv \beta\pmod 2.\] The five pairs $(\alpha\bmod 3,\beta\bmod 2)$ listed above are all distinct, so $(u\bmod 3,v\bmod 2)$ determines $t$ uniquely. Then $a=n/t$ is uniquely determined. Therefore no integer $n$ can belong to two distinct $S_a$, proving disjointness. $\square$

Lemma 4 (Counting the special $a$: density $3/7$). Let $\mathcal{A}$ be as in Lemma 3. Then \[\#\{a\le X:\ a\in\mathcal{A}\}=\left(\frac{3}{7}+o(1)\right)X\qquad (X\to\infty).\]

Proof. An integer $a$ lies in $\mathcal{A}$ iff $v_2(a)\equiv 0\pmod 3$ and $v_3(a)\equiv 0\pmod 2$. For fixed $k,\ell\ge 0$, the set of $a$ with $v_2(a)=3k$ and $v_3(a)=2\ell$ has natural density \[\left(\frac{1}{2^{3k}}-\frac{1}{2^{3k+1}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{3^{2\ell}}-\frac{1}{3^{2\ell+1}}\right)=\frac{1}{2^{3k+1}}\cdot \frac{2}{3^{2\ell+1}}.\] Summing over $k,\ell$ gives total density \[\sum_{k\ge 0}\frac{1}{2^{3k+1}}\sum_{\ell\ge 0}\frac{2}{3^{2\ell+1}} =\left(\frac12\cdot\frac{1}{1-1/8}\right)\left(\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{1-1/9}\right) =\frac{4}{7}\cdot\frac{3}{4}=\frac{3}{7}.\] Standard truncation of the absolutely convergent series implies the counting function is $(3/7+o(1))X$. $\square$

Proposition 5 (Upper bound $f(N)\le (25/28+o(1))N$ via the disjoint configuration method). Let $A\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}$ contain no solution to $(\dagger)$. Then \[|A|\le \left(\frac{25}{28}+o(1)\right)N.\]

Proof. Let $\mathcal{A}$ be as above and consider all $a\in\mathcal{A}$ with $a\le N/6$. By Lemma 3 the corresponding sets $S_a\cap[1,N]$ are disjoint. By Lemma 2, for $a\le N/12$ we have $|A\cap S_a|\le 3$ while $|S_a|=5$; for $N/12<a\le N/6$ we have $|A\cap S_a|\le 3$ while $|S_a\cap[1,N]|=4$.
