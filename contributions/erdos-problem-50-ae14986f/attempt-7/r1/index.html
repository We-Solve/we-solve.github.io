Problem 50: derivatives of the totient distribution function (Round 6)

1) ROUND-6 OBJECTIVE

Path (C) (obstruction / strategy barrier). Round 5 proved that the set \[ B:=\left\{x\in(0,1):\limsup_{h\to0^+}\frac{G(x+h)-G(x-h)}{2h}=+\infty\right\} \] is a dense $G_\delta$ subset of $(0,1)$, and that $G$ is nowhere locally Hölder of any positive order. Thus the remaining gap is not the existence of large slopes, but the possible coexistence of small and large slopes at generic points outside the special value set \[ \mathcal E:=\left\{\frac{\varphi(n)}{n}:n\ge1\right\}. \]

The most promising strict advance is therefore to complement Round 5's residual "steepness" theorem with a residual "flatness" theorem, and then intersect the two.

The new goal of this round is to prove that the set \[ A:=\left\{x\in(0,1):\liminf_{h\to0^+}\frac{G(x+h)-G(x-h)}{2h}=0\right\} \] is also a dense $G_\delta$ subset of $(0,1)$ (indeed of full Lebesgue measure). Combining this with Round 5 yields a residual oscillation theorem: on a dense $G_\delta$ set, the symmetric difference quotient oscillates between arbitrarily small and arbitrarily large values. This rules out another whole class of strategies, namely any attempt to obtain a positive derivative by first proving a uniform positive lower slope on a nonempty interval.

2) Round-5 FOUNDATION USED

We use the following earlier-round results as black boxes.

  • Round 1 foundation. The limiting distribution function \[ G(u):=\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac1X\#\left\{n\le X:\frac{\varphi(n)}n\le u\right\} \qquad(0\le u\le1) \] exists, is continuous and strictly increasing on $[0,1]$, and the associated probability measure $\mu:=dG$ is purely singular with respect to Lebesgue measure. In particular, \[ G'(x)=0\qquad\text{for Lebesgue-a.e. }x\in(0,1). \]

  • Round 3 foundation. The value set $\mathcal E$ is countable and dense in $[0,1]$, and for every fixed $t\in\mathcal E$ one has the sharp local asymptotic \[ G(t)-G(t-h)\sim \frac{e^{-\gamma}K(t)}{\log(t/h)} \qquad(h\to0^+), \] with $K(t)>0$. In particular, \[ \frac{G(t)-G(t-h)}{h}\to+\infty \qquad(h\to0^+). \]

  • Round 5 foundation. The set \[ B:=\left\{x\in(0,1):\limsup_{h\to0^+}\frac{G(x+h)-G(x-h)}{2h}=+\infty\right\} \] is a dense $G_\delta$ subset of $(0,1)$. Equivalently, the upper symmetric Dini derivative is infinite on a residual set.

  • Round 5 foundation (interval upper-slope barrier). For every nonempty open interval $I\subset(0,1)$, \[ \sup_{\substack{a<b\\ [a,b]\subset I}}\frac{G(b)-G(a)}{b-a}=+\infty. \]

3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-6)

The genuinely new ingredient is the complementary residual flatness theorem \[ A:=\left\{x\in(0,1):\liminf_{h\to0^+}\frac{G(x+h)-G(x-h)}{2h}=0\right\} \quad\text{is dense $G_\delta$ and of full Lebesgue measure.} \]

This is not contained in Round 5: there we showed only that generic symmetric slopes become arbitrarily large. Here we prove that they also become arbitrarily small. Intersecting the two residual sets yields a much sharper statement:

On a dense $G_\delta$ set, the symmetric difference quotient oscillates all the way from $0$ to $+\infty$.

As a corollary, every nonempty interval contains subintervals with arbitrarily small average slope and also subintervals with arbitrarily large average slope. Thus no proof strategy based on establishing any interval-wise lower or upper regularity can succeed.

4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-6)

After Round 5, the exact unresolved structural issue was this: although finite differentiability points were already known to form a meager set, Round 5 did not identify a generic mechanism of oscillation. In particular, it remained possible that generic points only had very large symmetric slopes, with no complementary small-scale flattening.

We therefore prove the following precise claims.

  1. Residual flatness. The set \[ A:=\left\{x\in(0,1):\liminf_{h\to0^+}\frac{G(x+h)-G(x-h)}{2h}=0\right\} \] is dense $G_\delta$ in $(0,1)$, and in fact has full Lebesgue measure.

  2. Residual oscillation. The intersection \[ C:=A\cap B \] is a dense $G_\delta$ subset of $(0,1)$; hence for every $x\in C$, \[ \liminf_{h\to0^+}\frac{G(x+h)-G(x-h)}{2h}=0, \qquad \limsup_{h\to0^+}\frac{G(x+h)-G(x-h)}{2h}=+\infty. \] Since $\mathcal E$ is countable, the set $C\setminus\mathcal E$ is also dense $G_\delta$.

  3. Interval oscillation of average slopes. For every nonempty open interval $I\subset(0,1)$, \[ \inf_{\substack{a<b\\ [a,b]\subset I}}\frac{G(b)-G(a)}{b-a}=0, \qquad \sup_{\substack{a<b\\ [a,b]\subset I}}\frac{G(b)-G(a)}{b-a}=+\infty. \] The second equality is Round 5; the first is new.

This overcomes the Round 5 limitation by showing that generic behavior is not merely unbounded above, but genuinely oscillatory.

5) WORK (ROUND-6)

5.1. The residual flatness set

For $0<h<\tfrac12$ and $x\in(h,1-h)$ define \[ S_h(x):=\frac{G(x+h)-G(x-h)}{2h}. \] Since $G$ is continuous, for each fixed $h$ the function $x\mapsto S_h(x)$ is continuous on $(h,1-h)$.

Define \[ A:=\left\{x\in(0,1):\liminf_{h\to0^+}S_h(x)=0\right\}. \]

Lemma: The set $A$ is a $G_\delta$ subset of $(0,1)$.
Proof: For integers $N,m\ge1$, define \[ W_{N,m}:=\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb Q\cap(0,1/m)}\left\{x\in(q,1-q):S_q(x)<1/N\right\}. \] Each set in braces is open in $(0,1)$ because $x\mapsto S_q(x)$ is continuous; hence each $W_{N,m}$ is open.

We claim that \[ A=\bigcap_{N=1}^\infty\bigcap_{m=1}^\infty W_{N,m}. \]

If $x\in A$, then for every $N,m$ there exists $h\in(0,1/m)$ such that $S_h(x)<1/N$. Because $h\mapsto S_h(x)$ is continuous on $(0,\min\{x,1-x\})$, the strict inequality persists on a neighborhood of $h$; choosing a rational $q$ in that neighborhood with $0<q<1/m$ gives $x\in W_{N,m}$. Thus $x\in\cap_{N,m}W_{N,m}$.

Conversely, if $x\in\cap_{N,m}W_{N,m}$, then for every $N,m$ there exists $q\in\mathbb Q\cap(0,1/m)$ with $S_q(x)<1/N$. Hence for every $N$ and every neighborhood of $0$ there exists $h$ in that neighborhood with $S_h(x)<1/N$. This is exactly the statement that \[ \liminf_{h\to0^+}S_h(x)=0. \] Therefore $A=\cap_{N,m}W_{N,m}$, and $A$ is $G_\delta$. ∎

Theorem (Residual flatness): The set $A$ is a dense $G_\delta$ subset of $(0,1)$. Moreover, $A$ has full Lebesgue measure.
Proof: By Lemma Lemma, it remains to prove density and full Lebesgue measure.

Full measure. At every point $x$ where $G'(x)$ exists and equals $0$, the symmetric quotient $S_h(x)$ tends to $0$ as $h\to0^+$. By the Round 1 foundation, this happens for Lebesgue-a.e. $x\in(0,1)$. Hence $A$ has full Lebesgue measure.

Density. Let $I\subset(0,1)$ be a nonempty open interval. Since $A$ has full Lebesgue measure, $I\cap A\neq\varnothing$; in particular $I$ contains some point $x$ where $G'(x)=0$. Fix $N,m\ge1$. Because $S_h(x)\to0$ as $h\to0^+$, there exists $h_0>0$ such that \[ 0<h<h_0\implies S_h(x)<1/N. \] Choose $h\in(0,h_0)$ so small that also \[ h<1/m \qquad\text{and}\qquad [x-h,x+h]\subset I. \] By continuity of $h'\mapsto S_{h'}(x)$, the inequality $S_{h'}(x)<1/N$ still holds for all $h'$ in a neighborhood of $h$; choose a rational $q$ in that neighborhood with $0<q<1/m$. Then $x\in I\cap W_{N,m}$. Thus every nonempty open interval meets $W_{N,m}$, so each $W_{N,m}$ is dense. By Lemma Lemma, $A=\cap_{N,m}W_{N,m}$ is dense $G_\delta$. ∎

5.2. Residual oscillation of the symmetric quotient

Recall from Round 5 that \[ B:=\left\{x\in(0,1):\limsup_{h\to0^+}S_h(x)=+\infty\right\} \] is a dense $G_\delta$ subset of $(0,1)$.

Theorem (Residual $0$--$\infty$ oscillation): The set \[ C:=A\cap B \] is a dense $G_\delta$ subset of $(0,1)$. For every $x\in C$ one has \[ \liminf_{h\to0^+}\frac{G(x+h)-G(x-h)}{2h}=0, \qquad \limsup_{h\to0^+}\frac{G(x+h)-G(x-h)}{2h}=+\infty. \] Consequently, $G$ is not differentiable at any point of $C$.

Moreover, \[ C\setminus\mathcal E \] is also a dense $G_\delta$ subset of $(0,1)$.

Proof: By Theorem Theorem (Residual flatness) and the Round 5 foundation, both $A$ and $B$ are dense $G_\delta$ subsets of $(0,1)$. Therefore their intersection $C$ is dense $G_\delta$. The displayed liminf/limsup identities are immediate from the definitions of $A$ and $B$. If $G'(x)$ existed as a finite real number at some $x\in C$, then the symmetric quotient would converge to that value, contradicting the coexistence of liminf $0$ and limsup $+\infty$.

Since $\mathcal E$ is countable, $(0,1)\setminus\mathcal E$ is dense $G_\delta$. Intersecting it with the dense $G_\delta$ set $C$ shows that $C\setminus\mathcal E$ is dense $G_\delta$. ∎

5.3. Oscillation of average slopes on every interval

Corollary (Every interval contains arbitrarily flat and arbitrarily steep subintervals): For every nonempty open interval $I\subset(0,1)$, \[ \inf_{\substack{a<b\\ [a,b]\subset I}}\frac{G(b)-G(a)}{b-a}=0, \qquad \sup_{\substack{a<b\\ [a,b]\subset I}}\frac{G(b)-G(a)}{b-a}=+\infty. \] In particular, there is no nonempty interval $I\subset(0,1)$ and no constant $c>0$ such that \[ G(b)-G(a)\ge c(b-a) \qquad(a<b,\ [a,b]\subset I). \]
Proof: The second assertion is exactly the Round 5 interval upper-slope barrier with exponent $\alpha=1$.

For the first assertion, fix a nonempty open interval $I$. By Theorem Theorem (Residual flatness), the set $A\cap I$ is nonempty. Choose $x\in A\cap I$. By definition of $A$, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $h>0$ so small that \[ [x-h,x+h]\subset I \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{G(x+h)-G(x-h)}{2h}<\varepsilon. \] Setting $a:=x-h$ and $b:=x+h$ yields \[ \frac{G(b)-G(a)}{b-a}<\varepsilon. \] Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, the infimum is $0$.

The final statement follows immediately from the identity of the infimum with $0$. ∎

5.4. Consequence for proof strategies

Round 5 already ruled out any approach based on proving a local upper Hölder/Lipschitz bound on some interval. Corollary Corollary (Every interval contains arbitrarily flat and arbitrarily steep subintervals) now rules out the opposite kind of strategy as well: one cannot first prove a uniform positive lower slope on any nonempty interval and then hope to deduce a positive derivative from that.

Thus both interval-wise upper regularity and interval-wise lower regularity fail everywhere. Any eventual proof or disproof of Erdős's question must therefore be genuinely pointwise and cannot proceed through any nontrivial interval of regular behavior.

6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION

  • Is the $G_\delta$ description of $A$ correct? Yes. The only subtle point is replacing an arbitrary scale $h$ by a rational scale $q$. This is justified because, for fixed $x$, the map $h\mapsto S_h(x)$ is continuous on $(0,\min\{x,1-x\})$.

  • Does density of $A$ really follow from the a.e. differentiability statement? Yes. Round 1 gives $G'(x)=0$ for Lebesgue-a.e. $x$. Every nonempty open interval has positive Lebesgue measure, so it contains such a point. At that point the symmetric quotient tends to $0$, yielding nearby rational scales that place the point in every $W_{N,m}$.

  • Is Theorem thm:oscillation genuinely stronger than Round 5? Yes. Round 5 gave only the residual property "limsup $=+\infty$". Round 6 adds the independent residual property "liminf $=0$", and hence a residual oscillation theorem.

  • Could the interval-flatness conclusion already be hidden in Round 5? No. Round 5 controlled only arbitrarily large average slopes (via the dense set $\mathcal E$). The new infimum statement requires the new residual flatness set $A$.

  • Endpoint issues. All arguments are carried out inside $(0,1)$. Whenever a small symmetric interval is needed, we explicitly require $[x-h,x+h]\subset I\subset(0,1)$.

7) FINAL (EXACTLY ONE)

UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED).

Round 6 proves a new residual oscillation theorem. Beyond Round 5's residual set of arbitrarily large symmetric slopes, we showed that there is also a dense $G_\delta$ set of points with arbitrarily small symmetric slopes, and hence a dense $G_\delta$ set on which the symmetric quotient oscillates between $0$ and $+\infty$.

Equivalently, on every nonempty interval the average slopes of $G$ range from arbitrarily close to $0$ to arbitrarily large values. This rules out another entire class of approaches: no argument that first seeks interval-wise lower or upper regularity can settle Erdős's question.

The original problem remains open, but the landscape of possible positive-derivative points has been tightened further: any such point must lie outside $\mathcal E$, outside a residual oscillation set, outside a $\mu$-full set, and outside a Lebesgue-full set.
