From linear feasibility to globally optimal finite contracts

OpenAI gpt-6.1-sol, ultra reasoning

28 October 2026

Abstract

For explicit rational finite principal--agent instances with nonnegative payments, a zero-cost action, and principal-favorable tie breaking, we reduce rational linear feasibility, with recovery of a feasible point, to one globally optimal contract computation. The construction uses polynomially many arithmetic operations, polynomial-size rational encodings, full-support outcome distributions, and a reward on a single outcome. An expensive artificial incentive direction and an exposing reward close the distinction between implementing a specified action and selecting a globally optimal action. Together with the usual reduction in the opposite direction, this gives an equivalence of the existence of strongly polynomial algorithms. It does not establish that either algorithm exists.

Target, checked context, and statement

An instance has actions $a$, outcomes $j$, rational distributions $q_a$, costs $c_a\geq0$ with $\min_a c_a=0$, and rewards $r_j\geq0$. A contract $t\geq0$ induces a maximizer of $q_a\cdot t-c_a$; ties select an action maximizing $q_a\cdot(r-t)$. The computational output is an optimal rational contract and its selected action. Strong polynomiality means a number of arithmetic operations and comparisons polynomial in the numbers of actions and outcomes, independently of coefficient lengths, with polynomial-size intermediate rational encodings.

The checked survey [survey, Section 3.1] reduces optimal contracts to one minimum-payment linear program per action and asks whether the global problem admits a strongly polynomial algorithm. The pinned repository attempt [prior, specified-action embedding] encodes arbitrary linear feasibility into the inducibility of one distinguished action. It explicitly does not reduce feasibility to global optimization: with its zero rewards, a zero-payment contract is globally optimal whatever happens to that action. The present construction addresses precisely that selection gap.

Theorem: Given $A\in\mathbb Q^{r\times d}$, $b\in\mathbb Q^r$, $d\geq1$, one can construct an instance with $r+2d+2$ actions and $d+2$ outcomes such that any globally optimal contract decides the feasibility of \[ Ax\geq b,\qquad 0\leq x\leq\mathbf 1, \tag{1} \] and supplies a feasible point when one exists. The construction and decoding use polynomially many arithmetic operations in $r,d$, independently of numeric lengths, and all constructed encodings have polynomial length. Every action distribution has full support, and only one outcome has a nonzero reward.

We give the full construction, including the quantitative penalty and the global-selection argument. No promise of feasibility, nondegeneracy, or strict incentive compatibility is used.

A rational vertex gap and an exact artificial penalty

Let $D\geq1$ be the product of the positive denominators in the supplied rational entries of $A,b$. Set \[ H=\max\left(\{1,D\}\cup\{|DA_{ij}|\}_{i,j} \cup\{|Db_i|\}_i\right),\qquad k=d+2,\qquad \delta=(k!H^k)^{-1},\qquad \kappa=\delta/8,\qquad p=\frac1{2(d+1)}. \tag{2} \] Empty maxima simply contribute no entries. These are positive rational numbers, with $H$ an integer. Introduce $z,t_0$ and the polyhedron $P\subseteq\mathbb R^{d+2}$ consisting of \begin{align} A_i x+z&\geq b_i &&(i=1,\ldots,r),\nonumber\\ x_j+z&\geq0,\qquad -x_j+z\geq-1 &&(j=1,\ldots,d),\nonumber\\ z&\geq0,\qquad t_0\geq0,\qquad t_0+x_j\geq0 &&(j=1,\ldots,d). \tag{3} \end{align} Its objective will be \[ L(x,z,t_0)=t_0+p\sum_{j=1}^d x_j+\frac{z}{2\kappa}. \tag{4} \] The variable $t_0$ handles the limited-liability constraints on a payment representation; it must not be dropped when $x$ can have negative entries.
Lemma: The minimum of $L$ on $P$ is attained and its minimizing face is compact. If $1$ is feasible, every minimizer has $z=0$, and its $x$ solves $1$. If $1$ is infeasible, no point of $P$ has $z=0$.
Proof: Put $B=\max(0,b_1,\ldots,b_r)$. The point $(0,B,0)$ lies in $P$, so it is nonempty. For $(x,z,t_0)\in P$, define \[ t_j=t_0+x_j\quad(1\leq j\leq d),\qquad t_s=t_0+z/\kappa. \tag{5} \] Together with the coordinate $t_0$, these payments are nonnegative and \[ L=p\left(t_0+\sum_{j=1}^d t_j\right)+\tfrac12t_s. \tag{6} \] The transformation is invertible: $x_j=t_j-t_0$ and $z=\kappa(t_s-t_0)$. All coefficients in $6$ are strictly positive. Consequently every nonempty sublevel set of $L$ in $P$ is closed and bounded, hence compact. This proves attainment and compactness of the optimal face.

At any vertex of $P$, choose $k$ independent active rows. Multiplying each by $D$ gives a nonsingular integer square system whose coefficients and right-hand sides have absolute value at most $H$. Cramer's rule shows that a strictly positive $z$ at that vertex is at least $1/|\det M|$. The determinant expansion bounds $|\det M|\leq k!H^k$, so such a vertex has $z\geq\delta$. This argument does not assert a positive gap for every point of $P$.

The lower constraints in $3$ give $x_j\geq-z$. Thus every feasible point satisfies \[ L\geq \left(\frac1{2\kappa}-dp\right)z. \] Since $dp<1/2$ and $\delta\leq1$, every vertex with $z>0$ has \[ L\geq\left(\frac4\delta-dp\right)\delta =4-dp\delta>\frac72. \tag{7} \] If $1$ is feasible, any solution $x$ with $z=t_0=0$ belongs to $P$ and has $L=p\sum_jx_j\leq dp<1/2$. The compact optimal face is a polytope and is the convex hull of its vertices. Each of its vertices is also a vertex of $P$: any strict feasible convex decomposition must have both endpoints optimal, by linearity and optimality. Bound $7$ therefore makes $z=0$ at every optimal-face vertex, and hence at every minimizer. Finally, the constraints in $3$ at $z=0$ are exactly $1$, together with the additional limited-liability constraints on $t_0$. This proves both alternatives. ∎

Encoding the penalty as a distinguished action

List the first three types of inequalities in $3$, including $z\geq0$, as \[ a_\rho\cdot x+z\geq\beta_\rho \quad(\rho=1,\ldots,h),\qquad h=r+2d+1. \tag{8} \] For $z\geq0$ use $a_\rho=0,\beta_\rho=0$. There are $d+2$ outcomes labeled $1,\ldots,d,0,s$. Define the distinguished distribution and the row directions by \[ q_*=(\underbrace{p,\ldots,p}_{d+1\text{ coordinates}},1/2), \qquad v_\rho=\left(a_{\rho1},\ldots,a_{\rho d}, -\sum_{j=1}^d a_{\rho j}-\kappa,\kappa\right). \tag{9} \] Each $v_\rho$ has coordinate sum zero. Put \[ M_\rho=\max_\ell |v_{\rho\ell}|,\qquad \lambda_\rho=\frac{p}{2(1+M_\rho)},\qquad q_\rho=q_*-\lambda_\rho v_\rho. \tag{10} \] Every coordinate of $q_\rho$ is greater than $p/2=1/[4(d+1)]$: its deviation from $q_*$ has magnitude less than $p/2$, and the smallest coordinate of $q_*$ is $p$. Its coordinates sum to one. In particular all actions have full support with this inverse-polynomial lower bound. Define \[ C=\max\left(0,\max_\rho\lambda_\rho\beta_\rho\right), \qquad c_*=C,\qquad c_\rho=C-\lambda_\rho\beta_\rho. \tag{11} \] These costs are nonnegative and one is zero. Indeed, if $C=0$, the distinguished action is zero-cost; otherwise a maximizing competitor is.

For nonnegative payments, the incentive inequality of $*$ against $\rho$ is \[ (q_*-q_\rho)\cdot t\geq c_*-c_\rho \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a_\rho\cdot x+z\geq\beta_\rho, \tag{12} \] where $x,z$ are recovered from $5$. The payments $t_0,t_j\geq0$ give exactly $t_0\geq0,t_0+x_j\geq0$; the row $z\geq0$ also makes $t_s\geq0$ automatic. Thus $5$ is a bijection between $P$ and nonnegative contracts weakly implementing $*$. Its expected payment is $q_*\cdot t=L$. Let its attained minimum be $C_*$.

Forcing global selection

The artificial coordinate does more than supply slack: it exposes the target distribution, since \[ q_{*s}-q_{\rho s}=\lambda_\rho\kappa>0. \] Let \[ \eta=\min_\rho\lambda_\rho\kappa>0,\qquad U=\frac{B}{2\kappa},\qquad R=\frac{U+1}{\eta}, \qquad r_s=R,\quad r_\ell=0\ (\ell\ne s). \tag{13} \] The point $(0,B,0)$ shows $C_*\leq U$. Every competitor, under every nonnegative contract, gives principal payoff at most its gross reward \[ q_\rho\cdot(r-t)\leq q_{\rho s}R \leq(1/2-\eta)R. \tag{14} \] A minimum-payment contract for $*$ gives principal payoff \[ \tfrac12R-C_*\geq\tfrac12R-U =(1/2-\eta)R+1. \tag{15} \] Since $*$ is an agent best response there, $14$--$15$ imply that it is the unique principal-favored action among agent best responses. They also imply that any globally optimal contract selects $*$. Such a contract must have expected payment exactly $C_*$, since otherwise a minimum-payment contract for $*$ improves the principal's payoff.

By Lemma Lemma, the returned contract has $z=\kappa(t_s-t_0)=0$ if and only if $1$ is feasible. In that case $x_j=t_j-t_0$ is an exact feasible point. This argument applies to every optimal output, not merely a specially chosen optimal vertex.

Arithmetic bounds and general linear programming

There are $h+1=r+2d+2$ actions. Forming $D,H$, factorials, powers, row sums, maxima, and $9$--$13$ uses polynomially many arithmetic operations in $r,d$. Denominators are available in the supplied binary rational representation; clearing them by their product is explicit and uses one factor per entry. No precision-dependent search or repeated halving is used. The logarithm of $D$ is at most the sum of input denominator lengths. Therefore $\log H$, $\log(1/\delta)$, and all subsequent rational encoding lengths are polynomial in the input length. Products of polynomially many such denominators and the displayed bounded-depth formulas preserve this bound. This proves Theorem Theorem.

For completeness, bounded feasibility suffices for general rational linear feasibility with witness. Write a system, after splitting free variables if needed, as $Ax\geq b,x\geq0$. Clear denominators and let $H_0\geq1$ bound all integer coefficients and right-hand sides. This polyhedron is pointed, so a nonempty one has a vertex. A vertex is determined by $d$ independent active rows, including coordinate nonnegativity rows if needed. Cramer's rule gives \[ 0\leq x_j\leq M:=d!H_0^d. \] Thus adding $x_j\leq M$ preserves feasibility, and substituting $x=My$ reduces it to $1$. The same polynomial arithmetic and encoding bounds apply. Zero-dimensional systems are decided by their constant inequalities.

Corollary: The following algorithm-existence statements are equivalent, in the stated explicit rational model: a strongly polynomial algorithm for globally optimal finite contracts, and a strongly polynomial algorithm for general rational linear programming.
Proof: An algorithm for linear programming solves the minimum-payment program for each action and compares the finitely many principal payoffs. This is the standard direction in [survey, Proposition 3.1], and uses polynomially many calls on polynomial-size arrays.

Conversely, Theorem Theorem and the preceding bounded reduction give linear feasibility with an exact witness. This suffices for optimization without objective-value binary search. For \[ \min\{c^Tx:Ax\geq b,x\geq0\}, \] first test primal feasibility. If infeasible, report that. Otherwise test dual feasibility $y\geq0,A^Ty\leq c$. If infeasible, the feasible primal is unbounded below by linear-programming duality. If both are feasible, solve the joint linear feasibility system consisting of those primal and dual constraints and $c^Tx=b^Ty$. Strong duality ensures that it is feasible; any witness gives optimal primal and dual solutions by weak duality. Equality is represented by its two inequalities. There are at most three feasibility calls, with polynomially many variables and constraints. All transformations obey the arithmetic and encoding bounds. ∎

Consequences and limitations

The result is a complexity equivalence, not an algorithm for either open problem and not a lower bound ruling one out. Its importance for the stated contract target is that the probability-difference structure alone does not make the global strongly polynomial question easier than general rational linear programming under these reductions. It closes the precise specified-action/global-selection gap identified in the checked attempt.

The gadget uses coefficients with potentially large numerical magnitudes and small positive differences between action probabilities, although with polynomial binary length. Individual probabilities obey the lower bound proved after $10$. It does not establish hardness or equivalence for additional regularity, a fixed probability lower bound independent of the number of outcomes, bounded rewards, monotone contracts, strict implementation, adversarial ties, or approximate contract algorithms. Exact equality tests and exact optimal output are essential to the decoding. No global literature-priority certification is claimed: the checked survey and pinned attempts do not contain this reduction, but that is not an exhaustive claim about all literature.
