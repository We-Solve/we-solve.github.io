Definitions and conventions.
The standard circuit model uses finitely many qubits, preparation in a known input state, a universal set of local unitary gates, and final measurements. A uniform polynomial-size circuit family represents efficient quantum computation. Physical feasible realization requires scalable devices that preserve the necessary quantum coherence, control, and measurement accuracy under a specified noise model; it is stronger than a formal circuit construction.

The uncorrected circuit error budget.
Let $\mathcal U_j$ be ideal channels and $\mathcal E_j$ their implementations, with diamond norm $\|\mathcal E_j-\mathcal U_j\|_\diamond\leq\epsilon_j$. The diamond norm is the supremum trace-norm amplification after adjoining an arbitrary finite ancilla; channels have norm one. Inserting and subtracting one ideal gate at a time gives \[ \|\mathcal E_L\cdots\mathcal E_1-\mathcal U_L\cdots\mathcal U_1\|_\diamond \leq\sum_{j=1}^L\epsilon_j. \] Thus a direct implementation with no correction needs a per-gate bound of order $1/L$ to obtain a fixed worst-case error from this estimate. It is a sufficient error budget, not a necessary lower bound for every circuit.
Why a noise model is substantive.
In the elementary three-bit repetition code with majority decoding, independent bit flips of probability $p$ give logical failure $3p^2(1-p)+p^3=3p^2-2p^3$. If instead all three bits flip together with probability $p$, each physical marginal still has error $p$, but the logical failure is $p$. Hence the same marginal error rate does not certify even this simple redundancy scheme. The example is classical and is not a proof of quantum fault tolerance.
