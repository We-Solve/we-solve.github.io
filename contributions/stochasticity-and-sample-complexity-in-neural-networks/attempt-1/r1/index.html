Verbatim supplied catalogue question

How does stochasticity affect neural-network performance, and how much data is needed to achieve small estimation error with high probability?

The source model rather than an unspecified network

The primary report, printed pages3055--3057, gives the linear stochastic recurrent model $dY_t=(-Y_t+WY_t+u(x_t))dt+\Sigma dB_t$ and a final hyperplane classifier. It already describes agnostic PAC results and heuristic noise-induced robustness, while proposing nonlinear extensions. We analyze this explicit linear model, then distinguish repeated internal-noise simulation from new training data. The calculations are known elementary mechanisms, not a new bound for the entire unspecified architecture class.

Distribution of the linear recurrent state

Fix $T>0$, deterministic matrices $W,\Sigma$, deterministic integrable input forcing $b_t=u(x_t)$, and $Y_0=0$. Put $M=W-I$. The unique solution is \[ Y_T=\int_0^T e^{M(T-s)}b_s\,ds +\int_0^T e^{M(T-s)}\Sigma\,dB_s. \] Multiplying by the deterministic integrating factor $e^{-Mt}$ and integrating verifies the SDE; the difference of any two solutions with the same Brownian path solves the zero-initial linear ODE and is zero. The stochastic integral has mean zero and covariance \[ C_T=\int_0^T e^{M(T-s)}\Sigma\Sigma^T e^{M^T(T-s)}\,ds. \] To see the distribution directly, approximate the deterministic integrand by step matrices. Its integral is then a sum of independent centered Gaussian increments, with covariance the corresponding matrix Riemann sum. The sums converge in mean square by the defining isometry of the Brownian stochastic integral; their characteristic functions converge to that of a centered Gaussian with the displayed covariance. The covariance is finite on a finite interval for constant matrices. Thus $Y_T$ is Gaussian with deterministic mean $m_T=\int_0^T e^{M(T-s)}b_sds$ and covariance $C_T$.

For a fixed classifier score $v^TY_T+a$, let $\mu=v^Tm_T+a$ and $\sigma^2=v^TC_Tv$. If $\sigma>0$, its probability of a positive output is \[ p(\mu)=\Phi(\mu/\sigma),\qquad |p(\mu)-p(\nu)|\le\frac{|\mu-\nu|}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}, \] by integrating the standard Gaussian density, which is at most $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$. Thus injected noise smooths the prediction probability as a function of the mean score. If the desired label is positive and $\mu>0$, however, its error probability is $\Phi(-\mu/\sigma)$, increasing with $\sigma$ because its derivative is $\varphi(\mu/\sigma)\mu/\sigma^2>0$. Smoother prediction probabilities do not imply more accurate predictions on a fixed positive-margin example. If $\sigma=0$, the score is deterministic and this smoothing bound does not apply.

For example, in one dimension with $M=-\beta$, $\beta>0$, $\Sigma=\tau$ and constant forcing $b$, direct integration gives \[ m_T=b(1-e^{-\beta T})/\beta,\qquad C_T=\tau^2(1-e^{-2\beta T})/(2\beta). \] These formulas make both the saturation of noise variance and the accuracy/sensitivity tradeoff explicit. They do not prove that gradient descent in nonlinear recurrent networks becomes stable under noise.

New examples and repeated network noise are different

Let $Z_1,\ldots,Z_N$ be independent training or validation examples from a fixed distribution. For each example run a fixed randomized predictor $K$ times using mutually independent internal seeds $\omega_{ik}$, also independent of the examples. For its bounded loss $L(Z,\omega)\in[0,1]$ define \[ r(Z)=\mathbb E_\omega L(Z,\omega),\quad \widehat R=\frac1{NK}\sum_{i,k}L(Z_i,\omega_{ik}),\quad R=\mathbb E r(Z). \] Then the exact variance decomposition is \[ \operatorname{Var}(\widehat R) =\frac{\operatorname{Var}(r(Z))}{N} +\frac{\mathbb E\operatorname{Var}_\omega(L(Z,\omega)\mid Z)}{NK}. \] Conditional on all examples, the losses are independent across seeds and examples, giving the second term as the expectation of the conditional variance. The conditional mean is $N^{-1}\sum_i r(Z_i)$, whose variance is the first term. Expanding $\widehat R-R=(\widehat R-\mathbb E[\widehat R\mid Z_1,\ldots,Z_N]) +(\mathbb E[\widehat R\mid Z_1,\ldots,Z_N]-R)$ and observing that the cross term has expectation zero proves the variance identity without an independence shortcut. Markov's inequality on the squared error therefore gives \[ \Pr\{|\widehat R-R|\ge\eta\} \le\frac{\operatorname{Var}(r(Z))/N+ \mathbb E\operatorname{Var}_\omega(L\mid Z)/(NK)}{\eta^2}. \] Increasing $K$ only reduces internal simulation variance; the between-example term remains. If $L$ is deterministic given $Z$ and varies across examples, taking arbitrarily many noise replications does not change the estimator at all.

A finite-candidate high-probability bound

For $H$ predictors fixed independently of a fresh sample, average the $K$ bounded losses for each example first. For each predictor these $N$ example-level averages are independent and lie in $[0,1]$, with expectation its population risk. For any such bounded variable $V$, the second derivative of $\log\mathbb E e^{tV}$ is its variance under a tilted law, which is at most $1/4$ because $\operatorname{Var}V\le\mathbb E(V-1/2)^2\le1/4$. Integrating twice gives $\mathbb E e^{t(V-\mathbb EV)}\le e^{t^2/8}$. Markov's inequality with $t=4\eta$ gives each mean's upper tail at most $e^{-2N\eta^2}$, and $-t$ gives the lower tail. Union over $H$ candidates yields \[ \Pr\{\max_{j\le H}|\widehat R_j-R_j|>\eta\} \le2H e^{-2N\eta^2}. \] Thus $N\ge\log(2H/\delta)/(2\eta^2)$ suffices, and selecting the smallest empirical risk gives true risk at most the best candidate risk plus $2\eta$, by applying the bounds to the selected and best predictors. This is a finite-candidate validation guarantee. It does not bound a continuum of tuned recurrent matrices or allow candidates chosen adaptively from the same validation observations without additional analysis.

Audit and the failed universal inference

The audit checked deterministic versus stochastic inputs, possible covariance degeneracy, the direction of the Gaussian noise derivative, and the shared example within repeated noise runs. The attempted inference that more internal stochasticity universally improves both robustness and accuracy is false even in the scalar model; the inference that $NK$ repeated losses are independent population examples is also false. No general nonlinear recurrent-network training or sample-complexity theorem is obtained. No numerical simulation or theorem prover was used, and the source's existing learning guarantees remain attributed context rather than a claimed new result.
