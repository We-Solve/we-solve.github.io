10 Lectures and 42 Open Problems — Hardness at the Cheeger square-root gap

Definitions and mathematical statement

Let $G=(V,E)$ be a finite undirected graph without isolated vertices. Write $d(v)$ for its degree, $\operatorname{vol}(S)=\sum_{v\in S}d(v)$, and $\delta(S)$ for the edges with one endpoint in $S$ and one outside. Its conductance, or normalized Cheeger constant, is \[ h_G=\min_{0<\operatorname{vol}(S)\le\operatorname{vol}(V)/2} \frac{|\delta(S)|}{\operatorname{vol}(S)}. \] The minimization ranges over all eligible vertex sets, without a prescribed cardinality or a requirement that the two sides be equal.

Does there exist an absolute constant $c>0$ for which the following promise problem is NP-hard? The input is $G$ and a positive rational $\phi$, promised to satisfy one of \[ h_G\le\phi, \qquad h_G\ge c\sqrt\phi; \] the task is to decide which alternative holds. Restrict to $0<\phi<\min\{c^2,c^{-2},1\}$ so the alternatives are separated and the second threshold is below one. The parameter $\phi$ is part of the input, not a universal fixed accuracy.

NP-hardness here means a polynomial-time reduction from an NP-complete decision problem whose outputs satisfy the promise. The question seeks unconditional hardness at the square-root gap; hardness conditional on another unproved conjecture is not that conclusion. The normalization by volume is essential, since replacing it by $|S|$ on irregular graphs defines a different expansion parameter. Disconnected graphs have $h_G=0$ and cause no ambiguity.

Short English statement

Is it NP-hard to distinguish very small graph conductance from conductance at least a constant times its square root?

Research attempt

The target is an unconditional NP-hardness reduction for the variable-$\phi$ conductance promise. This attempt tests whether a family witnessing a sharp square-root relation between conductance and a spectral quantity can itself establish hardness. Cycles give an exact countercheck: they exhibit that relation, but their conductance and the promise decision are explicitly easy. A second calculation identifies a small-$\phi$ regime where the promise reduces to connectivity.

All graph calculations here concern finite simple undirected graphs with at least one edge and no isolated vertices, so the conductance minimization is nonempty. The complete original definition is retained; no cardinality-balanced cut is substituted for volume normalization.

Proposition: For the cycle $C_n$, $n\ge3$, \[ h_{C_n}=\frac1{\lfloor n/2\rfloor},\qquad \lambda_2(I-A/2)=1-\cos(2\pi/n). \] Consequently $h_{C_n}/\sqrt{\lambda_2}\to\sqrt2/\pi$ as $n\to\infty$, although the exact conductance is computable from $n$ alone.
Proof: Each vertex has degree two. An eligible set has $1\le|S|\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. Every nonempty proper subset of a cycle has at least two boundary edges: on traversing the cycle one must enter and leave the set. Therefore \[ \frac{|\delta(S)|}{\operatorname{vol}(S)}\ge\frac2{2|S|} \ge\frac1{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}. \] A consecutive block of $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ vertices has exactly two boundary edges and attains equality.

Index vertices by $j=0,\ldots,n-1$. The vectors $v_k(j)=e^{2\pi i k j/n}$, $0\le k<n$, are eigenvectors of $A$ with eigenvalues $2\cos(2\pi k/n)$, by adding the two neighboring coordinates. They are a basis because their inner products are zero for distinct $k$: the corresponding geometric series sums to zero. Thus the normalized Laplacian eigenvalues are $1-\cos(2\pi k/n)$, with the smallest positive value at $k=1,n-1$. Finally $1-\cos(2t)=2\sin^2t$ and $\sin t/t\to1$, while $\lfloor n/2\rfloor/n\to1/2$, give the stated limit. ∎

The attempted inference "a square-root spectral loss exists, therefore every polynomial algorithm encounters that computational barrier" is unsupported. These cycles witness the exponent but are solved exactly in constant-length arithmetic once $n$ is known. They do not refute NP-hardness on general graphs; they show that inequality sharpness supplies no reduction by itself.

Proposition: Let $G$ have $M\ge1$ edges and no isolated vertices. If $G$ is connected, then $h_G\ge1/M$. If it is disconnected, then $h_G=0$. Hence on promised inputs with $0<\phi<1/M$, the original two alternatives are decided by a connectivity test: disconnected graphs are in the first alternative, and connected promised graphs are in the second.
Proof: The total volume is $2M$. In a connected graph every eligible nonempty proper set has at least one boundary edge and volume at most $M$, proving the lower bound. In a disconnected graph, choose a component with volume at most half the total (one exists because there are at least two positive-volume components). It has positive volume and no boundary, so the minimum is zero. When $\phi<1/M$, a connected graph cannot meet $h_G\le\phi$. Under the separated promise in the original statement it must therefore meet the other alternative. Breadth-first search decides connectivity in $O(|V|+|E|)$ operations. ∎

This is a promise-restricted observation, not an algorithm for the entire source range of $\phi$. In particular, an alleged reduction producing only $\phi<1/M$ outputs would reduce its source problem to connectivity; establishing useful hardness must address a parameter regime not disposed of in this way (unless it also proves a collapse of the usual complexity classes).

A normalization check.
For $K_n$, $n\ge2$, a set of $k\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ vertices has boundary $k(n-k)$ and volume $k(n-1)$, so $h_{K_n}=\lceil n/2\rceil/(n-1)$. This checks the half-volume convention and includes $K_2$, where conductance is one.

Verification and remaining gaps

The script exhaustively enumerated all eligible vertex subsets for cycles $3\le n\le12$ and cliques $2\le n\le9$; every value matched the displayed exact formulas. The self-audit checked disconnected graphs, the strict range $\phi<1/M$, the second positive eigenvalue for odd and even cycles, and the distinction between a promise guarantee and a test on arbitrary inputs. No polynomial reduction from an NP-complete language was built, no absolute hardness constant $c$ was established, and no algorithm for all promised inputs was obtained. These are the original-target gaps, so the worked spectral examples and boundary regime count as no new completion of the hardness problem.
