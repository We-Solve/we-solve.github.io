OBJECTIVE AND SOURCE REVIEW

Erdős #212; independent attempt, 2026-09-23. Can a rational-distance set be dense in the plane? I read all of 212.tex: its quadratic-field normalization, genus-two line obstruction and inversion argument. Here is an elementary obstruction to constructing such a set as an additive group, requiring no algebraic-geometry theorem.

WORK: ADDITIVE CLOSURE IS IMPOSSIBLE

An additive subgroup $G\subset\mathbb R^2$ whose elements all have rational norm lies on a line. Suppose otherwise that $u,v\in G$ are independent. Write $a=|u|\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$, $b=u\cdot v\in\mathbb Q$, $c=|v|^2\in\mathbb Q$. Rationality of $b$ follows by subtracting $|u+v|^2-|u|^2-|v|^2$. Choose a positive integer $K$ such that $A=Ka$, $B=Kb/a$, and $K^2c$ are integers. Then, for every integer $m\ge1$, \[ (K|mu+v|)^2=(Am+B)^2+D,\qquad D=K^2c-B^2>0. \] Here $D$ is a positive integer, by independence and Cauchy--Schwarz. The left side is a rational square and an integer, so it must be the square of an integer (reduce a rational square root to lowest terms). For sufficiently large $m$, however, \[ (Am+B)^2<(Am+B)^2+D<(Am+B+1)^2, \] an impossibility. This proves the claim.

ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION AND REMAINING GAP

Only closure under $mu+v$ is used; general rational-distance sets have no such closure. The integer scaling is chosen once, independent of $m$. The positivity $D>0$ fails exactly in the collinear case, where rational additive groups can indeed be dense on a line. The lemma rules out dense additive groups and their translates as constructions, but gives no obstruction to irregular countable sets lacking additive closure. No extension theorem or unrestricted nonexistence result is obtained.
