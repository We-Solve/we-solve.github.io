Circuit-Size Hierarchy in Bounded Arithmetic

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

A Boolean circuit is an acyclic graph of Boolean gates computing a function on $n$ input bits; circuit size counts gates. A circuit-size hierarchy theorem separates classes of functions computable within different asymptotic circuit-size budgets, under the source gate and encoding conventions. The bounded-arithmetic theory $\mathrm{PV}_1$ formalizes polynomial-time reasoning with symbols for polynomial-time functions and its specified induction axioms. A language is in polynomial time if membership is decided by a uniform polynomial-time algorithm. An explicit circuit lower bound supplies such a uniform language together with a proved lower bound for its circuit sizes.

Statement recorded in the supplied source.
Does the bounded-arithmetic theory $\mathrm{PV}_1$ prove the Circuit-Size Hierarchy theorem? If it does, can one extract from such a proof explicit polynomial-time languages with the corresponding circuit lower bounds? [S2]

A classical hierarchy argument and its formalization gap

The source asks for provability inside weak arithmetic, not merely a classical existence proof. The 2025 primary paper reports circuit hierarchy results in a stronger theory and a restricted formula hierarchy in $\mathrm{PV}_1$; those do not establish the full recorded $\mathrm{PV}_1$ claim. We attempt a direct counting-and-interpolation route and identify the non-effective step. Count fan-in-two Boolean circuits with at most $s$ gates, allowing all 16 binary truth tables, two constants, and an output chosen among inputs, constants and gates. A topological encoding gives at most \[ (s+1)(n+s+2)[16(n+s+2)^2]^s \] circuits: sum the bounds for each number of gates from zero to $s$, overcounting predecessor choices by $n+s+2$.
Proposition: For $n\ge10$, some $n$-input Boolean function has circuit complexity exceeding $s=\lfloor2^n/(10n)\rfloor$. Consequently, for fixed real $a>b\ge1$ and all sufficiently large $n$, there exists a Boolean function of circuit complexity strictly between $n^b$ and $n^a$.
Proof: For the stated $s$, both $s+1$ and $n+s+2$ are at most $2^n$. Hence the base-two logarithm of the count is at most $2n+s(2n+4)$. For $n\ge10$, $2n\le2^n/10$ (true at ten and preserved by increasing $n$), and $s(2n+4)\le(6/25)2^n$. The count is therefore less than $2^{2^n}$, the number of truth tables. This proves the first assertion. For the second, put $S=n^b$. Eventually $S<s$. Choose a function $f$ of complexity greater than $s$, list its accepting inputs, and form partial disjunctions of the corresponding exact-input minterms, starting at the constant-zero function. Adding a minterm requires at most $n$ negations, $n-1$ conjunctions and one disjunction, at most $2n$ new gates. Thus the first partial function $h$ whose minimum circuit complexity exceeds $S$ has complexity at most $S+2n$: the preceding partial function has a circuit of size at most $S$ to which the new minterm can be added. Since $a>b\ge1$, $n^b+2n<n^a$ for all sufficiently large $n$. This $h$ has the required intermediate size. ∎
The definition of the first crossing uses the minimum circuit complexity of successive exponentially described truth tables. The proof supplies no polynomial-time method for testing that condition, no polynomially bounded sequence of formal witnesses, and no derivation in $\mathrm{PV}_1$. Replacing this semantic search by an efficient construction is the first unsupported inference in an attempted weak-arithmetic proof. Classical counting alone does not license it.

Verification and remaining gaps

The numerical inequalities were checked over a range of $n$ using integer/rational bounds, and small truth tables verify that adding one minterm changes the represented function exactly as stated. These are ordinary finite computations outside a weak-theory proof. The proposition is a classical auxiliary existence argument, not a new circuit lower bound or a resolution of the requested formal provability.
