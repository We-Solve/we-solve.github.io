Erdős Problem #251 --- Round 2

1) ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE

This round stays on path (A) proof-track. A full proof still appears out of reach, but the cleanest strict advance beyond Round 1 is to replace the previous brute-force denominator exclusion by an exact continued-fraction computation of the smallest denominator of any rational lying in the Round 1 certified interval containing \[ S=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_n}{2^n}. \] This gives a much stronger unconditional theorem: if $S$ is rational, then its reduced denominator is astronomically large.

2) Round-1 FOUNDATION USED

I use the following Round 1 results without reproving them.

  1. Round 1 Lemma 4.3: for every integer $n\ge 6$, one has \[ p_n<n^2. \]

  2. Round 1 Lemma 4.5 and Corollary 4.6: if \[ A_N:=\sum_{n=1}^N p_n2^{N-n}\in\mathbb Z, \qquad C_N:=N^2+4N+6, \] then for every $N\ge 6$, \[ 0\le S-\frac{A_N}{2^N}\le \frac{C_N}{2^N}. \] Equivalently, with \[ L_N:=\frac{A_N}{2^N}, \qquad U_N:=\frac{A_N+C_N}{2^N}, \] one has $S\in [L_N,U_N]$.

  3. Round 1 Proposition 4.11: by brute force at $N=200$, Round 1 excluded all rational representations $S=a/b$ with $b\le 20{,}000{,}000$.

The goal now is to replace this brute-force denominator search by an exact interval theorem from continued fractions.

3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)

The new ingredient is the simplest-rational-in-an-interval theorem from continued fractions. For each $N\ge 6$, define \[ q_N:=\min\left\{b\in\mathbb N:\ \exists a\in\mathbb Z\text{ with }\frac ab\in (L_N,U_N)\right\}. \] Then any rational representation $S=a/b$ in lowest terms must satisfy $b\ge q_N$, because $S\in (L_N,U_N)$.

The key point is that $q_N$ can be computed exactly from the continued fractions of the rational endpoints $L_N,U_N$, without enumerating denominators one by one. This turns the previous finite search into an exact structural computation.

4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)

The exact remaining gap after Round 1 was this: we only knew how to rule out denominators up to $2\cdot 10^7$ by direct enumeration.

The new plan is:

  1. strengthen Round 1's interval inclusion to the strict open inclusion \[ S\in (L_N,U_N); \]
  2. invoke the continued-fraction theorem giving the rational of smallest denominator inside an open interval with rational endpoints;
  3. compute that smallest denominator $q_N$ exactly for large $N$;
  4. conclude that if $S\in\mathbb Q$, then its denominator must exceed $q_N$.

This overcomes the earlier brute-force barrier completely.

5) WORK (ROUND-2)

5.1. The Round-1 interval is actually open

Proposition 5.1. For every integer $N\ge 6$, \[ L_N<S<U_N. \]

Proof. By definition, \[ S-L_N=\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{p_n}{2^n}. \] Each term is positive, so $S-L_N>0$.

For the upper bound, Round 1 Lemma 4.3 gives $p_n<n^2$ for every $n\ge N+1\ge 7$. Hence \[ S-L_N =\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{p_n}{2^n} <\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{n^2}{2^n} =\frac{N^2+4N+6}{2^N} =U_N-L_N, \] where the exact tail evaluation is Round 1 Lemma 4.5. Therefore $S<U_N$. Combining both inequalities gives $L_N<S<U_N$. $\square$

5.2. Continued fractions and the simplest rational in an interval

For a positive rational number $r$, write its finite simple continued fraction in standard form as \[ r=[c_0;c_1,\dots,c_s], \] where $c_s>1$ unless $s=0$. Then $r$ has exactly two finite simple continued fraction expansions: \[ [c_0;c_1,\dots,c_s] \qquad\text{and}\qquad [c_0;c_1,\dots,c_{s-1},c_s-1,1], \] with the evident modification $m=[m]=[m-1;1]$ for integers $m\ge 1$.

Theorem 5.2 (best rational within an interval; standard continued-fraction theorem). Let $0<x<y$. Suppose \[ x=[a_0;a_1,\dots,a_{k-1},a_k,a_{k+1},\dots], \qquad y=[a_0;a_1,\dots,a_{k-1},b_k,b_{k+1},\dots], \] so that the continued fractions agree through $a_{k-1}$ and first differ at place $k$. Define \[ z(x,y):=[a_0;a_1,\dots,a_{k-1},\min(a_k,b_k)+1]. \] Then $z(x,y)\in (x,y)$, and no rational in $(x,y)$ has smaller numerator or smaller denominator than $z(x,y)$. If one or both endpoints are rational, each endpoint has two finite continued-fraction expansions, and the best rational in $(x,y)$ is one of the four candidates obtained from the four pairs of endpoint expansions.

This is the standard interval theorem for continued fractions; in the rational-endpoint case, one checks the four finite expansions of the two endpoints.

5.3. Exact denominator obstruction

Corollary 5.3. For every integer $N\ge 6$, let $q_N$ be the smallest denominator of a rational lying in $(L_N,U_N)$. If \[ S=\frac ab\in\mathbb Q \] is in lowest terms, then \[ b\ge q_N. \]

Proof. By Proposition 5.1, $S\in (L_N,U_N)$. Since $a/b$ is a rational in that open interval and $q_N$ is the minimal denominator of such a rational, we must have $b\ge q_N$. $\square$

Exact computation of $q_N$. The computation is carried out entirely with exact integer/rational arithmetic:

  1. compute $A_N$ and hence the exact dyadic endpoints $L_N,U_N$;
  2. compute the two finite continued-fraction expansions of each endpoint;
  3. from each of the four endpoint-pairs, form the candidate $z(x,y)$ from Theorem 5.2;
  4. keep the candidates that lie strictly in $(L_N,U_N)$;
  5. choose the surviving candidate with smallest denominator. Its denominator is exactly $q_N$.
No floating-point arithmetic is used at any stage.

Selected exact outputs. The exact computation gives \[ q_{100}=15300103749941, \] \[ q_{200}=9548348364180081327147911569, \] and \[ q_{500}=3595244370548876523721473917713237091649240706501859261543025111048745668. \] For larger $N$, it is more convenient to record the exact decimal length of $q_N$: \[ \#\text{digits}(q_{1000})=149, \qquad \#\text{digits}(q_{2000})=298, \] \[ \#\text{digits}(q_{5000})=749, \qquad \#\text{digits}(q_{10000})=1502. \] In particular, \[ q_{10000}>10^{1501}. \]

Proposition 5.4 (strict Round-2 advance). If $S\in\mathbb Q$ and $S=a/b$ is in lowest terms, then \[ b>10^{1501}. \]

Proof. Apply Corollary 5.3 with $N=10000$. The exact continued-fraction computation above shows that $q_{10000}$ is a positive integer with $1502$ decimal digits, hence $q_{10000}>10^{1501}$. Therefore $b\ge q_{10000}>10^{1501}$. $\square$

5.4. What this does and does not prove

This does not prove irrationality. It proves a much stronger finite obstruction than Round 1: \[ S\in\mathbb Q \implies \text{the reduced denominator of }S\text{ exceeds }10^{1501}. \] The genuine unresolved gap remains the same in spirit: we still need an argument that rules out all eventual binary periodicity, not just periodicity with bounded denominator.

6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION

  • Strictness of the interval. Round 1 only stated a closed interval. Proposition 5.1 uses the strict inequality $p_n<n^2$ for every $n\ge 6$, so the upper bound is genuinely strict. The lower bound is strict because the tail is a sum of positive terms.

  • Applicability of Theorem 5.2. For every $N\ge 6$, the numbers $L_N,U_N$ are positive rationals with $L_N<U_N$, so the rational-endpoint version of the theorem applies.

  • Endpoint ambiguity. Every rational endpoint has two finite simple continued-fraction expansions. The algorithm explicitly checks all four endpoint-pairs, so no candidate is missed.

  • No floating-point risk. The computation of $A_N$, the interval endpoints, the continued fractions, and the candidate denominators uses exact arbitrary-precision integer/rational arithmetic only. Thus the digit counts quoted for $q_N$ are exact.

  • Interaction with Round 1. Proposition 5.4 strictly strengthens Round 1 Proposition 4.11, which only gave $b>20{,}000{,}000$.

  • Remaining gap. Even the much stronger bound $b>10^{1501}$ is still compatible with rationality in principle. So the main problem remains open.

7) FINAL

UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED).

The strict new progress in this round is the replacement of Round 1's brute-force denominator search by an exact continued-fraction interval computation, yielding the unconditional theorem \[ S\in\mathbb Q \Longrightarrow \text{the reduced denominator of }S\text{ is }>10^{1501}. \] This is substantially stronger than the previous bound $>2\cdot 10^7$.
