FORMAL RESTATEMENT

Erdős #34; reconstruction, 2026-09-23. For a permutation $\pi$ of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, let $S(\pi)$ count the distinct sums of nonempty intervals of positions. Must $S(\pi)=o(n^2)$ uniformly over permutations? We construct permutations of every even size $n=2m$ having at least $m(m+1)$ distinct interval sums. In particular the proposed assertion is false.

QUICK LITERATURE AND CONTEXT CHECK

The problem page marks the assertion disproved, crediting the first counterexample to Hegyvári. Konieczny's primary paper gives the simple alternating permutation used below in Proposition 1.1. We reconstruct its proof with explicit ranges for the sums. This is a known construction, not a new improvement of the extremal constant.

WORK

Let $H=2m+1$ and put \[ \pi(2i-1)=i,\qquad \pi(2i)=H-i\qquad(1\leq i\leq m). \] Thus the permutation is $1,2m,2,2m-1,\ldots,m,m+1$. Every pair beginning in an odd position sums to $H$.

Consider intervals of odd length $2k+1$, with $0\leq k\leq m-1$. An interval beginning at position $2i-1$ consists of $k$ complete odd-position pairs and one final entry $i+k$. Its sum is \[ kH+i+k\qquad(1\leq i\leq m-k). \tag{1} \] An interval beginning at position $2i$ consists of the initial entry $H-i$ followed by $k$ complete odd-position pairs. Its sum is \[ kH+H-i\qquad(1\leq i\leq m-k). \tag{2} \] For fixed $k$, the residues modulo $H$ in $1$ run through $k+1,\ldots,m$, while those in $2$ run through $m+k+1,\ldots,2m$. They are nonzero, distinct within each range, and the ranges are disjoint. Furthermore all these sums have quotient $k$ upon division by $H$. Sums arising from different $k$ therefore cannot coincide. Consequently all odd-length interval sums of this permutation are distinct. Their number is \[ \sum_{k=0}^{m-1}2(m-k)=m(m+1)=\frac{n^2}{4}+\frac n2. \] This establishes $S(\pi)/n^2\geq1/4$ along infinitely many $n$ and disproves $o(n^2)$. For an odd size $n=2m+1$, append the unused integer $2m+1$ to this permutation of $[2m]$. Every previously counted interval remains, giving $S(\pi)\geq m(m+1)=(n^2-1)/4$ as well. $\square$

VERIFICATION

For $n=6$, the permutation $1,6,2,5,3,4$ has odd-length sums \[ \{1,2,3,4,5,6\},\quad\{9,10,12,13\},\quad\{17,20\}. \] These twelve values match $m(m+1)$ for $m=3$. The argument never assumes that even-length sums are distinct or disjoint from the counted sums; they can only increase $S(\pi)$. The script accompanying this batch checks the formulas and distinctness for every even $n\leq200$.

FINAL STATUS

SOLVED (known negative answer reconstructed). The complete counterexample establishes a quadratic lower bound for the number of consecutive sums. Determining the optimal asymptotic constant is a further question, not a gap in answering the stated one. No novelty or independent formal-verification claim is made.
