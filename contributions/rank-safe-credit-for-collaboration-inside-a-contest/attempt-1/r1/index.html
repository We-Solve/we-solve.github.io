Attempt: private incentives versus repeated cooperation

The original competition rule is unspecified. I tested one limited incentive conflict rather than silently inventing a ranking mechanism. In a public-goods game, $n\ge2$ participants choose whether to contribute; each contribution costs its author $c$ and gives each participant benefit $b/n$. Assume $b/n<c<b$. This models private temptation to withhold a shared contribution, not an actual grading contest.
Proposition: Noncontribution is strictly dominant in the one-period game although universal contribution has higher total payoff. In the infinitely repeated game with common discount factor $\delta\in[0,1)$ and perfect monitoring, the strategy "contribute until any deviation, then never contribute" is an equilibrium after every history exactly when \[ \delta\ge\frac{nc-b}{b(n-1)}. \]
Proof: Changing one's action to contribution raises one's benefit by $b/n$ and costs $c$, giving negative net change regardless of other actions. Universal noncontribution is therefore the unique stage equilibrium with payoff zero. Universal contribution gives each participant $b-c>0$.

While cooperation continues, obeying forever gives discounted payoff $(b-c)/(1-\delta)$. Deviating to noncontribution while the other $n-1$ participants contribute gives immediate payoff $b(n-1)/n$ and future zero under the punishment. Cooperation is preferable exactly when the former is at least the latter; rearranging gives the displayed threshold. In the punishment phase, noncontribution is a stage best response and cannot restart cooperation, so deviation is not beneficial. Any alternative strategy with a first deviation has the same comparison discounted to that first deviation time; subsequent unilateral contributions only reduce payoff. Thus the condition is sufficient after every history. If it fails, immediate deviation at a cooperative history is profitable, proving necessity. ∎

Verification and remaining gaps

For $n=2,b=3,c=2$, the threshold is $1/3$. At $\delta=1/2$, continued payoff two exceeds the deviation payoff $3/2$. This known repeated-incentive calculation depends on perfect monitoring, patience and a credible continuing interaction. It neither provides a rank-safe contest rule nor proves that punishment is educationally desirable. The actual competition, cooperation objective and acceptable mechanism remain unspecified by the source.
