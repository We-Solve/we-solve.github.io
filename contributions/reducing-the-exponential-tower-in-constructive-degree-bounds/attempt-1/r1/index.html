Research attempt: Low-dimensional certificates and the denominator obstruction

Univariate factorization gives a small certificate over the reals.
Let $p\in\mathbb R[t]$ be nonnegative on $\mathbb R$. If $p=0$, its zero-square representation suffices. Otherwise its degree is even, say $2d$, its leading coefficient is positive, and each real root has even multiplicity: an odd multiplicity would change the sign across that root. Using the fundamental theorem of algebra, factor the remaining nonreal roots into conjugate pairs. Thus $p$ is a positive constant times even powers of real linear factors and positive quadratics of the form \[ (t-a)^2+b^2,\qquad b>0. \] Each factor is a sum of two squares, and \[ (u^2+v^2)(r^2+s^2)=(ur-vs)^2+(us+vr)^2 \] combines them. Induction gives $p=u^2+v^2$ with $u,v\in\mathbb R[t]$ of degrees at most $d$. The degree bound follows because each multiplication adds at most half the degree of the multiplied factor. It is sharp in this representation class for $p=t^{2d}$: squares of polynomials of degree less than $d$ cannot have degree $2d$. This is a known elementary univariate argument. It uses real factor coefficients; for rational input it does not by itself produce a rational-coefficient certificate over the original ordered coefficient field.
The polynomial-SOS shortcut fails in two variables.
Consider the Motzkin polynomial \[ M(x,y)=x^4y^2+x^2y^4+1-3x^2y^2. \] It is nonnegative: for nonnegative $a,b,c$, $a+b+c\ge3(abc)^{1/3}$; applying this to $x^4y^2,x^2y^4,1$ gives the claim. One proof of this elementary inequality is to minimize $a+b+c$ at fixed positive product: replacing two unequal entries by their geometric mean decreases their sum, and a minimizer exists after restricting to a bounded sublevel set, so the minimum occurs when all three agree. A zero product is immediate.

Suppose $M=\sum_j p_j^2$ with real polynomials. Every $p_j$ has degree at most three: otherwise the highest homogeneous terms give a nonzero sum of squares of higher degree, which cannot cancel. The coefficient of $x^6$ forces every $x^3$ coefficient in $p_j$ to vanish, and similarly the coefficient of $y^6$ removes every $y^3$ term. Then the zero coefficient of $x^4$ forces every $x^2$ coefficient to vanish, and similarly every $y^2$ coefficient. The zero coefficients of $x^2$ and $y^2$ now force every $x$ and $y$ coefficient to vanish. The only possible remaining monomials in $p_j$ are $1,xy,x^2y,xy^2$. Their squares have $x^2y^2$ coefficient equal to the sum of the squares of the $xy$ coefficients. This is nonnegative, contrary to the coefficient $-3$ in $M$. Thus a general method cannot simply replace rational-function squares by polynomial squares.

An explicit denominator repair, with its actual degree.
Set $s=x^2+y^2$ and \[ F_1=xy(s-2),\qquad F_2=x(y^2-1),\qquad F_3=y(x^2-1). \] Expansion gives the exact identity \[ sM=F_1^2+F_2^2+F_3^2. \] Consequently in the rational-function field $\mathbb R(x,y)$, \[ M=\sum_{j=1}^3\left[\left(\frac{xF_j}{s}\right)^2+ \left(\frac{yF_j}{s}\right)^2\right]. \] The common denominator has degree two and the displayed numerators have degree at most five. This is an identity of rational functions, so the common zero of the denominator at the origin does not invalidate it; it is not a claim that each displayed quotient is defined everywhere as a real function. The example shows concretely how a failed polynomial certificate can be repaired, but supplies no control of denominator growth for arbitrary dimension and degree. Extending the univariate factorization proof to multivariate irreducible factors is precisely where the argument stops.
