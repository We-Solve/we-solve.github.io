Problem #382 (Round 2)

1) ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE

Path C (obstruction / structural reduction). The Round-1 route gave only the conditional-style philosophy "short prime gaps force short bad intervals" and an unconditional bound of order $v^{0.525}$. The new goal is to remove that dependence entirely and force a square-divisibility anchor inside every bad interval, obtaining a sharper unconditional upper bound and a cleaner description of the intervals themselves.

2) Round-1 FOUNDATION USED

I use the following Round-1 facts.

  1. Every bad interval contains no primes.
  2. Therefore every bad interval satisfies $v<2u$.
  3. Round 1 recorded explicit examples with $v-u=3,4,5$.

3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)

The new tool is the anchor-smooth reduction from Problem #380. Applied here, it says:

  1. if $[u,v]$ is bad and $L=v-u$, then $Q:=P\!\left(\prod_{m=u}^{v}m\right)$ satisfies $Q>L$;
  2. the interval contains a unique multiple of $Q$, and that multiple is divisible by $Q^2$;
  3. every term in the interval is $Q$-smooth.

This immediately turns a bad interval into a short run of consecutive $Q$-smooth numbers with a built-in square anchor.

4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)

The exact Round-1 gap was that the $v^{0.525}$ bound still came from general theorems on primes in short intervals. The new plan is:

  1. use the Round-2 structure theorem instead of prime-gap technology;
  2. deduce a direct unconditional inequality of square-root type;
  3. verify a much longer explicit example than the Round-1 examples.

5) WORK (ROUND-2)

Theorem 1 (square-root bound).
Let $[u,v]$ be bad and put \[ L=v-u,\qquad Q=P\!\left(\prod_{m=u}^{v}m\right). \] Then \[ L<Q\le \sqrt{v}. \] In particular, \[ v-u<\sqrt{v}. \]
Proof.
By the anchor-smooth reduction from Problem #380, we already know that $Q>L$ and that there is a unique $t\in [u,v]$ with $Q\mid t$, necessarily satisfying $Q^2\mid t$. Since $t\le v$, we get \[ Q^2\le t\le v, \] hence $Q\le \sqrt{v}$. Combining this with $L<Q$ gives \[ v-u=L<\sqrt{v}. \] This proves the theorem.
Remark (optional deeper strengthening).
A survey of Shorey and Tijdeman records that if $n,n+1,\dots,n+k-1$ are all composite and $n>100$, then the greatest prime factor of their product exceeds $4.42k$. Since every bad interval is prime-free, this gives for all sufficiently large bad intervals \[ Q>4.42(L+1). \] Together with $Q^2\mid t\le v$, one gets \[ L<\frac{\sqrt{v}}{4.42}. \] I do not need this stronger constant below; the elementary square-root bound already strictly improves Round 1.
Structural reformulation.
Every bad interval is now seen to be a block of consecutive $Q$-smooth integers of length $L+1<Q+1$ containing a $Q^2$-divisible term. Therefore the second question in Problem #382 can be rephrased as follows:

Can one find arbitrarily long runs of consecutive $Q$-smooth integers, of length still $<Q$, containing a term divisible by $Q^2$, with $Q$ prime?

This is more precise than the Round-1 heuristic description.

A verified example with $v-u=13$.
The Round-1 notes mentioned an informal forum claim of a length-$13$ example. I verified it exactly.

Let \[ q=4237033,\qquad q^2=17952448643089. \] Consider the interval \[ [q^2-11,\ q^2+2] = [17952448643078,\ 17952448643091]. \] Direct exact factorisation gives:

  1. $q^2-11=2\cdot 89\cdot 30253\cdot 3333767$;
  2. $q^2-10=3\cdot 43\cdot 153719\cdot 905329$;
  3. $q^2-9=2^3\cdot 5\cdot 7^2\cdot 8647\cdot 1059259$;
  4. $q^2-8=31\cdot 193\cdot 239\cdot 2129\cdot 5897$;
  5. $q^2-7=2\cdot 3^2\cdot 47\cdot 103\cdot 109\cdot 1890121$;
  6. $q^2-6=19\cdot 431\cdot 18287\cdot 119881$;
  7. $q^2-5=2^2\cdot 331\cdot 80209\cdot 169049$;
  8. $q^2-4=3\cdot 5\cdot 11\cdot 25679\cdot 4237031$;
  9. $q^2-3=2\cdot 37\cdot 877\cdot 2063\cdot 134089$;
  10. $q^2-2=7\cdot 937\cdot 5711\cdot 479263$;
  11. $q^2-1=2^4\cdot 3\cdot 53\cdot 3331\cdot 2118517$;
  12. $q^2=q^2$;
  13. $q^2+1=2\cdot 5\cdot 13\cdot 17\cdot 29\cdot 113\cdot 2478877$;
  14. $q^2+2=3^2\cdot 41\cdot 67\cdot 739\cdot 982603$.

Every prime shown above is at most $q$, and the middle term contributes $q^2$. Hence the largest prime factor of \[ \prod_{m=q^2-11}^{q^2+2} m \] is $q$, with exponent at least $2$. Therefore \[ [17952448643078,\ 17952448643091] \] is a bad interval, and \[ v-u=13. \]

By-product from Problem #383.
The new explicit primes found in the Round-2 work for Problem #383 give the following additional bad intervals: \[ [510569^2,\ 510569^2+7],\qquad [2869123^2,\ 2869123^2+8],\qquad [3092701^2,\ 3092701^2+9]. \] So there are rigorously verified bad intervals of lengths $7,8,9$, in addition to the verified length-$13$ example above.

6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION

I checked the following possible issues.

  1. Does $Q>L$ really follow for every non-singleton bad interval? Yes: this is exactly the new Sylvester--Schur step from Problem #380.
  2. Could the $Q$-multiple occur twice anyway? No, because two distinct multiples of $Q$ differ by at least $Q>L$.
  3. Did I overstate the structure by saying the interval contains $Q^2$ itself? No; the theorem only guarantees a $Q^2$-divisible term. The verified length-$13$ example happens to be centred at $q^2$, but this need not happen in general.
  4. Is the length-$13$ example merely borrowed from the forum? No. The forum comment suggested it; the displayed exact factorisations above verify it independently.

7) FINAL

UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED).

The new Round-2 gains are:

  1. a direct unconditional bound \[ v-u<\sqrt{v}, \] which is sharper than the Round-1 $v^{0.525}$ route;
  2. a rigorous reformulation of every bad interval as a short run of consecutive $Q$-smooth integers with a $Q^2$-divisible anchor;
  3. a fully verified explicit example with $v-u=13$;
  4. further verified examples of lengths $7,8,9$ coming from the new Problem #383 computations.

The two original questions in Problem #382 remain open.
