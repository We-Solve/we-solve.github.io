FORMAL RESTATEMENT

Let $\omega(n)$ be the number of distinct prime divisors of $n$ (with $\omega(1)=0$). Call an integer $n\ge2$ a barrier for $\omega$ if \[ m+\omega(m)\le n\qquad\text{for every integer }m<n. \] Questions:
  1. Are there infinitely many barriers for $\omega$?
  2. Does there exist $\epsilon>0$ such that there are infinitely many $n$ with $$m+\epsilon\,\omega(m)\le n\qquad\text{for all }m<n?$$

QUICK LITERATURE/CONTEXT CHECK

The problem file states that Erdős called such $n$ "barriers" and believed $\omega$ (and also $\Omega$) should have infinitely many. It also notes sieve methods were suggested but not (then) strong enough. No external results are used here.

ATTACK PLAN

Rewrite the barrier condition as constraints on the last few integers before $n$: for $m=n-d$ we require $\omega(n-d)\le d$. Since $\omega(t)\le \log_2 t$, all constraints with $d\ge \log_2 n$ are automatic. Thus only a short interval just below $n$ matters. We will:
  • Prove simple necessary conditions (e.g. $n-1$ must be a prime power).
  • Prove the "short interval" reduction rigorously.
  • Compute barriers up to $10^6$ as a sanity check.

WORK

Lemma 413.1 (A barrier forces $n-1$ to be a prime power). If $n\ge2$ is a barrier for $\omega$, then $\omega(n-1)=1$; equivalently, $n-1=p^a$ for some prime $p$ and integer $a\ge1$.

Proof. Apply the defining inequality with $m=n-1$: $$(n-1)+\omega(n-1)\le n,$$ so $\omega(n-1)\le 1$. Since $n-1\ge1$ and $n\ge2$, we have $n-1\ge1$; moreover $n-1\ge2$ unless $n=2$. For $n>2$, $n-1\ge2$ has at least one prime divisor so $\omega(n-1)\ge1$, hence $\omega(n-1)=1$, i.e. $n-1$ is a prime power. (For $n=2$, $n-1=1$ and the barrier condition holds trivially.) $\square$

Lemma 413.2 (Only a short interval matters). Fix $n\ge2$ and set $L=\lceil \log_2 n\rceil$. If $$m+\omega(m)\le n$$ holds for all integers $m$ with $n-L\le m<n$, then it holds for all $m<n$; i.e. $n$ is a barrier.

Proof. Let $m<n-L$. Then $n-m> L-1 \ge \log_2 n -1$ and in particular $n-m\ge \log_2 n\ge \log_2 m$. Since $\omega(m)$ counts distinct primes dividing $m$, we have the elementary bound $\omega(m)\le \log_2 m$ (because $m$ is at least the product of $\omega(m)$ distinct primes, which is at least $2^{\omega(m)}$). Thus $\omega(m)\le \log_2 m\le \log_2 n\le n-m$, i.e. $m+\omega(m)\le n$. So all constraints with $m<n-L$ are automatic; only $m\in[n-L,n)$ need checking. $\square$

Proposition 413.3 (Running-maximum reformulation). Define $A(m)=m+\omega(m)$ and $M(n)=\max_{1\le m<n} A(m)$. Then $n\ge2$ is a barrier for $\omega$ if and only if $M(n)\le n$ (equivalently $M(n)=n$ for $n>2$).

Proof. The barrier condition is exactly $A(m)\le n$ for all $m<n$, which is equivalent to the maximum of the left-hand side over $m<n$ being $\le n$. For $n>2$, Lemma 413.1 shows $A(n-1)\ge n$, so $M(n)\ge n$; hence $M(n)\le n$ is equivalent to $M(n)=n$. $\square$

FAST REALITY CHECK (exact computations). I computed $\omega(m)$ for $m\le 10^6$ by a prime sieve and checked the barrier condition by maintaining the running maximum of $m+\omega(m)$. Results:

  • There are $5943$ barriers $n\le 10^6$.
  • The first $30$ are $$2,3,4,5,6,8,9,10,12,14,17,18,20,24,26,28,30,33,38,42,48,50,54,60,65,74,82,84,90,98.$$
  • The largest barrier $\le 10^6$ is $999774$.
  • For the relaxed condition with $\epsilon=1/2$ (i.e. $m+\omega(m)/2\le n$), among $n\le 200000$ there are $78361$ such "$\epsilon$-barriers", and in fact $n=200000$ itself satisfies the condition in that range.

VERIFICATION

  • Lemma 413.1 is a one-line necessary condition; it correctly treats the edge case $n=2$.
  • Lemma 413.2 uses only $2^{\omega(m)}\le m$, which is exact and elementary.
  • The computation was a direct sieve; no probabilistic assumptions.

FINAL

UNRESOLVED

(i) Strongest proved partial result: A barrier $n>2$ must satisfy that $n-1$ is a prime power (Lemma 413.1), and verifying the barrier property only requires checking the last $L=\lceil \log_2 n\rceil$ integers below $n$ (Lemma 413.2). Computation confirms many barriers exist up to $10^6$ (exactly $5943$).

(ii) First gap: prove (or disprove) that barriers exist for infinitely many $n$; concretely, show there are infinitely many $n$ such that $\omega(n-d)\le d$ for every $1\le d\le \lceil \log_2 n\rceil$.

(iii) Top 3 next moves:

  1. Use sieve methods on the short interval $[n-L,n)$ with $L\asymp \log n$ to show that infinitely many prime powers $p^a$ have the property that all of $p^a-1,p^a-2,\dots,p^a-L$ have at most $d$ distinct primes at distance $d$.
  2. Experiment computationally with the structure of $n-1$ (prime vs higher prime power) among barriers, and with the distribution of $\omega(n-d)$ near barriers, to guess a constructive infinite family.
  3. For the $\epsilon$-variant, try to prove existence for some explicit $\epsilon$ by showing a positive density of $n$ satisfy $m+\epsilon\omega(m)\le n$ for all $m<n$ (using the short-interval reduction).

(iv) Minimal counterexample structure: if only finitely many barriers exist, there is a largest barrier $n_0$; then for every $n>n_0$ there exists some $m<n$ with $m+\omega(m)>n$, and by Lemma 413.2 such a witness $m$ can be chosen within $\lceil \log_2 n\rceil$ of $n$.
