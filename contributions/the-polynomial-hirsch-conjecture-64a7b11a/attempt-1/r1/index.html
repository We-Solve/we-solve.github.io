The Polynomial Hirsch Conjecture

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
$\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, and $\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$ denote the integers, rationals, reals and complex numbers. Any other conventions are specified locally.

Let $P\subseteq\mathbb R^d$ be a bounded convex polytope of affine dimension $d\ge1$; equivalently, it is the convex hull of finitely many points whose affine span is $\mathbb R^d$. A facet is a face of dimension $d-1$, and let $n$ be the number of facets. The graph $G(P)$ has the vertices of $P$ as graph vertices and its one-dimensional faces as graph edges. For vertices $v,w$, their graph distance is the smallest number of edges in an edge path from $v$ to $w$. The diameter $\operatorname{diam}G(P)$ is the maximum of these distances. [S2]

Statement recorded in the supplied source.
Are there constants $C>0$ and $k\in\mathbb N$, independent of $d$, $n$ and $P$, such that every bounded $d$-dimensional convex polytope with $n$ facets satisfies \[ \operatorname{diam}G(P)\le C(d+n+1)^k? \] Equivalently, is there a single polynomial in $d$ and $n$ that bounds the vertex-edge diameter of every such polytope? [S2] The exponent must be uniform across dimensions. A dimension-dependent polynomial or a bound with exponent $\log_2 d$ does not give the requested fixed polynomial. The question uses the vertex-edge graph, rather than the circuit diameter of a system of inequalities.

Short English statement

Can every pair of vertices of any bounded polytope be joined by an edge path whose length is bounded by one fixed polynomial in its dimension and number of facets?

Research attempt

The target is one fixed polynomial for all bounded polytopes, with dimension allowed to grow. The attempted disproof route was to take repeated Cartesian products of a polytope violating the linear Hirsch bound, hoping its diameter would multiply. The calculation below rules out that amplification mechanism. It is elementary polytope geometry, not a new diameter bound for arbitrary polytopes.
Proposition: If bounded full-dimensional polytopes $P_i$ have dimensions $d_i$, facet counts $n_i$, and graph diameters $D_i$, then their product has dimension $\sum_i d_i$, facet count $\sum_i n_i$, and graph diameter $\sum_i D_i$.
Proof: It suffices to treat two factors. A face exposed by the functional $(a,b)$ on $P\times Q$ is exactly $F_a(P)\times F_b(Q)$: attaining the maximum of $a\cdot x+b\cdot y$ requires attaining both separate maxima. Every face of a polytope is exposed, so this describes all faces, including a whole factor when its functional is zero. Dimensions add under products because the direction spaces occupy disjoint coordinates. Consequently, vertices are pairs of vertices. A one-dimensional face is an edge in one factor times a vertex in the other. A codimension-one face is a facet in one factor times the whole other factor, giving the asserted facet count.

Along any edge path from $(v,w)$ to $(v',w')$, each step changes exactly one coordinate. The number of changes in the first coordinate is at least $\operatorname{dist}_P(v,v')$, and likewise in the second. Conversely concatenate a shortest first-coordinate path and a shortest second-coordinate path. Thus \[ \operatorname{dist}_{P\times Q}((v,w),(v',w')) =\operatorname{dist}_P(v,v')+\operatorname{dist}_Q(w,w'). \] Choosing pairs attaining each factor diameter proves the result. Induction handles finitely many factors. ∎

For a fixed seed $P$, the $r$th product has $rd$ dimensions, $rn$ facets and diameter $rD$. Its Hirsch excess is $r(D-n+d)$, but its ratio $D/(n-d)$ stays fixed. Even a positive excess for every $r$ is only a linear lower bound in the growing parameters. More generally, if each chosen factor satisfies $D_i\le C(d_i+n_i+1)^k$, for fixed $C$ and integer $k\ge1$, write $a_i=d_i+n_i+1$. Then \[ \sum_iD_i\le C\sum_i a_i^k\le C(\sum_i a_i)^k \le C(2\sum_i(d_i+n_i))^k, \] where the middle inequality follows by expanding the nonnegative integer power and the last uses $d_i+n_i\ge1$. Thus products of uniformly polynomially bounded factors remain polynomially bounded. Allowing factors with already growing unbounded exponents merely relocates the original difficulty.

A boundary test.
An interval has two facets and diameter one. Its $r$th product has $2^r$ vertices but diameter $r$. Counting vertices, even exponentially many, cannot establish a superpolynomial diameter. A polygon with $m\ge3$ vertices has a cycle graph and diameter $\lfloor m/2\rfloor$: the two paths between vertices have lengths adding to $m$, so the shorter has at most that length, attained by opposite or nearly opposite vertices.

Verification and remaining gaps

An exhaustive breadth-first distance computation on $C_5\mathbin\square C_7$ returned diameter $5=2+3$. The proof, rather than this finite check, covers all bounded factors. The self-audit specifically corrected the tempting but false multiplication of diameters and checked that graph edges, not circuit steps, are being counted. The argument supplies no operation that produces a seed with superpolynomial diameter, and no upper bound on arbitrary polytopes. Both alternatives of the original problem remain unsettled by this attempt.
