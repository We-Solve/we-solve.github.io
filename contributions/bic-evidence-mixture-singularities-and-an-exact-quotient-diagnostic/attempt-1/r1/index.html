A singular Bernoulli mixture and the penalty missed by nominal parameter counting

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$. Specialized notation and index ranges follow the cited source and the definitions below.

The Bayesian information criterion uses $-2\log L(\widehat\theta)+k\log N$, where $k$ is parameter dimension and $N$ sample size. Its usual asymptotic justification approximates integrated likelihood under regular local identifiability assumptions. A finite mixture combines component distributions with nonnegative weights summing to one; coinciding components and zero weights can create singularities.

Verbatim supplied catalogue question

Question 17. Why does the BIC work for model fitting with mixture models? What is being approximated (and why is it good to approximate these things?) This question raised some controversy during the discussion following the talk which was later resolved. The main issue that is interesting in this situation is that the likelihood function for mixture models does not satisfy the necessary regularity conditions to apply the classical results about the BIC.

A concrete test of the proposed BIC explanation

The source asks why BIC works for mixture fitting, then explicitly notes that classical regularity assumptions fail. It does not identify a mixture family, a prior, or a model-selection loss. Here BIC means the regular Laplace approximation $\log L(\widehat\theta)-(d/2)\log n+O(1)$ to log integrated likelihood. We test whether using the nominal mixture parameter count $d$ can justify that approximation universally.
A three-parameter mixture with one observable parameter.
Let $0<q<1$, $1/4<\lambda<3/4$, and $|h|<\min(q,1-q)/2$. Set \[ p_1=q+(1-\lambda)h,\qquad p_2=q-\lambda h. \] Both probabilities are strictly between zero and one, and a mixture with weights $\lambda,1-\lambda$ has Bernoulli success probability $\lambda p_1+(1-\lambda)p_2=q$. This parameter region is genuinely three-dimensional: the inverse transformation is $q=\lambda p_1+(1-\lambda)p_2$, $h=p_1-p_2$, with the same $\lambda$, and its Jacobian in $(q,h)$ has determinant $-1$. Choose a proper prior by taking $q$ uniform on $(0,1)$, $\lambda$ uniform on $(1/4,3/4)$, and, conditionally on $q$, $h$ uniform on the displayed nonempty interval. This prior is explicitly specified; its density need not extend boundedly to the boundary $q=0,1$. For an ordered sample with $s$ successes in $n$ trials the likelihood is $q^s(1-q)^{n-s}$, independent of $\lambda,h$. Integrating out those two normalized conditional priors leaves exactly \[ Z_{n,s}=\int_0^1q^s(1-q)^{n-s}dq =\frac{s!(n-s)!}{(n+1)!}. \] For completeness, integration by parts gives $B(a,b)=aB(a-1,b+1)/(b+1)$ for integers $a\ge1,b\ge0$, where $B(a,b)=\int_0^1x^a(1-x)^b dx$. Iterating and using $B(0,k)=1/(k+1)$ proves the formula.
Interior penalty proved without a regular Hessian argument.
Take $n=2m$, $s=m$, $m\ge1$. The maximum likelihood is $4^{-m}$, and \[ \frac{Z_{2m,m}}{4^{-m}}= \int_{-1/2}^{1/2}(1-4u^2)^m du. \] For an upper bound use $1-v\le e^{-v}$, proved by differentiating $e^{-v}-1+v$ for $v\ge0$. Hence the integral is at most $\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-4mu^2}du=\sqrt\pi/(2\sqrt m)$. The Gaussian integral equality follows by squaring the integral of $e^{-x^2}$, using polar coordinates in the plane, and taking the positive square root. For a lower bound integrate over $|u|\le1/(4\sqrt m)$. On this interval, $(1-4u^2)^m\ge(1-1/(4m))^m\ge3/4$. The last inequality is Bernoulli's inequality $(1-v)^m\ge1-mv$, proved by induction for $0\le v\le1$. The interval length is $1/(2\sqrt m)$, giving lower bound $3/(8\sqrt m)$. Consequently \[ \log Z_{2m,m}=\log L_{\max}-\tfrac12\log n+O(1). \] The nominal three-parameter BIC instead subtracts $\tfrac32\log n$. The discrepancy grows as $\log n$, so it is not a missing constant such as the effect of finitely many label permutations. The reason is visible directly: two parameter directions leave every observation probability unchanged, so the likelihood Hessian cannot be nondegenerate in all three coordinates.
Boundary behavior is a separate issue.
For $s=0$, the same exact calculation gives $Z_{n,0}=1/(n+1)$ and likelihood supremum one as $q\downarrow0$. The log penalty is then $\log n+O(1)$, even though the observable family is still one-dimensional. Thus replacing nominal dimension by observable dimension alone does not justify a single formula at all sample limits. This statement concerns the explicitly chosen prior and data sequence, not every prior.
