It follows the argument from the symmetric first-order wave system and its analytically continued positive current, through the quaternionic determinant equation on moving fibers, to the corrected energy, the boundary and interior barriers, and the two-circle propagation estimate. The target, the reported theorem, and the scope of this report

The primary catalogue entry is Local Smoothing for the Euclidean Wave Equation, Math-273, with stable handle prb_94f7553139864cf78d6311e3db49c212 [catalogue]. The source snapshot throughout is the openai/math commit
\sourcecommit.
The original manuscript is dated October 25, 2026. All source-file links in the bibliography point to this snapshot, not to a moving branch.

Let $n$ denote spatial dimension, put $d=n-1$, and define \[ U(T)=e^{-\mathrm iT|D|},\qquad J=(1-\Delta)^{1/2},\qquad I=[1,2],\qquad p_{\mathrm c}=\frac{2n}{n-1}. \] The Sobolev order appearing in the catalogue's supercritical formulation is \[ (n-1)\left(\frac12-\frac1p\right)-\frac1p+\varepsilon =\frac{n-1}{2}-\frac np+\varepsilon. \] Thus its local-smoothing assertion for $p>p_{\mathrm c}$ is a time-averaged wave estimate with this order. The theorem reported in the source includes this range and also states the critical and subcritical positive-loss bounds.

Theorem (OpenAI's reported all-dimensional theorem): For every $n\ge2$, $2\le p<\infty$, and $\varepsilon>0$, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6d-61-69-6e} \|U(T)f\|_{L^p(\mathbb R^n\times I)} \le C_{n,p,\varepsilon}\|J^{s_+(p)+\varepsilon}f\|_{L^p(\mathbb R^n)},\qquad s_+(p)=\max\left\{0,\frac{n-1}{2}-\frac np\right\}, \end{equation} for Schwartz data $f$. At $p=2$, $\varepsilon=0$ is allowed.
The manuscript's introduction states this as thm:local-smoothing; scope document 079 describes the matching formal target [intro,scope]. The sign in the half-wave is immaterial to the catalogue comparison: conjugation changes $U(T)$ into $e^{\mathrm iT|D|}$ without changing either norm. For $p>p_{\mathrm c}$, $s_+(p)$ is exactly the catalogue's Sobolev exponent before the positive loss. This is the precise sense in which the reported theorem is a full solution of Math-273.

This report does not extend the statement to variable-coefficient equations, curved manifolds, arbitrary mixed space--time exponents, or a lossless endpoint. Nor does it transfer discovery credit to the preparer. Its purpose is to make clear what the cited proof does, why its intermediate constructions are needed, and which analytic implications connect it to the exact catalogue question.

The proof dependency chain and the critical frequency estimate

The source reduces the theorem to the following annular assertion.
Theorem (Reported critical annular estimate): For a smooth compactly supported symbol $\varphi$ away from zero and $N\ge2$, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-61-6e-6e-75-6c-61-72} \|U(T)\varphi(D/N)f\|_{L^{p_{\mathrm c}}(\mathbb R^n\times I)} \le C_{n,\varphi}[\log(2+N)]^{2/p_{\mathrm c}}\|f\|_{p_{\mathrm c}}. \end{equation}
This is the source theorem labelled thm:main [intro]. It is stronger than an $N^\varepsilon$ bound, but its logarithmic factor is not asserted to be optimal for arbitrary data. For example, in dimension three it reads as an $L^3$ space--time estimate with loss $(\log N)^{2/3}$; in dimension four the critical exponent is $8/3$.

The hard part is not the final interpolation. It is the production of (eq:annular) from an energy living in extra variables. The dependencies are as follows:

ConstructionMathematical task it performs
Continued wave currentRetains interference while producing a positive, column-divergence-free tensor.
Rank-one background and fiber equationTurn the square of a wave into an energy with the critical $2n/(n-1)$ power.
Corrected determinant energyRemove an indefinite determinant from the base-variable Laplacian and cancel unwanted cofactor boundary terms.
Interior and boundary barriersCompare energy to the observed wave and make the remaining positive flux exponentially small.
Two-circle comparisonPropagate from observed time back to initial time, with two logarithmic costs.

The principal notation is $h=N^{-1}$, a small frequency scale, and $a=Kh$, where $K$ is a large fixed constant. In addition to physical time $T$, the construction uses an auxiliary two-dimensional time variable $\tau$; its radius is $s=|\tau|$. Constants are chosen in a definite order, before $h$ tends to zero. Losing that order could turn an apparent uniform estimate into a frequency-dependent one.

Why analytic continuation is used instead of packetwise squaring

Start with finitely many Fourier modes in a fixed small angular cap of an annulus, on a fixed spatial torus. The final result is obtained by density, a finite cap partition, and a period-removal argument. Periodic localization makes the auxiliary integrations manageable; it is not a new hypothesis on the Euclidean initial data.

For a mode $e^{\mathrm i(X\cdot\xi-T|\xi|)}$, imaginary translation by $-\mathrm i\rho(1,\omega)$ gives the multiplier \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6c-69-67-68-74-66-69-6c-74-65-72} e^{\rho(\xi\cdot\omega-|\xi|)} =\exp\left(-\frac{\rho|\xi|}{2} \left|\omega-\frac\xi{|\xi|}\right|^2\right). \end{equation} It is a Gaussian directional filter, with angular width approximately $(N\rho)^{-1/2}$ when $N\rho$ is large. The calculation uses $|\omega|=|\xi/|\xi||=1$, so $1-\omega\cdot\xi/|\xi|=|\omega-\xi/|\xi||^2/2$.

The important operation is to filter the sum of the modes and then take its square. Squaring each mode separately first would destroy cross terms. Those terms are precisely what can make an incoming spherical wave focus. Consequently a positive quantity built only from independent mode energies would not automatically control the relevant $L^p$ norm. The source builds a positive conserved tensor from the continued full wave, rather than replacing it by such an incoherent sum [current].

The first-order system and the positive conserved current

Null coordinates expose the appropriate polarization

Write $X=(x,b)$ with $x\in\mathbb R^d$, and introduce \[ t=\frac{T+b}{\sqrt2},\qquad s_0=\frac{b-T}{\sqrt2}. \] In these coordinates the wave operator is $2\partial_t\partial_{s_0}+\Delta_x$. A cap mode can be written \[ u_{\kappa,v}=c_{\kappa,v} e^{\mathrm i\kappa(s_0+v\cdot x-|v|^2t/2)}, \qquad \kappa>0, \] with $v$ confined to a small fixed ball. Let $E_{ab}$ be matrix units in $n=d+1$ coordinates. The source uses symmetric matrices \[ C^t=2E_{00},\qquad C^{x_j}=E_{0j}+E_{j0},\qquad C^{s_0}=-\sum_{j=1}^dE_{jj}. \] At the mode covector, the negative matrix symbol is \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-73-79-6d-62-6f-6c} -C(p)=\kappa \begin{pmatrix}|v|^2&-v^{\mathsf T}\\-v&I_d\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation} Its quadratic form on $(w_0,w')$ is $\kappa|w'-vw_0|^2$. It is positive semidefinite and has kernel spanned by $E(v)=(1,v)$. Thus a vector-valued superposition with polarization $E(v)$ solves the symmetric first-order system. The extra power $\kappa^{d/2}$ in its normalization is chosen to match the later Gaussian window.

This calculation explains why the source does not simply use the usual physical stress tensor: the useful positivity is attached to a particular polarization and an enlarged set of variables. The conservation identities must then be checked for that enlarged object.

Two states and a sum of real squares

Take two real state vectors $w_1,w_2$ and the quadratic fluxes \[ q^\mu=\frac12\sum_{\ell=1}^2 w_\ell^{\mathsf T}C^\mu w_\ell. \] The continued wave is evaluated at the associated imaginary displacement. Combining the states into an auxiliary complex vector $z=(R,\zeta)$ makes its Gaussian factor proportional to \[ \exp\left(-\frac\kappa2|\zeta-vR|^2\right). \] The source defines differential fields from the state derivatives and the quadratic fluxes, and multiplies them by the continued vector wave. In its notation these are fields $Z_1,Z_2$, and the real tensor is \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-72-65-61-6c-63-75-72-72-65-6e-74} B=\Re\sum_{\ell=1}^2 Z_\ell\otimes\overline{Z_\ell}. \end{equation} This is a sum of four real rank-one squares, hence positive semidefinite.

Positivity alone is not conservation. There are two further identities. The first-order equation gives $\operatorname{div} Z_\ell=0$. The acceleration contribution in $\sum_\ell\nabla_{Z_\ell}\overline{Z_\ell}$ is purely imaginary. Taking the real part therefore leaves every real column of $B$ divergence free. Both identities depend on the specific paired-state construction; they are not generic properties of analytically continued functions. The full differentiated formulas are in the source current section [current].

There is also a rank obstruction worth making explicit. Although $B$ is positive, it is a sum of only four real squares. In larger ambient dimensions its ordinary determinant vanishes. The proof therefore cannot end by applying an off-the-shelf estimate directly to $\det B$. Instead it uses a component of this tensor as the density in a different determinant equation.

The lifted coordinates, tangency, and wave detection

The next transformation has to preserve the conservation identities while making the directional Gaussian explicit. With a passive auxiliary variable $\eta$, the source introduces the quadratic holomorphic flux map \[ \Theta(z,\eta)_\mu=\frac12z^{\mathsf T}C^\mu z+c_\mu\eta. \] For the wave system its null-coordinate version is \[ Y=R^2,\qquad P=R\zeta,\qquad S_1=\eta-\frac12\zeta\cdot\zeta. \] Consequently \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-66-69-72-73-74-69-6e-74-65-67-72-61-6c} W=P/Y,\qquad \Phi=S_1+\frac{P\cdot P}{2Y}=\eta, \qquad y=|Y|. \end{equation} The quadratic products here are the auxiliary complex bilinear products, not squared absolute values. Confusing these two products would destroy the holomorphic cancellation.

Under a nonlinear change of coordinates, divergence-free tensor columns do not in general remain divergence free merely by applying a Jacobian. The source verifies the transformed conservation law using holomorphicity and the equality of the two state blocks. The real Jacobian factor and the pushforward tensor density are part of this verification. This is a substantive step in the construction, not a coordinate convention that can be omitted.

The resulting wave tensor can be expressed as a positive quaternionic Hermitian rank-one tensor $A_0=\chi\chi^*$. The vector $\chi$ sums the full wave against windows of the form \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-77-69-6e-64-6f-77} m_{\kappa,v}(y,W) =\left(\frac\kappa y\right)^{d/2} e^{-\kappa y|W-v|^2/2}. \end{equation} The accompanying polarization, in the source's null coordinates, is \[ V^v=\left(1,\frac{v+W}{2},-\frac{W\cdot v}{2}\right). \] A direct substitution gives $\partial\Phi\,V^v=0$. Hence the tensor is tangent to the level sets of $\Phi$, a fact later used both for cutoffs and for the rank-one background.

The physical time component is still sensitive to the original wave. More precisely, the source writes $(A_0)_{TT}$ as the sum of squares of two scalar windowed waves. In the small cap and near the central window, the first scalar window is invertible on the cap. The corresponding inverse multiplier estimate is what permits a lower bound by the original wave after suitable averaging. No claim is being made that a single arbitrary Gaussian sample recovers the wave with a universal constant. The location of the window and the scale $y\asymp s$ matter [current,barrier].

The quaternionic Hessian calculus behind the energy

Why the determinant is an auxiliary spatial determinant

For each quaternionic coordinate the source uses three real coordinate slots, with quaternionic units $e_0=1,e_1=\mathrm i,e_2=\mathrm j$. Its gradient and Hessian operators have the schematic, and in its conventions literal, forms \[ G_a(u)=\sum_{\alpha=0}^2e_\alpha\partial_{a\alpha}u, \qquad H_{ab}(u)=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=0}^2 e_\alpha\overline{e_\beta}\, \partial_{a\alpha}\partial_{b\beta}u. \] The matrix $H(u)$ is quaternionic Hermitian and $\operatorname{tr} H(u)=\Delta u$. Positivity of a real Hessian implies positivity of this Hermitian Hessian. The product rule is \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-70-72-6f-64-75-63-74-68-65-73-73} H(uv)=uH(v)+vH(u)+G(u)G(v)^*+G(v)G(u)^*. \end{equation} In the appropriate auxiliary holomorphic variables, $H(|F|^2)$ is a positive Jacobian square. In particular, when $F$ is the single scalar first integral $\Phi$, the background Hessian has rank one.

The determinant used below is the Moore determinant of a Hermitian matrix. On positive matrices it is the product of the real eigenvalues. Its cofactor, written $\operatorname{cof}(A)$, satisfies \[ \operatorname{cof}(A)=\det(A)A^{-1} \] when $A$ is invertible, and extends polynomially to singular matrices. For positive semidefinite $A$ the cofactor is positive semidefinite. At a singular matrix, its image is confined to null directions of $A$. These facts allow the proof to exploit degeneracy rather than erase it by assuming every matrix is invertible [calculus].

The cofactor divergence identity

A central identity is that the real columns of $\operatorname{cof}(H(u))$ are divergence free. This is the analogue of the divergence structure of Hessian cofactors in real Monge--Ampere calculus. The source proves it through polarization, including at singular and indefinite Hessians.

The useful algebraic observation is that the cofactor is homogeneous of degree one less than the matrix size. Polarize it on rank-one symbols $g_jg_j^*$. The resulting mixed cofactor annihilates the relevant span of the $g_j$. Testing on exponential functions turns differentiation into multiplication by the corresponding frequencies; the annihilation gives the cancellation. Since the desired statement is a differential polynomial identity, the polarized calculation proves the identity for general smooth $u$.

It follows that, for a tensor with divergence-free real columns, pairing it with $H(u)$ is a real divergence. This permits the integrations by parts that produce the corrected energy. The identities are not restricted to a formal interior calculation: the boundary contractions still have to be evaluated.

Schur complement and normal compression

For a Hermitian block matrix with positive spatial block $D$, \[ H=\begin{pmatrix}\ell&c^*\\c&D\end{pmatrix},\qquad \sigma=\ell-c^*D^{-1}c, \] one has $\det H=\det D\,\sigma$ and $H-\sigma e_Te_T^*\succeq0$. This isolates the possibly negative scalar part of the full space--time Hessian.

A second identity identifies a normal cofactor contraction with the determinant of the compression to the normal's orthogonal complement. It is this compression, not the full determinant by itself, that controls a cofactor boundary flux. A common tangential null direction can make that compression singular and the flux vanish. The fiber geometry is designed to supply exactly such a direction.

The moving fibers and the Dirichlet equation

The geometry is part of the proof

Let $z=(T,\tau)$ denote the base variables and $s=|\tau|$. For a small fixed $w_0$, the source's defining function is \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-66-69-62-65-72} r=\frac{|W|^2}{w_0^2}+100|Q|^2-1, \qquad Q=\Phi/a, \qquad \Omega_z=\{r<0\},\quad a=Kh. \end{equation} The real spatial directions are periodic. On the relevant annulus in $s$, the change of variables gives $y\asymp s$, an auxiliary-volume factor comparable to $a^2s^{2d}$, and spatial Hessian scales \[ H_{\mathrm{fib}}(r)\succeq c s^{-2}I, \qquad \det H_{\mathrm{fib}}(r)\asymp a^{-2}s^{-2d}. \] The complete Hessian has a null direction which is tangent to the fiber wall and also belongs to the kernel of $H(|\Phi|^2)$. This is the shared direction needed in the normal-compression argument.

The positive tensor is regularized by spatial convolution and a cutoff in $Q$, followed by a small cofactor term. In the source's normalization it has the structure \[ A=A_w+h^{50n}\operatorname{cof}(H(r)),\qquad A_w=g(Q)(J_h*_X A_0),\qquad \mu=A_{TT}>0. \] Tangency to $\Phi$ makes the cutoff compatible with conservation. The cofactor term preserves the divergence law and has no outward wall flux of the type that must be controlled later. These choices prevent regularization from introducing an uncontrolled error in the central identity [energy].

A rank-one background creates the critical exponent

Set $B_0=\lambda|\Phi|^2$, with $\lambda=h^{-30n}$ in the source. Its spatial Hessian $B_{\mathrm{fib}}$ has one nonzero eigenvalue $\beta\asymp\lambda$. The construction solves \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-4d-41} D_u=H_{\mathrm{fib}}(B_0+u)>0, \qquad \det D_u=\lambda\mu, \qquad u|_{\partial\Omega_z}=0. \end{equation} The solution satisfies $u\le0$. Tangency gives $A:H(B_0)=0$, so this background has no cost in the current pairing, even though it contributes curvature to the spatial determinant.

The exponent can already be predicted. Adding isotropic curvature $\alpha I$ to the rank-one background changes the determinant to \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-72-61-6e-6b-6f-6e-65} \det(B_{\mathrm{fib}}+\alpha I)=(\beta+\alpha)\alpha^{n-1}. \end{equation} In the controlled range, the $\lambda$ factor cancels the right side of (eq:MA); the necessary curvature is of order $\mu^{1/(n-1)}$. The energy pairs a depth of this order with $\mu$, creating $\mu^{n/(n-1)}$. Since $\mu$ detects a squared wave, the resulting wave power is $2n/(n-1)=p_{\mathrm c}$. An unstructured full-rank background would not give this same determinant scaling.

What the existence theorem supplies

The source proves a Dirichlet theorem for periodic-real fibers with a smooth strictly Hessian-convex defining function, a semipositive background, and a positive density above its background determinant. It supplies a unique smooth admissible solution, comparison barriers of the form \[ b r\le u\le b' r,\qquad b\ge b'>0, \] and smooth dependence on locally varying parameters [dirichlet]. These are substantial analytic inputs. They are not assumed merely because one can write the equation.

The proof uses a continuity path between a known barrier density and the target density. Linearizing the logarithmic determinant at a positive matrix $D$ gives the operator $D^{-1}:H(w)$, whose real principal symbol is positive. The logarithmic determinant is concave. Comparison follows by averaging the linearizations between two admissible potentials and applying the maximum principle.

For the a priori estimates, the defining function gives a strict subsolution and controls the boundary gradient. Tangential second derivatives at the boundary are expressed through $u=\rho r$. Mixed normal--tangential derivatives are bounded using tangential rigid vector fields and barriers involving $|x|^2$ and $-r$. The restriction of the Hessian to the tangent complement has a uniform positive lower bound. The determinant equation and Schur complement then recover the missing pure normal derivative estimate.

The source temporarily introduces an additional periodic real slot to permit the full quaternionic rotation argument. Uniqueness and invariance under translations in that slot imply that the solution descends to the original three-slot setting. Interior second-derivative control, concavity, and uniform ellipticity permit regularity estimates and bootstrapping. Pulling moving fibers back to a fixed domain, followed by the implicit-function argument, gives smooth parameter dependence.

There is an important uniformity distinction. This theorem provides smooth solutions for each admissible regularized problem. It does not by itself give the frequency-uniform wave estimate. The estimates with controlled dependence on $h$, $s$, and $a$ are proved by the explicit barriers below.

Why the naive energy must be corrected

The natural positive quantity is \[ X_0(z)=\int_{\Omega_z}(-u)\mu. \] A calculation with the full Hessian of $B_0+u$ and its spatial block $D_u$ produces a Schur decomposition \[ H(B_0+u)=\sigma e_Te_T^*+\Sigma,\qquad \Sigma\succeq0. \] Since $\det D_u=\lambda\mu$ and $A:H(B_0)=0$, its pairing contains \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-75-6e-77-61-6e-74-65-64} A:H(u)=A:\Sigma+\lambda^{-1}\det H(B_0+u). \end{equation} The first term has a sign; the full determinant need not. Dropping the last term would therefore be invalid. The proof adds an energy whose variation cancels it exactly.

Define the fiber determinant energy \[ E_z(v)=\lambda^{-1}\int_{\Omega_z}v \int_0^1\det H_{\mathrm{fib}}(B_0+\theta v)\,\mathrm d\theta, \qquad \mathcal F=X_0+E_z(u). \] The interpolation in $\theta$ is the essential part of the definition. It is not an arbitrary correction chosen only to make the final inequality true. The cofactor differentiation formula, combined with its divergence identity, turns the variation of this integral into the determinant term occurring in (eq:unwanted) [energy,calculus].

The cancellation on a moving domain

The integrations by parts are carried out over the total moving-fiber domain, using a test function of the base variable $z$. Treating the fiber as fixed while differentiating in $z$ would miss boundary contributions. Writing $\rho=u/r$ at the wall, the resulting identity has the form \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-65-6e-65-72-67-79-69-64-65-6e-74-69-74-79} \Delta_z\mathcal F+\int_{\Omega_z}A:\Sigma =\int \rho\,G(r)^*A_wG(r)\,\delta(r)\,\mathrm d\zeta. \end{equation} Here the right-hand side is the boundary integral written by the coarea convention with $\delta(r)$. It is nonnegative.

Why are there not additional cofactor terms? At the wall, the Hessian appearing in the relevant normal compression is built from the rank-one background and a multiple of $H(r)$. Their common tangential null direction makes the compressed determinant vanish. The cofactor normal--normal contraction is therefore zero. The regularizing cofactor current has the same zero-flux property. What remains is the physical wave flux $A_w$ on the right of (eq:energyidentity). This precise boundary cancellation is one of the main reasons for the elaborate fiber construction.

The corrected energy remains positive

The source derives the identity \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-65-6e-65-72-67-79-63-6f-6d-70-61-72-69-73-6f-6e-69-64-65-6e-74-69-74-79} \mathcal F=\frac{n-1}{n+1}X_0+ \frac{1}{\lambda n(n+1)} \int_{\Omega_z}G_{\mathrm{fib}}(u)^* \operatorname{cof}(D_u)G_{\mathrm{fib}}(u), \end{equation} and the two-sided comparison \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-65-6e-65-72-67-79-63-6f-6d-70-61-72-69-73-6f-6e} \frac{n-1}{n+1}X_0\le\mathcal F\le\frac n{n+1}X_0. \end{equation} Thus the cancellation does not destroy positivity or change the relevant size of the energy.

The rank-one hypothesis makes this computation particularly transparent. Along the interpolation segment, \[ \det((1-\theta)B_{\mathrm{fib}}+\theta D_u) =\theta^n\det D_u+ (1-\theta)\theta^{n-1}\operatorname{cof}(D_u):B_{\mathrm{fib}}. \] Terms with two or more columns from the rank-one background vanish. Integrating these two powers of $\theta$ and then using the cofactor integration by parts gives (eq:energycomparisonidentity); positivity and the companion estimate give (eq:energycomparison). This is the point where the determinant structure yields an actual usable energy rather than just a formal identity.

Boundary decay and interior wave recovery

The next objective is to replace the right side of (eq:energyidentity) by a small multiple of $\mathcal F$ itself. An upper bound for the boundary flux alone is not enough: one also needs a lower bound for the energy that detects the same wave.

A barrier that controls the boundary normal derivative

The $Q$ cutoff is supported near the center of its allowed disk. At the portion of the wall where that cutoff is nonzero, (eq:fiber) forces $|W|$ to be bounded away from zero. The frequency directions satisfy $|v|\le\gamma$ for a much smaller fixed $\gamma$. Hence $|W-v|$ is uniformly bounded below there, and (eq:window) gives exponential decay $e^{-c_0s/h}$.

To make a comparison function robust under differentiation, the source uses a positive spatially smoothed density \[ P_*(X)=\left(J_{Ds}*|g_T|^2(X)\right)^{1/d}, \qquad g_T=U(T)f, \] with a broad smoothing kernel and a large fixed $D$. Its first two derivatives are controlled relative to itself at scale $Ds$, and \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-70-73-6d-6f-6f-74-68} \int P_*^{d+1}\lesssim\|g_T\|_{p_{\mathrm c}}^{p_{\mathrm c}}. \end{equation} The broad scale makes the mixed derivative errors in $H_{\mathrm{fib}}(rP_*)$ small compared with its positive principal part. A sufficiently large multiple of $(s/h)^m rP_*$ is therefore a determinant subsolution, where $m$ is a fixed exponent depending on the construction.

Comparison with $u$ bounds the boundary ratio $\rho=u/r$ and yields both an upper bound for the energy and an exponentially small flux estimate. With $\sigma=s/h$, the estimates have the schematic quantitative form \begin{align} \mathcal F(T,\tau)&\le C a^2\sigma^m\|g_T\|_{p_{\mathrm c}}^{p_{\mathrm c}}, \label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-75-70-70-65-72-46}\\ \Delta_z\mathcal F(T,\tau)&\le C(a/s)^2\sigma^m e^{-c_0\sigma}\|g_T\|_{p_{\mathrm c}}^{p_{\mathrm c}}. \label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-66-6c-75-78-46} \end{align} The symbols $C,m,c_0$ here stand for the fixed constants established by the source's barrier estimates, not quantities allowed to grow with frequency [barrier].

An interior comparison recovers the critical norm

Near the central window, the inverse scalar window multiplier gives a lower bound for the regularized density on small interior balls. In the source's scale notation, a useful form of the lower density is \[ m_X\gtrsim (hs)^{-d}\sigma^{-m}e^{-C\gamma^2\sigma}|g_T(X)|^2. \] The ball radius is comparable with $h$, and it lies inside the cutoff-one region when $K$ is sufficiently large.

On such a ball, use a negative quadratic potential with curvature proportional to $m_X^{1/d}$. Equation (eq:rankone), together with the density ceiling in the construction, makes its determinant no larger than $\lambda m_X$. The comparison principle forces the depth of $u$ at the center to be at least a constant multiple of $h^2m_X^{1/d}$. Multiplication by the density and integration over the interior windows give \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6c-6f-77-65-72-46} \mathcal F(T,\tau)\ge c a^2\sigma^{-m}e^{-C_1\gamma^2\sigma}\|g_T\|_{p_{\mathrm c}}^{p_{\mathrm c}}. \end{equation} The power $p_{\mathrm c}$ is the rank-one determinant mechanism of Section sec:dirichlet; the factor $a^2$ comes from the fiber-volume normalization and the wave-window scaling.

Combining (eq:fluxF) with (eq:lowerF), first choose $\gamma$ so small that $C_1\gamma^2<c_0/2$. Then choose $K$ large enough that the remaining polynomial factors in $\sigma$ are absorbed by exponential decay. This gives \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-73-63-61-6c-61-72-69-6e-65-71-75-61-6c-69-74-79} \mathcal F\ge0,\qquad \Delta_{T,\tau}\mathcal F \le C s^{-2}e^{-cs/h}\mathcal F, \qquad 2a\le s\le3. \end{equation} At the two particular radii needed later, the same estimates give \begin{align} \mathcal F(T,\tau)&\ge c a^2\|U(T)f\|_{p_{\mathrm c}}^{p_{\mathrm c}} &&(|\tau|=2a),\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-69-6e-6e-65-72-6f-62-73-65-72-76-65}\\ \mathcal F(0,\tau)&\le C a^2\|f\|_{p_{\mathrm c}}^{p_{\mathrm c}} &&(|\tau|=4a).\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6f-75-74-65-72-69-6e-69-74-69-61-6c} \end{align} The constants may depend on the fixed cap and $K$, but not on $h$. The order of choices is essential: shrinking the cap after choosing frequency-dependent constants would not prove the desired uniform estimate.

The two-circle propagation argument and its two logarithms

Let $H(T,s)$ be the circular average of $\mathcal F(T,\tau)$ over $|\tau|=s$. The analytic construction has now been reduced to a scalar comparison problem in one physical time and two auxiliary time coordinates.

For $0<b<1/4$, the source proves a general propagation lemma for a nonnegative smooth function $E$ on $\mathbb R_T\times\{\tau\in\mathbb R^2:b\le|\tau|\le3\}$, satisfying $\Delta_{T,\tau}E\le V(|\tau|)E$ in the interior, where $V:[b,3]\to[0,\infty)$ is continuous. If \[ \int_b^3 s\log(s/b)V(s)\,\mathrm d s\le M, \qquad \sup_{1/2\le s\le3/2}V(s)\le M, \] then its circular mean obeys \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-70-72-6f-70-61-67-61-74-69-6f-6e} H(0,r)\ge c(M) \frac{\log(r/b)}{[\log(3/b)]^2} \int_1^2 H(T,b)\,\mathrm d T, \qquad b\le r\le1. \end{equation} The detailed scalar lemma and its boundary comparisons are in [propagation]. The two logarithms arise from two distinct radial passages, not from an unexplained final loss.

The propagation lemma above now explicitly records the source hypothesis $0<b<1/4$, its full cylindrical domain, and the continuity and nonnegativity of the potential. These conditions were omitted from the original summary; the stated wave-equation application already satisfies them.

The radial equation becomes a one-dimensional problem

The relevant radial comparison equation is \[ u''(s)+s^{-1}u'(s)=(V(s)+\kappa^2)u(s). \] Set $x=\log(s/b)$. Then the left side becomes $s^{-2}u_{xx}$, so the comparison is a second-order equation on a logarithmic interval. A Volterra integral equation and a Gronwall estimate control its normalized positive solutions by the integral condition on $V$. When $V=\kappa=0$, the radial solutions are affine in $\log s$; this already explains why logarithms, rather than a power of $b$, govern communication between concentric circles.

Outward propagation, time transfer, and return

For the outward passage, integrate the circular mean against a positive sine weight in physical time. Integration by parts gives an inequality with a fixed spectral term $\kappa^2$; the boundary time contributions have the favorable sign. The radial comparison moves observed mass from radius $b$ to a fixed-radius annulus, with a factor comparable to $1/\log(3/b)$.

At fixed radius the potential is bounded. A local positive comparison or Harnack argument moves the resulting information in physical time to a neighborhood of initial time. This step has a fixed-scale constant, not a new small-radius logarithm.

The return passage uses a second positive time weight and a radial function vanishing at the inner boundary. Moving back from unit radius to $r$ costs another factor $\log(r/b)/\log(3/b)$. Multiplying the two radial costs gives (eq:propagation). In particular, when $r=2b$, the numerator is only $\log2$, so both inverse logarithms remain.

Closing the cap estimate

Apply the lemma with $b=2a$ and $V(s)=Cs^{-2}e^{-cs/h}$. Its weighted integral is bounded uniformly as $h\downarrow0$: after $s=bt$, the exponential is $e^{-2cKt}$, so the integral is controlled by an integrable function of $t$ independent of $h$. Taking $r=4a=2b$, and using (eq:innerobserve) and (eq:outerinitial), yields \[ a^2\int_1^2\|U(T)f\|_{p_{\mathrm c}}^{p_{\mathrm c}}\,\mathrm d T \lesssim [\log(2/h)]^2 a^2\|f\|_{p_{\mathrm c}}^{p_{\mathrm c}}. \] The $a^2$ factors cancel. Taking a $p_{\mathrm c}$-th root gives the periodic fixed-cap version of (eq:annular). The source observes that two logarithms can occur for the scalar comparison problem itself. This does not prove that the wave estimate's logarithmic power is optimal: the wave-generated energy is only one special class of scalar functions satisfying the inequality.

Removing the torus and deriving all Sobolev estimates

A finite angular cover

The cap aperture is fixed independently of $h$. A finite smooth partition of the frequency annulus therefore reduces general annular data to the cap estimate without introducing a growing number of caps. Each cap cutoff has a uniformly integrable spatial kernel after rescaling. Summing the finitely many bounds preserves the logarithmic power.

The off-cone kernel controls periodization errors

The Euclidean annular wave kernel is \[ K_h(T,X)=(2\pi h)^{-n}\int e^{\mathrm i(X\cdot\eta-T|\eta|)/h}\varphi(\eta)\,\mathrm d\eta. \] For $T\in[1,2]$ and $|X|\ge3$, the gradient of the unscaled phase has magnitude at least $|X|-2\ge|X|/3$. Repeated integration by parts therefore gives, for every sufficiently large integer $M$, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6f-66-66-63-6f-6e-65} |K_h(T,X)|\le C_Mh^M(1+|X|)^{-M}. \end{equation} The amplitude is supported away from zero, so its differentiated phase and symbol terms are uniformly controlled.

Tile Euclidean space by unit cubes $Q$. For points in $Q$, split the input into its restriction to $10Q$ and its complement. The far contribution is bounded using (eq:offcone) and Young's inequality. Periodize the near input on a torus of a fixed sufficiently large side, for example the source's side $100$. All nonzero translates of the kernel are off cone on $Q$ and contribute an $O(h^M)$ error. Apply the periodic estimate to the near part, raise to the $p_{\mathrm c}$-th power, and sum over cubes. The enlarged cubes have bounded overlap. This proves the Euclidean annular estimate without letting the torus size depend on frequency [transfer].

Interpolation gives exactly the stated derivative threshold

At $p=2$, Plancherel and the length-one observation interval give a uniform bound. At $p=\infty$, the annular wave kernel has $L^1$ norm $O(N^{d/2})$. One way to see the latter is to split the annulus into $O(N^{d/2})$ angular caps of aperture $N^{-1/2}$; each rescaled cap kernel has uniformly bounded $L^1$ norm after its linear phase is removed.

For $2\le p\le p_{\mathrm c}$, interpolate the $L^2$ estimate with (eq:annular). If $1/p=(1-\theta)/2+\theta/p_{\mathrm c}$, then $2\theta/p_{\mathrm c}=d(1-2/p)$. No positive power of $N$ appears. For $p_{\mathrm c}\le p<\infty$, interpolate the critical estimate with $L^\infty$. The weight of the critical estimate is $p_{\mathrm c}/p$, so the power of $N$ is \[ \frac d2\left(1-\frac{p_{\mathrm c}}{p}\right)=\frac d2-\frac np. \] Together these give \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-61-6c-6c-70} \|U(T)P_Nf\|_{L^p_{T,X}} \lesssim N^{s_+(p)}[\log(2+N)]^{\beta(p)}\|f\|_p, \quad \beta(p)=\min\{d(1-2/p),2/p\}. \end{equation} The two formulas for $\beta$ agree at $p_{\mathrm c}$.

The positive loss is what makes frequency summation immediate

Set $g=J^{s_+(p)+\varepsilon}f$ and take a smooth dyadic frequency partition. On the annulus $N=2^j$, a rescaled multiplier estimate gives \[ \|U(T)P_{2^j}f\|_{L^p_{T,X}} \lesssim 2^{-j\varepsilon}(1+j)^{\beta(p)}\|g\|_p. \] The series of coefficients converges for every $\varepsilon>0$. The remaining low-frequency multiplier is uniformly bounded by standard smooth multiplier estimates. Summing gives (eq:main). At $p=2$, use the unitary estimate directly, avoiding a needless loss.

The source also gives a finer logarithmic Sobolev summation. Put $\delta_p=1/2-1/p$ and $B_*(p)=\beta(p)+\delta_p$. A bound with the exact power $J^{s_+(p)}$ and additional weights $[\log(2+J)]^B[\log(2+\log(2+J))]^C$ follows when $B>B_*(p)$, or when $B=B_*(p)$ and $C>\delta_p$ [transfer]. The reason is that a spatial Littlewood--Paley square sum costs an $\ell^2$ norm across scales; Holder from $\ell^p$ requires the remaining weight in $\ell^r$, $1/r=\delta_p$. At the boundary one obtains the convergence test $\sum j^{-1}(\log j)^{-Cr}<\infty$. These summation conditions are not claimed to be optimal for every $p$.

The focusing test and what the endpoint still does not say

The source supplies a radial test that explains both the critical exponent and the unavoidable logarithm [sharpness]. Fix $t_0=3/2$ and take \[ \widehat f_N(\xi)=e^{\mathrm it_0|\xi|}\psi(|\xi|/N), \] where $\psi$ is a nonzero nonnegative smooth bump in a compact subinterval of $(1,2)$. The data are an incoming spherical wave chosen to focus at $t_0$. Plancherel gives $\|f_N\|_2\asymp N^{n/2}$, while stationary phase on the sphere gives $\|f_N\|_\infty\lesssim N^{(n+1)/2}$. Interpolation yields \[ \|f_N\|_p\lesssim N^{(n+1)/2-1/p}. \]

Write $T=t_0+u$. On an outgoing shell $|r-u|\lesssim N^{-1}$ with $A/N\le u\le1/4$, stationary phase gives wave magnitude comparable from below to $N^{(n+1)/2}u^{-d/2}$, after choosing the shell so the principal oscillatory term dominates its opposite phase and remainder. The shell volume is comparable to $N^{-1}u^d$. Therefore \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-66-6f-63-75-73-69-6e-74-65-67-72-61-6c} \frac{\|U(T)f_N\|_{L^p_{T,X}}^p}{\|f_N\|_p^p} \gtrsim \int_{A/N}^{1/4}u^{d-dp/2}\,\mathrm d u. \end{equation} At $p=p_{\mathrm c}$ the integrand is $u^{-1}$, giving a lower logarithmic loss $(\log N)^{1/p_{\mathrm c}}$. Above $p_{\mathrm c}$, the lower power is $N^{d/2-n/p}$. Below $p_{\mathrm c}$, this radial test gives a constant lower bound.

Thus the critical unrestricted annular logarithmic exponent lies between the reported lower value $1/p_{\mathrm c}$ and upper value $2/p_{\mathrm c}$. The source has further weak-norm and angular-regularity refinements; they are not needed for the Math-273 implication, and no stronger endpoint conclusion is inferred here. In particular, taking $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ in (eq:main) is not legitimate without uniform control of $C_{n,p,\varepsilon}$.

From a focusing shell to spherical extension

Let \[ Eg(X)=\int_{S^{n-1}}e^{\mathrm iX\cdot\omega}g(\omega)\,\mathrm d\sigma(\omega) \] be the spherical extension operator. The source derives a local critical extension estimate by using a modulated time average of annular waves [kakeya-proof]. This is a useful bridge: spherical data emerge from a wave whose incoming radial phase is chosen to focus.

Take an operator of the form \[ M_N=\int\psi(T)e^{\mathrm iNT}U(T)\chi(|D|/N)\,\mathrm d T. \] The annular local-smoothing estimate bounds its $L^{p_{\mathrm c}}$ norm by a power of $\log N$. Its kernel on a fixed radial shell has leading stationary-phase term $N^{d/2}|X|^{-d/2}e^{\mathrm iN|X|}$ times a smooth amplitude. Apply this to incoming data $f_N(r\omega)=e^{-\mathrm iNr}\psi(r)g(\omega)$ and evaluate near the focus at scale $X/N$. The oscillating radial factors cancel, and the rescaled output tends to a nonzero multiple of $Eg(-X)$.

On a ball of radius $R$, the source controls the error by a polynomial in $R$ divided by $N$ and chooses $N$ as a sufficiently large polynomial of $R$. The scaling factors cancel at the critical exponent because $n/p_{\mathrm c}=d/2$. This produces \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6c-6f-63-61-6c-65-78-74-65-6e-73-69-6f-6e} \|Eg\|_{L^{p_{\mathrm c}}(B_R)}\lesssim [\log(2+R)]^{A}\|g\|_{L^{p_{\mathrm c}}(S^{n-1})},\qquad A=2/p_{\mathrm c}. \end{equation} Modulating $g$ translates the spatial ball, so the bound is uniform in its center. That uniformity is important in the later tube argument.

The source then applies an epsilon-removal theorem to obtain global diagonal extension for every exponent strictly larger than $p_{\mathrm c}$; this is an external analytic implication explicitly used in the source, not a new elementary consequence proved solely by summing balls [global-extension]. Interpolating the diagonal bounds with $L^1(S^{n-1})\to L^\infty(\mathbb R^n)$ gives the mixed strict range \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-72-65-73-74-72-69-63-74-69-6f-6e-72-61-6e-67-65} \|Eg\|_{L^b(\mathbb R^n)}\lesssim\|g\|_{L^a(S^{n-1})},\qquad b>\frac{2n}{n-1},\quad b>\frac{n+1}{n-1}a', \end{equation} for $1<a\le\infty$ and finite $b$.

For clarity, the exponent calculation when $a<b$ can be made explicit. Choose a diagonal exponent $s=1+b/a'$. Interpolation with weight $\theta=s/b$ gives $1/b=\theta/s$ and $1/a=1-\theta+\theta/s$. The two strict inequalities ensure that a valid diagonal exponent can be used, with a small adjustment if needed. If $a\ge b$, the finite surface measure gives the necessary embedding. The source also obtains the corresponding truncated-cone bounds by slicing in its radial parameter.

The inspected Math-96 entry includes equality on one of its exponent boundaries. The released statement (eq:restrictionrange) has strict inequalities. Accordingly this exposition does not mark the entire Math-96 formulation resolved from that statement alone. Taking a boundary limit of constants which may diverge is not a proof of the boundary estimate [restriction-catalogue].

Tube overlap, the Kakeya maximal operator, and Hausdorff dimension

Random signs extract tube overlap from extension

Consider unit tubes $T_j$ of radius comparable to $\delta$ with $\delta$-separated directions and nonnegative weights $a_j$. Let $q=p_{\mathrm c}/2=n/(n-1)$. The source obtains \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-74-75-62-65-73} \left\|\sum_j a_j\mathbf 1_{T_j}\right\|_q \lesssim[\log(2/\delta)]^{2A} \left(\delta^{n-1}\sum_j a_j^q\right)^{1/q}. \end{equation} To understand this implication, place disjoint small spherical caps around the tube directions. Modulate each cap by a phase determined by its tube center. On the appropriate rescaled tube, the cap extension has magnitude bounded below by a constant times $\delta^{n-1}$.

Apply (eq:localextension) to a random-sign sum of these modulated caps, with coefficient $\sqrt{a_j}$. Averaging over the signs retains a square function; its $p_{\mathrm c}/2$ power dominates the weighted tube-overlap expression. The spatial dilation is $\delta^{-2}$, and the critical scaling again cancels: $dp_{\mathrm c}-2n=0$. Grouping tube centers into fixed-size spatial cells allows the uniform local extension estimate to be applied without an unbounded-radius constant. The logarithmic power doubles because the tube overlap is obtained from a square of the extension. This gives (eq:tubes) [kakeya-proof].

Duality gives the maximal bound

Define the Kakeya maximal operator by taking the largest average of $|f|$ over unit $\delta$-tubes in each direction. Discretize the sphere into $\delta$-separated directions, apply duality to (eq:tubes), and then pass to the spherical measure formulation. Since $q'=n$, the result is \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6b-6d-61-78} \|K_\delta f\|_{L^n(S^{n-1})} \lesssim [\log(2/\delta)]^{2A}\|f\|_{L^n(\mathbb R^n)}. \end{equation} In particular, every fixed positive power loss $\delta^{-\varepsilon}$ is allowed. The source's formal scope states this latter epsilon-loss maximal theorem. One should not automatically identify every sharper logarithmic estimate in the manuscript with the exact statement checked by the published comparator.

Applying (eq:kmax) to an appropriate neighborhood of a Kakeya set gives a volume lower bound and full Minkowski dimension. But full Minkowski dimension does not by itself prove full Hausdorff dimension. The proof needs a genuinely multiscale covering argument.

The multiscale step cannot be replaced by a neighborhood bound

Let a set contain a unit segment in every direction, and cover it by small balls. Partition the balls according to their radii, for example $e^{-2^{k+1}}<r_j\le e^{-2^k}$. Let $G_k$ be the union of their doubled balls, $\delta_k=e^{-2^{k+1}}$, and $v_k$ the sum of their $n$-th powers of radii. The total length of a covered segment forces a pointwise directional inequality of the form \[ 1\lesssim\sum_k K_{\delta_k}\mathbf 1_{G_k}. \] After integrating over directions and using (eq:kmax), \[ 1\lesssim\sum_k 2^{2Ak}v_k^{1/n}. \] Holder's inequality converts this to a lower bound for the cost of the cover in the gauge \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-67-61-75-67-65} h_\gamma(r)=r^n[\log(1/r)]^{2An} [\log\log(1/r)]^\gamma, \qquad \gamma>n-1. \end{equation} The second logarithmic weight is selected so that the complementary tail $\sum_k k^{-\gamma/(n-1)}$ converges. As the maximum covering radius tends to zero, its tail tends to zero, forcing the gauge cost to diverge. Since for every $s<n$ the gauge in (eq:gauge) is eventually smaller than $r^s$, this implies Hausdorff dimension at least $s$ for all $s<n$, hence exactly $n$. The argument uses covers, so it does not assume measurability of the original set.

This supplies the full-dimensional Hausdorff conclusion relevant to Math-95 for every $n\ge4$, as well as the lower dimensions included in the source. It says full dimension, not positive Lebesgue measure [kakeya-proof,kakeya-catalogue].

Bochner--Riesz means: the shell argument

For the multiplier \[ B_r^\alpha=(1-|D|^2/r^2)_+^\alpha, \] the source derives uniform $L^p$ bounds for $\alpha>\max\{0,n|1/p-1/2|-1/2\}$, and a maximal bound for finite $p\ge2$ when $\alpha>s_+(p)$ [consequences]. The mechanism is worth explaining because it also prepares the square-function argument.

Rescale the local-smoothing theorem to unit-frequency input and a long time interval. For $\sigma>s_+(p)$, \[ \|U(t)g\|_{L^p_{x,t}(R\le|t|\le2R)} \lesssim R^{1/p+\sigma}\|g\|_p, \qquad R\ge1. \] A smooth frequency shell of relative width $\delta$ around radius $r$ has an integral representation \[ \psi((1-|D|/r)/\delta)f =\frac{\delta r}{2\pi}\int e^{\mathrm itr} \widehat\psi(\delta rt)U(t)g\,\mathrm d t, \] where $g$ is an appropriate annular cutoff of the input. On one octave $1\le r\le2$, take the supremum over $r$ and split the $t$ integral into dyadic intervals. Holder in time gives a contribution bounded by \[ C_M\delta(1+\delta R)^{-M}R^{1+\sigma}\|g\|_p. \] Summing over $R$ costs $\delta^{-\sigma}$. Scale the argument over radius octaves, and use Littlewood--Paley with $p\ge2$ to combine the inputs.

Near the sphere, the Bochner--Riesz multiplier is a sum of shell multipliers weighted by $\delta^\alpha$. Choosing $s_+(p)<\sigma<\alpha$ makes $\sum\delta^{\alpha-\sigma}$ converge. The part away from the sphere is a smooth multiplier with integrable kernel. This proves the maximal estimate. For each fixed radius, self-adjointness and duality extend the uniform estimate to $1<p<2$. At $p=1,\infty$, the kernel is integrable for $\alpha>(n-1)/2$, giving those endpoint bounds directly.

The distinction between the two types of uniformity matters: $\sup_r\|B_r^\alpha f\|_p$ is not the same expression as $\|\sup_r|B_r^\alpha f|\|_p$. The maximal argument proves the stronger second expression only in its specified range. Norm convergence follows by density; almost-everywhere convergence follows in the maximal range by approximation and the maximal inequality.

Stein's square function: keeping the radius integral inside

The square function is \[ \mathcal G_\alpha f(x)= \left(\int_0^\infty|r\partial_rB_r^\alpha f(x)|^2\frac{\,\mathrm d r}{r}\right)^{1/2}. \] The source proves its $L^p$ bound for $2\le p<\infty$ and \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-73-71-75-61-72-65-74-68-72-65-73-68-6f-6c-64} \alpha>\frac12+s_+(p) =\max\left\{\frac12,n\left(\frac12-\frac1p\right)\right\} \end{equation} [stein]. A supremum estimate alone does not establish this square integral, since the radius measure has infinitely many scales.

On a single interval $1\le r\le2$, differentiating the multiplier near the sphere costs $\delta^{\alpha-1}$. A shell of thickness $\delta$ yields $\delta^{1/2}$ from the $L^2$ integral in radius. The wave estimate costs $\delta^{-\sigma}$, where $\sigma>s_+(p)$. The product is $\delta^{\alpha-1/2-\sigma}$, which is summable precisely in the strict range (eq:squarethreshold).

More explicitly, Plancherel in the radius variable transforms the shell square norm into \[ C\delta\, \|\widehat\varphi(\delta t)U(t)g(x)\|_{L^2_t}. \] On a time interval of length comparable with $R$, Holder gives $R^{1/2-1/p}$, and the rescaled wave estimate gives $R^{1/p+\sigma}$. Summing dyadic $R$ thus yields a one-octave norm $O(\delta^{1/2-\sigma})$. At $p=2$ the corresponding bound is $O(\delta^{1/2})$ directly by spatial Plancherel.

To combine all radius octaves inside the square norm, the source proves a Hilbert-valued dilate lemma. On a test cube, very coarse scales are nearly constant by a derivative estimate, and very fine scales are controlled by the $L^2$ estimate plus the decaying kernel tail. Only $O(1+\log(1/\delta))$ middle scales need the $L^p$ estimate. Holder over those scales introduces the factor $[1+\log(1/\delta)]^{1/2-1/p}$. A sharp-function inequality and the spatial Littlewood--Paley estimate complete the vector-valued bound.

Consequently the full shell cost is bounded by \[ \delta^{\alpha-1} \left(\delta^{1/2}+\delta+ [1+\log(1/\delta)]^{1/2-1/p}\delta^{1/2-\sigma}\right). \] Its dyadic sum converges because the inequality for $\alpha$ is strict. The smooth part away from the sphere vanishes quadratically at zero and is summed separately by ordinary multiplier bounds. At $p=2$ the exact calculation is \[ \|\mathcal G_\alpha f\|_2^2 =2\alpha^2\int_0^1u(1-u)^{2\alpha-2}\,\mathrm d u\,\|f\|_2^2 =\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha-1}\|f\|_2^2, \] valid for $\alpha>1/2$. This explicitly identifies the low-end square-function threshold.
