The residual time-scale barrier.
Let $H$ be self-adjoint and let a differentiable approximate state $\Psi_a(t)$ lie in its domain, with residual $R(t)=(i\partial_t-H)\Psi_a(t)$. If the exact state has the same initial value, differentiation of $e^{itH}\Psi_a(t)$ and integration give \[ \|\Psi(t)-\Psi_a(t)\|\le\int_0^{|t|}\|R(s)\|\,ds \] with the corresponding time orientation for negative $t$. Thus a bound $\|R\|\le C\alpha^{-p}$ only yields $C|t|\alpha^{-p}$. It tends to zero on $|t|\le\alpha^{p-\delta}$, $\delta>0$, but yields no vanishing control at $\alpha^p$ or later.
A cancellation that would be needed.
If in the interaction representation a residual has form $e^{i\omega t}B(t)$, with $B$ norm differentiable, integration by parts gives \[ \left\|\int_0^t e^{i\omega s}B(s)ds\right\| \le\frac{2\sup\|B\|+\int_0^t\|B'(s)\|ds}{|\omega|},\qquad\omega\ne0. \] This shows precisely how oscillatory cancellation can beat direct residual integration, and why a small or resonant frequency defeats that route.
