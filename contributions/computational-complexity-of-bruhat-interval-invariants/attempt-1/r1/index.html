Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$. Specialized notation and index ranges follow the cited source and the definitions below.

A Coxeter system is a group generated by involutions with pairwise product orders specified by its Coxeter matrix. $\ell(w)$ is the shortest generator-word length. The Bruhat interval $[u,v]$ consists of elements between $u$ and $v$ in Bruhat order.

Attempt and results

An exact algorithm once the labelled acyclic graph is supplied.
Let a finite directed acyclic graph have source $u$, target $v\ne u$, and ordered edge labels. For a path with labels $t_1,\ldots,t_k$, assign the noncommutative word $w_1\cdots w_{k-1}$, where $w_i=a$ if $t_i<t_{i+1}$ and $w_i=b$ otherwise, following a fixed declared descent convention. Start at $u$ with one empty path. For each first edge, create state (endpoint, last label, empty word) with count one. Process vertices in a topological order. Extending a state across an outgoing edge of label $t$ appends $a$ or $b$ according to comparison with the stored last label, and adds its count into the new state. Sum states at $v$ over their final labels. Induction on path length proves correctness: each nonempty path has a unique last edge and shorter prefix, and the extension records exactly its last comparison. Thus every path contributes once and no extraneous path occurs. It is an exact finite procedure for the supplied graph, with states potentially exponential in the longest path length. It is not a polynomial algorithm for constructing the graph from compressed Coxeter input.
A proved finite conversion from an $ab$ expansion.
Put $c=a+b$ and $d=ab+ba$. Among $ab$ words of each fixed ordinary degree, use lexicographic order with $a>b$. Expanding any $cd$ word, its leading $ab$ word is obtained by replacing $c$ with $a$ and $d$ with $ab$, and its coefficient is one. These leading words are distinct: parse a resulting word from the right, pairing every $b$ with the immediately preceding $a$ as a $d$, and reading any remaining $a$ as a $c$. This is a unique inverse parse for words built from the tokens $a,ab$. Thus the expansions of $cd$ words of a fixed weighted degree are linearly independent. If an $ab$ polynomial is known to lie in their span, recover its coefficients by leading-term subtraction: its largest remaining word must be the largest leading word among the still-present $cd$ terms, since all other terms of each expansion are smaller. Subtract its integer coefficient times that expansion and continue. The process terminates in the finite word list. This proves uniqueness and a finite reconstruction algorithm, conditional on membership in the span. In the Bruhat application that membership is the source's theorem, not a theorem re-established in this attempt.
Dense output alone can be exponential.
Let $D$ be a requested maximum weighted degree, with $\deg c=1$, $\deg d=2$. There are at least $2^{\lfloor D/2\rfloor}$ different $cd$ words of degree $D$: independently choose the block $cc$ or $d$ in each of $\lfloor D/2\rfloor$ positions and append $c$ if $D$ is odd. A format explicitly listing the coefficient of every such word, even zeros, therefore needs exponentially many entries. This does not bound the size of a sparse representation, one selected coefficient, or the Kazhdan--Lusztig polynomial. Consequently a polynomial-time statement must distinguish those outputs rather than infer identical costs from a mathematical conversion. For a simple path-count check, take the Boolean interval on three commuting generators labelled $1<2<3$. Its directed paths are the six permutations. Their comparison words are $aa$, $bb$, twice $ab$, and twice $ba$, so the path polynomial is $a^2+2ab+2ba+b^2=c^2+d$ by direct expansion. This is an illustrative small interval, not a general complexity estimate.
