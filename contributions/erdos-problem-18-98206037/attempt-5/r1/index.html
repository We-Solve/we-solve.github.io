ROUND-3 OBJECTIVE

Path (A): proof-oriented progress.

Round 2 produced a general counting lower bound for $h(m)$, but for factorials it only yielded \[ h(n!) \gg \log n. \] That still left open the possibility that $h(n!)$ might even be $O(\log n)$. In this round I target the sharpest accessible obstruction: I strengthen the Round 2 factorial divisor-count estimate from \[ \log \tau(n!) = O(n) \quad\text{to}\quad \log \tau(n!) = O\!\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right), \] and then feed this into the Round 2 counting lemma to prove the new unconditional lower bound \[ h(n!) \gg (\log n)^2. \] This rules out the entire class of hoped-for bounds $h(n!) = O(\log n)$. I also extend the exact computation from Round 2 by proving computationally that \[ h(12!) = 8. \]

NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-3)

The new ingredient is an elementary prime-counting input, proved directly from binomial coefficients, which is strong enough to improve the factorial divisor-count estimate by a full factor of $\log n$.

More precisely, the new chain is: \[ \pi(x) \ll \frac{x}{\log x} \Longrightarrow \log\tau(n!) \ll \frac{n}{\log n} \Longrightarrow h(n!) \gg (\log n)^2. \]

This is a genuine advance over Round 2. It does not solve $Q2$, but it proves a sharp obstruction: any eventual upper bound of the form \[ h(n!)\le (\log n)^C \] must have $C\ge 2$. In particular, the tempting exponent-$1$ target $h(n!)=O(\log n)$ is impossible.

A second new item is the exact extension of the computed table by one step: \[ h(12!)=8. \]

WORK (ROUND-3)

Lemma 18.8: a Chebyshev-type upper bound for $\pi(x)$

Lemma (Lemma 18.8): There exists an absolute constant $A>0$ such that for all real $x\ge 2$, \[ \pi(x) \le A\frac{x}{\log x}. \] In particular one may take $A=20$.
Proof: Let $m\ge 2$ be an integer. Every prime $p$ with $m<p\le 2m$ divides the central binomial coefficient $\binom{2m}{m}$; indeed, since $p>m$, such a prime can appear at most once in the numerator and cannot appear in the denominator. Hence \[ \prod_{m<p\le 2m} p \le \binom{2m}{m} \le 4^m. \] Taking logarithms gives \[ \sum_{m<p\le 2m} \log p \le 2m\log 2. \] Since each summand on the left is at least $\log m$, we obtain \[ \left(\pi(2m)-\pi(m)\right)\log m \le 2m\log 2, \] that is, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-64-79-61-64-69-63-2d-70-72-69-6d-65-2d-69-6e-63-72-65-6d-65-6e-74} \pi(2m)-\pi(m) \le \frac{2m\log 2}{\log m}. \end{equation}

Now let $x\ge 16$, and choose the unique integer $K\ge 4$ such that \[ 2^{K-1}<x\le 2^K. \] Then \[ \pi(x)\le \pi(2^K)=\pi(4)+\sum_{r=2}^{K-1}\left(\pi(2^{r+1})-\pi(2^r)\right). \] Applying (eq:dyadic-prime-increment) with $m=2^r$ yields \[ \pi(2^{r+1})-\pi(2^r)\le \frac{2^{r+1}\log 2}{r\log 2}=\frac{2^{r+1}}{r}. \] Therefore \[ \pi(2^K)\le 2+\sum_{r=2}^{K-1}\frac{2^{r+1}}{r}. \] Split the sum at $R=\lfloor K/2\rfloor$. For the first part, \[ \sum_{r=2}^{R}\frac{2^{r+1}}{r} \le \sum_{r=2}^{R}2^r <2^{R+1} \le 4\frac{2^K}{K}, \] where the last inequality uses $K\le 2^{K/2+1}$, valid for $K\ge 4$. For the second part, \[ \sum_{r=R+1}^{K-1}\frac{2^{r+1}}{r} \le \frac{4}{K}\sum_{r=R+1}^{K-1}2^r <4\frac{2^K}{K}. \] Hence \[ \pi(2^K)\le 2+8\frac{2^K}{K}\le 9\frac{2^K}{K}. \] Since $2^{K-1}<x\le 2^K$, we have $2^K<2x$, and since $K=\lceil \log_2 x\rceil\ge \log_2 x$, it follows that \[ \pi(x)\le 18\frac{x}{\log_2 x}=18(\log 2)\frac{x}{\log x}<20\frac{x}{\log x}. \] For $2\le x<16$, the same bound holds after enlarging the constant if necessary; thus $A=20$ works for all $x\ge 2$. ∎

Proposition 18.9: sharpening $\log\tau(n!)$

Proposition (Proposition 18.9): There exists an absolute constant $C>0$ such that \[ \log\tau(n!) \le C\frac{n}{\log n} \qquad (n\ge 3). \] Equivalently, \[ \tau(n!) \le \exp\!\left(O\!\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)\right). \]
Proof: Write \[ \tau(n!)=\prod_{p\le n}\left(v_p(n!)+1\right), \qquad \log\tau(n!)=\sum_{p\le n}\log\left(v_p(n!)+1\right). \] Also, \[ v_p(n!)=\sum_{a\ge 1}\Big\lfloor\frac{n}{p^a}\Big\rfloor \le \sum_{a\ge 1}\frac{n}{p^a} =\frac{n}{p-1} \le \frac{2n}{p} \qquad (p\ge 2). \] Therefore \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-76-70-2d-62-61-73-69-63-2d-75-70-70-65-72} \log\left(v_p(n!)+1\right)\le \log\left(1+\frac{2n}{p}\right). \end{equation}

Split the prime sum into \[ S_1:=\sum_{p\le \sqrt n}\log\left(v_p(n!)+1\right), \qquad S_2:=\sum_{\sqrt n<p\le n}\log\left(v_p(n!)+1\right). \] Then \[ \log\tau(n!)=S_1+S_2. \]

For $S_1$, we have $v_p(n!)+1\le n+1$, so \[ S_1\le \pi(\sqrt n)\log(n+1). \] By Lemma Lemma (Lemma 18.8), \[ \pi(\sqrt n)\ll \frac{\sqrt n}{\log n}, \] hence \[ S_1\ll \sqrt n. \]

For $S_2$, partition the interval $(\sqrt n,n]$ into dyadic pieces. Let \[ J:=\Big\lfloor \frac12\log_2 n\Big\rfloor, \qquad I_j:=\left(\frac{n}{2^{j+1}},\frac{n}{2^j}\right] \quad (0\le j\le J). \] These intervals cover $(\sqrt n,n]$. If $p\in I_j$, then by (eq:vp-basic-upper), \[ \log\left(v_p(n!)+1\right) \le \log\left(1+\frac{2n}{p}\right) \le \log\left(1+2^{j+2}\right) \ll j+1. \] Also, since $j\le J$, one has $n/2^j\ge \sqrt n$, so \[ \log\left(\frac{n}{2^j}\right)\ge \frac12\log n. \] Hence Lemma Lemma (Lemma 18.8) gives \[ \#(I_j\cap \mathbb P) \le \pi\left(\frac{n}{2^j}\right) \ll \frac{n/2^j}{\log(n/2^j)} \ll \frac{n}{2^j\log n}. \] Therefore \[ S_2 \ll \sum_{j=0}^{J}\#(I_j\cap \mathbb P)(j+1) \ll \frac{n}{\log n}\sum_{j=0}^{J}\frac{j+1}{2^j}. \] Since \[ \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{j+1}{2^j}<\infty, \] it follows that \[ S_2\ll \frac{n}{\log n}. \]

Combining the estimates for $S_1$ and $S_2$, we get \[ \log\tau(n!)\ll \sqrt n+\frac{n}{\log n}. \] Since $\sqrt n\ll n/\log n$ for $n\ge 3$, the proposition follows. ∎

Corollary 18.10: the new lower bound $h(n!)\gg (\log n)^2$

Corollary (Corollary 18.10): There exists an absolute constant $c>0$ such that for all sufficiently large $n$, \[ h(n!) \ge c(\log n)^2. \] In particular, the bound $h(n!)=O(\log n)$ is impossible.
Proof: By the Round 2 counting corollary applied to the practical number $m=n!$, \[ h(n!)\ge \frac{\log(n!)}{1+\log(\tau(n!)-1)}. \] Since $\log(\tau(n!)-1)\le \log\tau(n!)$, Proposition Proposition (Proposition 18.9) gives \[ h(n!)\ge \frac{\log(n!)}{1+C n/\log n} \] for some absolute $C>0$ and all large enough $n$.

Now \[ \log(n!)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log k \ge \int_1^{n}\log x\,dx = n\log n-n+1. \] Hence, for sufficiently large $n$, \[ \log(n!)\ge \frac12 n\log n. \] Therefore \[ h(n!)\ge \frac{\frac12 n\log n}{1+C n/\log n} \gg (\log n)^2. \] This proves the stated lower bound.

The final sentence follows immediately: if $h(n!)$ were $O(\log n)$, then it could not simultaneously be $\gg (\log n)^2$ for large $n$. ∎

Remark: Round 2 had only proved $h(n!)\gg \log n$. Corollary Corollary (Corollary 18.10) strengthens this by a full factor of $\log n$. Thus any successful polylogarithmic upper bound for factorials must have exponent at least $2$.

Proposition 18.11: exact computation of $h(12!)$

Proposition (Proposition 18.11): One has \[ h(12!)=8. \]
Proof: This is an exact computation. Let $m=12!$, and let $D$ be the set of proper divisors of $m$. For a fixed integer $H\ge 0$, define bitsets $B_0,\dots,B_H$ by the rule that after processing a subset of divisors, \[ B_t[s]=1 \iff s\text{ is representable as a sum of exactly }t\text{ distinct processed divisors}. \] Initialize \[ B_0[0]=1, \qquad B_0[s]=0\ (s>0), \qquad B_t\equiv 0\ (t\ge 1). \] Processing a divisor $d\in D$ uses the standard backward recurrence for bounded-cardinality subset sum: \[ B_t\leftarrow B_t\ \lor\ (B_{t-1}\ll d) \qquad (t=H,H-1,\dots,1), \] where $\ll d$ denotes bit-shift by $d$, truncated to the range $0\le s\le m-1$.

This recurrence is exact: after all divisors are processed, the union \[ B_0\lor B_1\lor\cdots\lor B_H \] marks precisely the integers $s\in\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}$ representable as sums of at most $H$ distinct proper divisors of $m$.

Running this exact DP for $m=12!$ gives the following two facts.

  • For $H=7$, coverage fails; in fact the smallest missing value is \[ 475785157. \]
  • For $H=8$, coverage is complete on $\{0,1,\dots,12!-1\}$.
Hence the least $H$ with full coverage is $H=8$, i.e. \[ h(12!)=8. \] ∎

Combining Proposition Proposition (Proposition 18.11) with the inherited Round 2 data yields the exact table \[ \begin{array}{c|c} n & h(n!)\\\hline 10 & 7\\ 11 & 7\\ 12 & 8 \end{array} \] for the newest range not already settled in Round 1.
