Erdős Problem 846

Statement and result.
Suppose an infinite set $A\subset\mathbb R^2$ has the property that every finite $P\subseteq A$ contains at least $\epsilon|P|$ points with no three collinear, for some fixed $\epsilon>0$. Must $A$ be a finite union of sets with no three collinear? No. We reconstruct the explicit Putterman--Sawhney--Valiant counterexample, which works with $\epsilon=1/2$.
The points.
Choose a sequence $t_1,t_2,\ldots$ of algebraically independent real numbers, and for $i<j$ put \[ P_{ij}=(t_i+t_j,\ t_i^2+t_it_j+t_j^2),\qquad A=\{P_{ij}:i<j\}. \] Such a sequence exists by induction: the field generated by finitely many chosen reals over $\mathbb Q$ is countable, and so is its algebraic closure within $\mathbb R$. Choose the next real outside that countable set. Distinct unordered index pairs give distinct points, since equality of their first coordinates would be a nonzero rational linear relation among the $t_i$.
Exactly which triples are collinear.
We claim that three distinct points of $A$ are collinear exactly when their three index pairs form a triangle.

For pairs $ij,ik,jk$, write $a=t_i,b=t_j,c=t_k$. All three points satisfy \[ y=(a+b+c)x-(ab+ac+bc), \] as direct substitution shows. Thus triangle triples are collinear.

For the converse, three distinct edges of a simple graph have one of five forms: a triangle, a three-edge star, a three-edge path, a two-edge path together with a disjoint edge, or three disjoint edges. Collinearity is the vanishing of the polynomial determinant \[ D=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&x_1&y_1\\1&x_2&y_2\\1&x_3&y_3\end{pmatrix}. \] For the four nontriangle forms this is a nonzero polynomial. Here is an explicit certificate: substitute $(t_1,\ldots,t_6)=(0,1,3,7,11,13)$, using only the coordinates needed in each row. In the stated edge order the determinants are \[ \begin{array}{c|r} \text{index pairs}&D\\ \hline 12,13,14&48\\ 12,23,34&126\\ 12,13,45&356\\ 12,34,56&2094 \end{array} \] These evaluations prove that the four determinant polynomials are not identically zero. Algebraic independence then prevents them from vanishing at the chosen $t_i$. Relabelling indices handles every triple of each type, proving the claim.

Every finite subset has a large general-position subset.
A finite $P\subseteq A$ corresponds to a finite graph whose edges are its index pairs. Place each vertex of this graph independently in one of two classes with equal probability. Each edge crosses the cut with probability $1/2$, so some cut retains at least $|P|/2$ edges. The retained graph is bipartite and has no triangle. By the classification above, the corresponding points have no collinear triple. This proves the required hereditary density property.
No finite partition works.
Suppose $A$ were partitioned into $r$ sets containing no collinear triple. Colour each edge $ij$ of the countably infinite complete graph by the part containing $P_{ij}$. The classification says there is no monochromatic triangle.

For completeness, the needed finite multicolour Ramsey fact follows by a short induction. Let $R_1=3$ and \[ R_r=r(R_{r-1}-1)+2. \] In an $r$-edge-colouring of $K_{R_r}$, some vertex has at least $R_{r-1}$ neighbours joined to it in one colour. An edge of that colour among those neighbours gives a triangle with the vertex; if no such edge exists, their complete graph uses at most $r-1$ colours and the induction hypothesis applies. Thus every finite $r$ admits a finite triangle Ramsey bound. Restricting our infinite colouring to its first $R_r$ vertices is a contradiction.
