The three-terminal question on a tree

The source distinguishes edge lengths from deletion costs and asks about hitting every shortest path for three terminal pairs forming an undirected triangle. We first restrict the underlying graph to a tree, with unit edge lengths and nonnegative deletion costs. For three distinct terminals $a,b,c$, their pairwise paths meet at a unique median vertex $v$; the three terminal-to-$v$ arms are edge-disjoint. Let $m_a,m_b,m_c$ be the minimum edge costs on these arms, with value $+\infty$ for an empty arm.
Proposition: The minimum cost of increasing all three pairwise distances on the tree is the sum of the two smallest arm minima.
Proof: The union of the three pairwise paths is either a subdivided claw or a path with one terminal between the other two, giving the stated median and arms. Each pairwise path consists of the corresponding two arms. Deleting an edge outside these arms affects none of the three paths. Because paths in a tree are unique, a pair's distance increases exactly when its path is cut; the new distance is then infinite. All three pairwise paths are cut if and only if at least two of the three arms are cut. Necessity follows because if at most one arm is cut, the path joining the other two terminals survives; sufficiency follows because every pair of arms includes one of the two cut arms. Any cut arm costs at least its cheapest edge, and deleting one cheapest edge from each of the two cheapest nonempty arms attains their sum. At most one arm is empty because the terminals are distinct, so two finite minima exist. ∎
This gives a linear-time tree algorithm after finding the three paths. A three-arm star with costs $1,2,3$ has optimum 3. However the route cannot be extended simply by replacing shortest-path interdiction with terminal separation on arbitrary graphs.
Proposition: In the unit-length, unit-cost complete graph on terminals $a,b,c$ and a fourth vertex $v$, increasing all three terminal distances costs 3, whereas separating all three terminals into different components costs 5.
Proof: Each terminal pair initially has distance one and its direct edge is its unique length-one path. All three terminal-to-terminal edges must therefore be deleted, and these three deletions suffice: the remaining star through $v$ has all terminal distances two. For complete separation those three edges still must go, and at most one of the three edges from $v$ to a terminal may remain, since two would connect two terminals. Thus at least five edges must be deleted, and keeping exactly one such edge attains five. ∎

Verification and remaining gaps

All subsets of the star's three edges and of the complete graph's six edges were enumerated. The cost distinction shows the precise failure of transporting the tree multiway-cut description to graphs with alternate longer paths. Neither a polynomial algorithm nor an NP-hardness reduction for the report's general undirected three-pair case is obtained. The source already records easier and harder nearby cases; these toy cases do not change the original complexity status.
