Topographic Rossby Waves and Inertial-Wave Coexistence

Definitions and mathematical statement

$\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$; the standard number fields have their usual meanings. All additional conventions are stated locally.
Definitions and conventions.
A rotating fluid supports inertial waves due to Coriolis restoring forces. A sloping boundary or varying depth gives an effective topographic potential-vorticity gradient and supports topographic Rossby waves. The report compares these waves in a rectangular rotating basin with a sloping boundary and examines an asymptotic series whose leading term is a topographic Rossby wave.

A two-branch singular-perturbation test.
Let $\omega>0$ represent an inertial frequency and let $\epsilon$ measure coupling to a slow branch. The real symmetric matrix \[ L_{\epsilon,\omega}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\epsilon\\\epsilon&\omega\end{pmatrix} \] has eigenvalues $\lambda_\pm=(\omega\pm\sqrt{\omega^2+4\epsilon^2})/2$. For $|\epsilon|<\omega/2$, the slow eigenvalue expands as $\lambda_-=-\epsilon^2/\omega+\epsilon^4/\omega^3+\cdots$. The Taylor series has radius $\omega/2$, since the square root has branch points at $\epsilon=\pm i\omega/2$.
Coexistence does not guarantee a uniform series.
For each real $\epsilon$ both exact eigenvalues exist and are distinct unless $\epsilon=\omega=0$. If a sequence of inertial modes has frequencies $\omega_n\downarrow0$, the radii of convergence shrink to zero. Thus no fixed nonzero coupling admits this expansion uniformly in $n$, even though every exact two-mode problem has two coexisting branches. At $\omega=0$ the ordered eigenvalues are $\pm|\epsilon|$, and the expansion from fixed positive $\omega$ plainly fails.
Limit of the analogy.
This does not identify $L_{\epsilon,\omega}$ with the rectangular sloping-basin operator or prove local divergence of its Rossby series. It demonstrates that the source's two alternatives need not be mutually exclusive at the level of perturbation reasoning: exact modes can coexist while a mode-uniform expansion fails. The first missing step is a spectral reduction of the actual boundary-value problem, including its small divisors and spatial convergence norm. Without that reduction the observed basin paradox remains unresolved.
