Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

For a vector $z$, $\lVert z\rVert_1=\sum_i|z_i|$, $\lVert z\rVert_\infty=\max_i|z_i|$, and sparsity is the number of nonzero coordinates. Proximity measures the distance between a continuous optimum and an integral optimum.

A relaxation contains the integral feasible solutions. For a nonnegative minimization objective, its integrality gap is the supremum of the ratio between the integral optimum and the relaxation optimum over the specified instances.

A linear program optimizes $c^Tx$ over a polyhedron $\{x:Ax\leq b\}$. In the simplex method a pivot moves between adjacent basic feasible solutions; a pivot rule chooses the next such move.

An integer program imposes integral coordinates on a system of constraints. A mixed-integer program allows both integral and real coordinates; $\operatorname{conv}(S)$ denotes the set of finite convex combinations of feasible points of $S$.

Research attempt: A support-collision argument and a necessary coefficient dependence

Let $A\in\mathbb Z^{m\times n}$, $m\ge1$, have entries of absolute value at most $\Delta$, and suppose minimizing $c^Tx$ over $Ax=b$, $x\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^n$ has an attained optimum. Choose an optimum of minimum support size $s$. Then \[ 2^s\le(2s\Delta+1)^m. \] For $s=0$ this is immediate. Otherwise consider all $2^s$ subset sums of the columns on its support. Every coordinate of a subset sum is an integer between $-s\Delta$ and $s\Delta$. If the displayed inequality failed, two distinct subsets would have the same sum. Subtract their indicator vectors to obtain nonzero $y\in\{-1,0,1\}^n$ supported on the positive coordinates of $x$, with $Ay=0$. Since these coordinates of $x$ are at least one, both $x+y$ and $x-y$ are nonnegative integer feasible. Optimality forces $c^Ty=0$: either nonzero sign would improve one of those two points.

Replace $y$ by $-y$ if needed so it has a positive coordinate. Put $t=\min\{x_i:y_i=1\}$, a positive integer. Then $x-ty$ stays nonnegative: coordinates with $y_i=1$ decrease by at most their original values, those with $y_i=-1$ increase, and other coordinates do not change. At least one positive support coordinate becomes zero, none outside the support becomes positive, feasibility is preserved by $Ay=0$, and the objective is unchanged by $c^Ty=0$. This contradicts support minimality and proves the inequality. It is a standard pigeonhole method, not an optimal sparsity estimate.

A coefficient-independent proximity bound cannot hold even for one equation and two variables. For any integer $a\ge2$, minimize $y$ subject to \[ ax+y=a-1,\qquad x,y\ge0. \] The LP optimum is uniquely $x_*=(a-1)/a$, $y_*=0$. For integer feasible solutions, $0\le x\le(a-1)/a<1$ forces $x=0$, so the unique integer solution is $(0,a-1)$. The distances are $a-1$ in $\ell_\infty$ and $(a-1)+(a-1)/a$ in $\ell_1$, both unbounded with fixed numbers of variables and equations. The relevant matrix coefficient grows with $a$; this example therefore challenges only bounds omitting such matrix information, not bounds parameterized by determinants or coefficients.

The support argument does not produce proximity to a prescribed LP optimum: its long feasible move may be far from that LP point. Conversely the proximity example has a one-sparse integer optimum. Thus neither elementary observation merges the two source questions into one solved bound.
