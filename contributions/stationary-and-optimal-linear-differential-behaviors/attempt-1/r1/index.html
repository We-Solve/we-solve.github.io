Stationary and Optimal Linear Differential Behaviors

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

A linear time-invariant differential behavior is a space of trajectories satisfying constant-coefficient linear differential equations. For a polynomial matrix $R$, $\ker R(d/dt)$ denotes trajectories annihilated by its associated differential operator. $\succeq0$ means positive semidefinite.

A state-space system evolves as $\dot x=f(x,u)$ with output $y=h(x,u)$. Model reduction seeks a lower-dimensional system preserving specified input-output properties. Observability distinguishes initial states from their output trajectories, while controllability concerns reachable states under allowed inputs.

Statement recorded in the supplied source.
Characterize the linear time-invariant differential behaviors $\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^w$ for which there exists a symmetric two-variable polynomial matrix $\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{w\times w}[\zeta,\eta]$ satisfying $$\mathcal{B}=\ker\Phi\!\left(-\frac{d}{dt},\frac{d}{dt}\right).$$ These are the stationary behaviors. Characterize also the optimal ones, for which one can additionally require $\Phi(-i\omega,i\omega)\succeq0$ for every $\omega\in\mathbb{R}$. [S2]

The trajectory regularity and interpretation of the two-variable operator are the report's conventions.

Testing a positive normal-equation construction

Given an arbitrary behavior $\ker R(D)$, where $D=d/dt$, a tempting route is to use the formally self-adjoint normal operator $R(-D)^T R(D)$. Its frequency symbol is positive semidefinite. The gap is that equality of kernels on square-integrable vectors does not automatically give equality on the source's entire trajectory space $C^\infty(\mathbb R)$.
Proposition: The scalar behavior $\mathcal B=\{Ce^t:C\in\mathbb R\}$ is not stationary in the source sense. The normal-equation construction applied to $R(z)=z-1$ produces the strictly larger behavior $\operatorname{span}\{e^t,e^{-t}\}$ despite having a nonnegative frequency symbol.
Proof: For a symmetric scalar two-variable polynomial, $\Phi(\zeta,\eta)=\Phi(\eta,\zeta)$. Thus $p(z)=\Phi(-z,z)$ satisfies $p(-z)=\Phi(z,-z)=p(z)$: it is even. If $e^t$ belongs to its differential kernel, then $p(D)e^t=p(1)e^t=0$, so $p(1)=0$. Evenness gives $p(-1)=0$ and hence $p(D)e^{-t}=0$. But $e^{-t}$ is not a constant multiple of $e^t$ on $\mathbb R$. Consequently no such polynomial has kernel exactly $\mathcal B$, including the possibility $p=0$, whose kernel is all smooth trajectories. For the proposed construction choose $\Phi(\zeta,\eta)=(\zeta-1)(\eta-1)$. It is symmetric and gives $p(z)=1-z^2$. The solutions of $w''=w$ are exactly $Ae^t+Be^{-t}$: putting $v=w'-w$ gives $v'=-v$, and solving this first-order equation and then $w'-w=v$ yields the displayed form. At $z=i\omega$, $p(i\omega)=1+\omega^2>0$. This proves all claims and the strict enlargement. ∎
The failed step would have been to integrate a squared residual over the whole real line and infer that a zero normal equation forces zero residual. The source does not require these exponential trajectories to be square integrable, so that inference has no applicable finite-energy hypothesis. Compactly supported variations defining stationarity do not impose square integrability on the trajectory itself.
