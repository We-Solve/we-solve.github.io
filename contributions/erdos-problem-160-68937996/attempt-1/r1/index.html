Erdős Problem 160: three colours on every four-term progression

Statement and outcome

Let $h(N)$ be the fewest colours needed on $[1,N]$ so that every nonconstant four-term arithmetic progression uses at least three colours. For $N\ge2$ we prove \[ h(N)\ge\frac{M}{r_3(M)},\quad M=\lfloor N/2\rfloor, \qquad h(N)\le\left\lceil\sqrt{7e(16N+1)}\right\rceil, \tag{1} \] where $r_3(M)$ is the largest size of a three-term-progression-free subset of $[1,M]$. These bounds do not determine the growth of $h$.

A necessary three-term condition

If $a,a+d,a+2d\le M$ were all one colour, then $a+3d\le3M/2\le N$. Appending this point gives a four-term progression with at most two colours, a contradiction. Each restricted colour class therefore has size at most $r_3(M)$, proving $1$'s lower bound.

More generally, a monochromatic triple $a,a+d,a+2d$ is impossible whenever it extends at either end within $[1,N]$. The only possible exceptions satisfy both $a\le d$ and $a+3d>N$. Thus one must not assert that entire colour classes on $[1,N]$ are three-term-free.

The explicitly external Bloom--Sisask theorem gives $r_3(M)\le CM/(\log M)^{1+c}$ for absolute $c,C>0$, and hence $h(N)\gg(\log N)^{1+c}$. Their Theorem 1.1 is not proved here. The problem page records stronger subsequent bounds; these are partial arguments, not record claims.

A local-lemma construction

Colour the integers independently and uniformly with $k$ colours. For each four-term progression $Q$, let $B_Q$ say that at most two colours occur. Then \[ \mathbb P(B_Q)=\frac{k+14\binom k2}{k^4} =\frac{7k-6}{k^3}\le\frac7{k^2}. \] There are $k$ constant assignments and $2^4-2=14$ assignments using a specified pair of colours. A fixed integer lies in at most $4N$ progressions, by specifying its position and positive step. Each bad event consequently shares variables with fewer than $16N$ others. It is independent of the entire collection of events on disjoint variables.

Here is the local lemma needed for completeness. If event probabilities are at most $p$, dependency degree is at most $D$, and $ep(D+1)\le1$, all events can be avoided. Set $x=1/(D+1)$, so $x(1-x)^D\ge1/(e(D+1))$. Induction on the size of a conditioning set proves $\mathbb P(B_i\mid\text{specified other events fail})\le x$. Split that set into neighbours and non-neighbours of $i$. Independence bounds the numerator by $p$ after conditioning on non-neighbours, and the induction hypothesis bounds the conditional probability of failure of the at most $D$ neighbours from below by $(1-x)^D$. This proves the induction step; multiplying the positive conditional failure probabilities proves the conclusion. The case $D=0$ is immediate.

Use $D=16N$ and the value of $k$ in $1$. This proves $h(N)=O(\sqrt N)$. The trivial upper bound $N$ can be substituted when smaller.

Assessment and attribution

The old local gpt_pro_5.2/160.tex observes that any union of two colours is four-term-free. Its repeated $1,2,3$ witness through $12$ is invalid: positions $1,4,7,10$ all have colour $1$. Sisask, Breaking the logarithmic barrier in Roth's theorem on arithmetic progressions, Theorem 1.1, https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03528. No novelty claim is made.
