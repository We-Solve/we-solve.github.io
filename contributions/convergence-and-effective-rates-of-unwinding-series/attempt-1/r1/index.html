Unwinding series: a Hardy-space projection proof and the remaining boundary-rate question

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

For a holomorphic function $F$ on the unit disk, write $F=B_0G_0$, extracting a Blaschke factor $B_0$ for its zeros. Iteratively factor $G_j-G_j(0)=B_{j+1}G_{j+1}$. The resulting unwinding expansion is formally $F=\sum_{j\geq0}G_j(0)B_0\cdots B_j$, with remainder obtained from the unfixed final $G_j$. Blaschke factors are inner factors associated with zeros in the disk.

Boundary pointwise or uniform convergence is not inferred from an $H^2$ calculation.

Finite-step identities and orthogonality

Let $F$ be holomorphic in a neighbourhood of the closed unit disk. If it is identically zero the expansion terminates immediately. Otherwise extract all its finitely many zeros in the open disk, including multiplicities, into a finite Blaschke product $B_0$ and put $F=B_0G_0$. At each nonterminal step set $c_j=G_j(0)$ and factor \[ G_j-c_j=B_{j+1}G_{j+1}, \] where $B_{j+1}$ extracts all zeros in the disk. In particular $B_{j+1}(0)=0$. If $G_j-c_j$ is identically zero, stop and set all later remainders zero. Unimodular factor conventions may change coefficients and factors but not these identities. All finite-step quotients are holomorphic near the closed disk: the divisions at the extracted zeros are removable.

Use $H^2=\{\sum_{k\ge0}a_kz^k:\sum_k|a_k|^2<\infty\}$ with the coefficient inner product. Multiplication by a finite Blaschke product is an isometry. For polynomials this follows from the boundary integral norm and $|B(e^{it})|=1$; for all $H^2$ functions use polynomial density and extend the isometry by completion. The extension agrees with analytic multiplication, since point evaluation is bounded by $|h(z)|\le\|h\|_{H^2}/\sqrt{1-|z|^2}$, directly from Cauchy--Schwarz applied to the power series.

Write $\psi_j=B_0\cdots B_j$. Iterating the recursion gives \[ F=\sum_{j=0}^N c_j\psi_j+R_N, \qquad R_N=\psi_{N+1}G_{N+1}. \] For $j\le N$ and arbitrary $h\in H^2$, cancellation of the common inner factor in the inner product gives \[ \langle\psi_j,\psi_{N+1}h\rangle =\langle1,(B_{j+1}\cdots B_{N+1})h\rangle=0, \] because the analytic function on the right vanishes at zero. Complex-conjugate inner-product conventions yield the same zero. Thus the partial sum is orthogonal to the closed subspace $K_N=\psi_{N+1}H^2$. This subspace is closed because it is the range of an isometry from a complete space. Since $R_N\in K_N$, the identity proves that $R_N$ is exactly the orthogonal projection of $F$ onto $K_N$, without an unproved completeness claim about the functions $\psi_j$.

A quantitative comparison with Taylor tails

Each $B_j$, $j\ge1$, has a zero at zero, so $K_N\subseteq z^{N+1}H^2$. If $F(z)=\sum_{k\ge0}a_kz^k$ and $T_NF=\sum_{k=0}^Na_kz^k$, every element of $K_N$ is orthogonal to $T_NF$. Consequently \[ \|R_N\|_{H^2}^2=\langle F,R_N\rangle =\langle F-T_NF,R_N\rangle \le\|F-T_NF\|_{H^2}\|R_N\|_{H^2}, \] where taking absolute values gives the same inequality under either inner-product convention. Dividing when the remainder is nonzero gives the explicit estimate \[ \|R_N\|_{H^2}\le \left(\sum_{k>N}|a_k|^2\right)^{1/2}. \] The right side tends to zero. This proves $H^2$ convergence for the stated analytic-neighbourhood class, and in any more general $H^2$ setting where the displayed inner factorizations and recursions exist. Such existence is not proved here for arbitrary boundary-singular functions.

Two effective rates follow. If $\sum_{k\ge0}(1+k)^{2s}|a_k|^2\le C_s^2$ for $s>0$, then $\|R_N\|_{H^2}\le C_s(N+2)^{-s}$ by bounding each tail weight below by $(N+2)^{2s}$. If $F$ is holomorphic on a neighbourhood of $|z|\le R$ with $R>1$ and $\sup_{|z|=R}|F(z)|\le M$, Cauchy's coefficient formula gives $|a_k|\le MR^{-k}$. Summing the geometric series yields \[ \|R_N\|_{H^2}\le \frac{M R^{-(N+1)}}{\sqrt{1-R^{-2}}}. \] Both the radius and the bound $M$ are needed for this effective estimate; their existence alone is not an algorithm to compute them from arbitrary data.

There is also a compact-interior estimate independent of such extra regularity. Since $R_N=z^{N+1}h_N$ with $\|h_N\|_{H^2}=\|R_N\|_{H^2}\le\|F\|_{H^2}$, for $|z|\le r<1$, \[ |R_N(z)|\le\frac{r^{N+1}}{\sqrt{1-r^2}}\|F\|_{H^2}. \] This proves uniform convergence on every smaller closed disk.

Why this route does not prove a boundary-uniform rate

The implication from small $H^2$ norm to small supremum on the unit circle is false. For any integer $L\ge1$, the polynomial \[ h_L(z)=L^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}z^j \] has $H^2$ norm $L^{-1/2}$ but $h_L(1)=1$. Multiplying by arbitrarily high powers of $z$ preserves both facts and can enforce any desired order of vanishing at zero. These polynomials are not claimed to be actual unwinding remainders for one fixed function; they show that the norm and zero-order estimates used above cannot alone imply the boundary supremum conclusion. Additional control of the remainders, their factors, or a stronger norm would be needed.
