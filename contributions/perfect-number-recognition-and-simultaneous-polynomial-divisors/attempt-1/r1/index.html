Perfect-number recognition and polynomial divisors: exact easy cases and the enumeration gap

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

Polynomial time means a bound polynomial in the finite input length; for an integer input, its binary length is proportional to its logarithm. A positive integer $N$ is perfect if $\sum_{d\mid N,\,d<N}d=N$, equivalently $\sigma(N)=2N$. The second question asks for integer arguments at which two given monic integer polynomials divide prescribed positive integers. Coprimality is over $\mathbb Q[X]$. Both recognition and bounded enumeration targets are retained.

Both source tasks retained

The source combines deterministic perfect-number recognition in binary with simultaneous divisibility by two monic coprime polynomials. Its window $H$ is not quantified, and it explicitly rejects a tiny-window brute-force answer as uninteresting. We prove what the even case and an elementary divisibility route actually deliver, then retain both remaining gaps.
Complete even-perfect characterization.
Write an even integer $N=2^a b$ with $a\ge1$ and $b$ odd. Divisors of a product of coprime positive integers decompose uniquely into products of their respective divisors; summing gives multiplicativity of $\sigma$. Thus $N$ is perfect exactly when \[ (2^{a+1}-1)\sigma(b)=2^{a+1}b. \] Put $M=2^{a+1}-1$. Since $\gcd(M,2^{a+1})=1$, the equation forces $M\mid b$, say $b=Mc$, and then $\sigma(b)=(M+1)c=b+c$. If $c>1$, the three distinct numbers $1,c,b$ are divisors of $b$ ($M\ge3$), so $\sigma(b)\ge1+c+b>b+c$, a contradiction. Hence $c=1$, $b=M$, and $\sigma(b)=b+1$ implies that $b$ is prime: a composite $b>1$ has an additional proper divisor strictly between one and $b$. Conversely if $b=M$ is prime, the same multiplicativity calculation gives $\sigma(N)=2N$. This proves the characterization without assuming the unknown classification of odd perfect numbers.

Accordingly an even input can be recognized by extracting its power of two, comparing its odd part with $2^{a+1}-1$, and testing that odd part for primality. All arithmetic has bit length $O(\log N)$. The source lecture supplies deterministic polynomial-time primality testing; using that established algorithm as a subroutine gives an even-input procedure. The AKS theorem is a cited external algorithm, not a theorem newly proved in this manuscript. An odd input does not satisfy this reduction. A supplied complete prime factorization would allow calculation of $\sigma(N)=\prod_{p^e\parallel N}(1+p+\cdots+p^e)$, but finding such a factorization is an additional computational task.

A simultaneous-divisor obstruction from Bezout.
For coprime $f,g\in\mathbb Q[X]$, the polynomial Euclidean algorithm produces $A_0f+B_0g=1$ with rational polynomial coefficients: repeated division strictly reduces degrees, ends at a nonzero constant gcd, and back-substitution expresses that gcd as a combination. Clearing coefficient denominators gives integer polynomials $A,B$ and a nonzero integer $R$ with $Af+Bg=R$. Therefore every integer $x$ with $f(x)\mid m$ and $g(x)\mid n$ satisfies \[ \gcd(|f(x)|,|g(x)|)\mid\gcd(m,n,|R|). \] The evaluations cannot be zero because $m,n$ are positive and zero does not divide a nonzero integer. Evaluation of the Bezout identity proves the displayed assertion. It screens common factors of the two values, but does not determine the individual values or the argument.
An intrinsic finite window is not a fast enumeration.
Let $f(X)=X^d+\sum_{j<d}a_jX^j$ with $d\ge1$ and $C=\sum_{j<d}|a_j|$. For $|x|\ge\max(1,2C)$, \[ |f(x)|\ge |x|^d-C|x|^{d-1}\ge |x|^d/2. \] Thus $0<|f(x)|\le m$ forces either $|x|<\max(1,2C)$ or $|x|\le(2m)^{1/d}$. This is a valid full-solution envelope, and an analogous one follows from $g$ if it has positive degree. But for $f=X$ it can have length proportional to $m$, exponential in its binary input length. Scanning that envelope is not a polynomial-time algorithm. The direction $f(x)\mid m$ also cannot be reversed to $m\mid f(x)$: with $f=X$, $m=6$, the argument $x=2$ satisfies the first and fails the second. If a polynomial is the allowed monic constant $1$, it supplies no restriction; if both are $1$, every integer in whatever window is specified must be output. This makes the window and output encoding material and prevents treating an arbitrary tiny $H$ as a solution.
