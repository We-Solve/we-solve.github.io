A Jacobi-Maupertuis and McGehee scale-shape dictionary

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
$\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$; the standard number fields have their usual meanings. All additional conventions are stated locally.
Definitions and notation.
In the Newtonian $n$-body problem, positions $q_i\in\mathbb R^d$ and masses $m_i>0$ obey $m_i\ddot q_i=\nabla_{q_i}U$, where $U(q)=\sum_{i<j}m_im_j/|q_i-q_j|$ and the gravitational constant is normalized to one. Here $T=\tfrac12\sum_i m_i|\dot q_i|^2$, $E=T-U$, and a collision has $q_i=q_j$. The center of mass can be fixed at zero; moment of inertia is $I=\sum_i m_i|q_i|^2$. At fixed energy $E$, the Jacobi--Maupertuis metric on the Hill region is conformal to the mass metric by the factor $2(E+U)$. Its geodesics, with appropriate reparametrization away from degeneracies, describe mechanical trajectories. McGehee coordinates rescale position and time to analyze collision or asymptotic boundaries. The two frameworks encode different geometric features.

An explicit scale-shape Jacobi metric.
In center-of-mass mass coordinates write $q=rs$, with $r=\sqrt I>0$ and $\sum m_i|s_i|^2=1$. Since $s$ is tangent-orthogonal to its derivative, the mass metric is $dr^2+r^2g_{\mathrm{shape}}$, before rotation reduction or on its horizontal zero-angular-momentum part. Homogeneity gives $U(q)=U(s)/r$. At $E=0$, the Jacobi--Maupertuis metric therefore becomes \[g_{JM}=2U(s)\left(\frac{dr^2}{r}+r g_{\mathrm{shape}}\right). \] Set $\rho=2\sqrt r$. Direct substitution yields $g_{JM}=2U(s)[d\rho^2+(\rho^2/4)g_{\mathrm{shape}}]$. The shape-dependent factor on the radial term is retained; this is not automatically an ordinary metric cone with constant radial coefficient.
Collision distance and mechanical time.
Along a fixed noncollision shape the radial distance to total collision is finite, equal to $2\sqrt{2U(s)r_0}$ from radius $r_0$. In a zero-energy homothetic mechanical motion, $\tfrac12\dot r^2=U(s)/r$, so collision time is $\int_0^{r_0}\sqrt{r/(2U(s))}\,dr$, also finite. A coordinate blow-up that resolves a dynamical collision boundary is therefore not the same operation as completing this finite-distance metric.
