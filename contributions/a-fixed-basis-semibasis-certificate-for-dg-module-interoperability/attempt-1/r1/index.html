DG-module certificates: basis filtrations and information lost by forgetting the action

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

A differential graded (DG) algebra is a graded algebra with a differential d satisfying $d^2=0$ and the graded Leibniz rule. DG modules and ideals respect both multiplication and differential. Semifree resolutions build modules in stages from free graded generators; higher Massey operations record additional homological structure.

The action is additional data.
Use homological grading, so differentials have degree $-1$. For a DG algebra $(A,d_A)$, a left DG module must satisfy \[ d_M(am)=d_A(a)m+(-1)^{|a|}a d_M(m) \] for homogeneous $a$. Its underlying complex over the ground field does not determine the action. Take $A=k[t]$ in degree zero with zero differential and $M=k$ concentrated in degree zero. Let $t$ act by zero on $M_0$ and by one on $M_1$. The complexes are identical and both satisfy the displayed rule. An $A$-linear map $f:M_0\to M_1$ satisfies $0=f(t\cdot1)=t\cdot f(1)=f(1)$, so there is no nonzero such map, in particular no isomorphism. A bridge retaining only the complex therefore cannot recover the original DG-module isomorphism type.
A finite specified-basis semifree test.
Let $M=\bigoplus_{j=1}^s A e_j$ be graded free with a specified homogeneous basis and a valid DG differential. Expand it uniquely as $d_M(e_j)=\sum_i a_{ij}e_i$. Form a directed edge $j\to i$ exactly when $a_{ij}\ne0$. A basis-compatible semifree filtration means a filtration formed by subsets of this basis, in which each generator's differential is in the span of strictly earlier stages. Such a filtration exists exactly when this finite graph has no directed cycle. One direction is immediate: the stage index decreases strictly along every edge, which cannot occur around a cycle. For the converse, a finite acyclic graph has a vertex with no outgoing edge, since otherwise repeatedly following edges would revisit a vertex. Remove such vertices in successive rounds; induction exhausts all vertices and gives the required stage order. The Leibniz rule shows each earlier span is a DG submodule, since its basis generator differentials stay inside it. The quotient at any new stage is a direct sum of shifted copies of $A$ with the differential inherited from $A$, exactly as required for semifreeness. More precisely, assign a sink level zero and $\ell(j)=1+\max_{j\to i}\ell(i)$ otherwise. Induction in the removal order shows this is the length of a longest path starting at $j$. Every admissible stage assignment, starting at zero, must be at least this large along that path. Consequently $1+\max_j\ell(j)$ is the minimum number of nonempty stages for this fixed basis when $s>0$; the zero module needs zero stages. This assertion does not optimize over changes of basis.
Why checking only $d^2=0$ is insufficient.
Take $A=k[\epsilon]/(\epsilon^2)$ with $|\epsilon|=-1$ and $d_A=0$. Let $M=Ae$, $|e|=0$, and define $d_M(e)=\epsilon e$, extending by the Leibniz rule. This is well defined and $d_M^2(e)=d_M(\epsilon e) =-\epsilon^2e=0$; on $a e$ the same computation gives zero. But the dependency graph has a self-loop, and no filtration by subsets of this basis is semifree. This exhibits a valid graded-free DG module that fails the specified-basis certificate. It is not a claim that a detected cycle in an arbitrary larger presentation rules out every possible semibasis. An actual verifier must check the Leibniz rule and $d_M^2=0$ as well as the graph; acyclicity alone says nothing about incorrectly supplied coefficients.
