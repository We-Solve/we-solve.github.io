Quitting Games and Uniform Equilibria: A Consolidated Mathematical Audit of Truncation, Cyclic Equilibria, and Finite Certificates

Abstract

We investigate the open question whether every quitting game with finitely many players admits an ordinary uniform ${\varepsilon}$-equilibrium for every ${\varepsilon}>0$. We collect and carefully distinguish three kinds of conclusions. First, the stopping-time representation yields an exact identity for the only discontinuity created when quitting times escape to infinity, and an error bound for Nash equilibria of finite terminal truncations. Second, in an explicit classical three-player cyclic quitting game, we prove that the infinite game has a subgame-perfect exact equilibrium although every exact Nash equilibrium of every finite terminal truncation has unrestricted deviation gain strictly exceeding $1/4$; consequently, a tempting universal truncation criterion fails. Third, a quantile-rounding and date-compression argument gives a quantitative finite-grid approximation to the infimum of unrestricted regret, with error at most $(2n-1)D/k$. In particular, a game without arbitrarily accurate undiscounted equilibria would admit a rigorous finite nonexistence certificate. We supply explicit conversions to long-run-average and discounted equilibrium inequalities and illustrate the certificates by exact rational grid calculations. These statements do not decide whether every finite-player quitting game has uniform ${\varepsilon}$-equilibria.

The problem, literature, and scope

A quitting game is a stochastic game with one nonabsorbing state. At each stage all players simultaneously choose to continue or to quit. The first stage at which somebody quits determines the absorbing payoff; if everybody always continues, a specified continuation payoff applies. Despite this spare description, the existence of ordinary approximate equilibria for an arbitrary number of players has proved difficult.

The classical treatments of Solan and Vieille [SolanVieille2001] and Flesch, Thuijsman, and Vrieze [Flesch1997] exhibit some of the underlying phenomena. Two- and three-player quitting games admit the relevant approximate equilibrium payoffs, while the unrestricted existence question for four or more players is identified as open in the 2026 treatment of Ashkenazi-Golan, Krasikov, Rainer, and Solan [AKRS2026]. Solan and Solan [SolanSolan2020] prove sunspot ${\varepsilon}$-equilibrium existence for every finite-player quitting game, but public randomization is additional structure, not an ordinary equilibrium of the game as given. The same paper obtains a positive ordinary uniform-equilibrium conclusion under a non-$Q$-matrix condition; other positive classes follow from absorption-path arguments [AKRS2024,AKRS2026].

There is a distinction between exact $0$-equilibria and approximate equilibria for every ${\varepsilon}>0$. Exact $0$-equilibria need not exist [Flesch1996,AKRS2026]. Throughout, our open question concerns existence for each positive tolerance, with strategies permitted to depend on that tolerance. We do not assert that exact uniform equilibria always exist.

Our analysis of this question developed in stages. An initial approach tried to take exact equilibria of finite terminal games and send the terminal date to infinity. The approach admits a clean error lemma, but Section sec:counterexample disproves its universal convergence requirement. A replacement takes arbitrary finite-support strategies, optimizes their regret in the infinite game, and proves a quantitative approximation theorem (Section sec:finite-grid). The final implication is conditional and gives no positive or negative resolution of the open question.

Model, evaluations, and strategic regret

Let $I=\{1,\ldots,n\}$ with $n\ge2$. For each subset $S\subseteq I$ let $r^S\in{\mathbb{R}}^n$. If exactly the players in a nonempty set $S$ quit at the first terminating stage, payoffs equal $r^S$ thereafter. Write $c=r^\varnothing$ for the payoff vector if nobody ever quits. We often use the convenient normalization \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6e-6f-72-6d-61-6c-69-7a-61-74-69-6f-6e} r_i^{\{i\}}=0,\qquad c_i<0,\qquad i\in I, \end{equation} which is the convention in the motivating problem. Except where explicitly indicated, the quantitative finite-grid theorem does not require (eq:normalization).

At stage $t\in{\mathbb{N}_0}$, each player independently chooses $C_i$ or $Q_i$; let $\theta\in{\mathbb{N}_0}\cup\{\infty\}$ be the first stage with at least one quitter, with $\theta=\infty$ if there is none. A behavior strategy for player $i$ is the sequence $x_i=(x_i^t)_{t\ge0}$, where $x_i^t$ is the conditional probability of quitting at $t$, provided the game has not ended. The undiscounted (terminal) payoff is \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-67-61-6d-6d-61} \gamma_i(x)={\mathbb{E}}_x\!\left[ {\mathbf 1}_{\{\theta<\infty\}}r_i^{S_\theta} +{\mathbf 1}_{\{\theta=\infty\}}c_i\right]. \end{equation} A profile is an undiscounted ${\varepsilon}$-Nash equilibrium if for every player $i$ and every behavior deviation $\widetilde x_i$, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-75-6e-64-69-73-63-2d-65-71} \gamma_i(\widetilde x_i,x_{-i})-\gamma_i(x)\le{\varepsilon}. \end{equation} The equilibrium is ${\varepsilon}$-subgame-perfect if the analogous inequality holds in the continuation game starting at every stage $t$.

For stochastic-game uniformity, give the nonabsorbing state the stage payoff $c$, and from the first quitting stage onward give the constant absorbing payoff $r^{S_\theta}$. If $g_i^t$ is the resulting stage-payoff process, define \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-65-76-61-6c-75-61-74-69-6f-6e-73} \gamma_i^T(x)={\mathbb{E}}_x\left[\frac1T\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g_i^t\right],\qquad \gamma_i^\lambda(x)={\mathbb{E}}_x\left[\sum_{t\ge0}\lambda(1-\lambda)^t g_i^t\right]. \end{equation} A uniform ${\varepsilon}$-equilibrium is one profile $x$ for which, for all sufficiently large $T$ and all sufficiently small $\lambda>0$, no unilateral behavior deviation improves either payoff in (eq:evaluations) by more than ${\varepsilon}$.

We use the payoff coordinate range \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-44} D(r):=\max_{i\in I}\left(\max_{S\subseteq I}r_i^S-\min_{S\subseteq I}r_i^S\right). \end{equation} All payoff comparisons below are bounded by $D(r)$, regardless of the absolute size of the payoffs.

Stopping-time normal form and the tail discontinuity

Set $K={\mathbb{N}_0}\cup\{\infty\}$ with the order topology of the one-point compactification of ${\mathbb{N}_0}$. A player's behavior strategy is equivalent to a distribution $\mu_i$ of a planned quitting time $T_i\in K$: \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-68-61-7a-61-72-64-73} \mu_i(t)=x_i^t\prod_{s<t}(1-x_i^s),\qquad \mu_i(\infty)=\prod_{s\ge0}(1-x_i^s). \end{equation} Conversely, as long as the denominator is positive, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-72-65-76-65-72-73-65-2d-68-61-7a-61-72-64} x_i^t=\frac{\mu_i(t)}{\mu_i(\{t,t+1,\ldots\}\cup\{\infty\})}; \end{equation} subsequent hazards may be assigned arbitrarily when the denominator is zero. Planned times $T_1,\ldots,T_n$ are mutually independent. Their minimum identifies the absorption time, and the set of minimizers identifies the quitting coalition. This construction is equivalent to behavior strategies, including for unilateral deviations.

Let $U_i(t;\mu_{-i})$ be the expected payoff to player $i$ from the pure planned quitting time $t\in K$, with opponents following $\mu_{-i}$. Expected payoff is affine in a player's own distribution, so \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-72-65-67-72-65-74} {\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\mu):=\max_{i\in I}\left\{ \sup_{t\in K}U_i(t;\mu_{-i})-\gamma_i(\mu)\right\} \end{equation} is precisely the maximum possible gain from any unilateral behavior deviation. In particular, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-67} g(r):=\inf_{\mu\in\prod_i\Delta(K)}{\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\mu) \end{equation} vanishes exactly when the game has undiscounted ${\varepsilon}$-equilibria for all ${\varepsilon}>0$.

Proposition (Exact tail discontinuity): For arbitrary independent opponents' quitting times, let $q_j=\mu_j(\infty)$. Under (eq:normalization), \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-74-61-69-6c} \lim_{t\to\infty}\left(U_i(t;\mu_{-i})-U_i(\infty;\mu_{-i})\right) =(-c_i)\prod_{j\ne i}q_j. \end{equation} Without the normalization, the right-hand side is $(r_i^{\{i\}}-c_i)\prod_{j\ne i}q_j$.
Proof: Fix a realization of the opponents' planned times. If at least one opponent quits at a finite time, sufficiently late finite $t$ and $t=\infty$ give the same payoff. If every opponent chooses $\infty$, a finite $t$ gives the sole-quitter payoff $r_i^{\{i\}}$, whereas $\infty$ gives $c_i$. Take expectations and apply bounded convergence; the probability of the latter event is $\prod_{j\ne i}q_j$. ∎

The payoff kernel is continuous at pure time profiles with at least one finite coordinate, but may be discontinuous at $(\infty,\ldots,\infty)$. Formula (eq:tail) identifies the lost payoff when finite quitting dates move arbitrarily far into the future. Ordinary weak compactness of quitting-time distributions consequently does not preserve best-response inequalities automatically.

The exact-terminal-truncation attempt

For $h\ge1$, let $H_h$ be the finite normal-form timing game in which all players choose a planned time from \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-4b-68} K_h:=\{0,1,\ldots,h-1,\infty\}, \end{equation} using the original payoff rule (in particular, the all-$\infty$ outcome pays $c$). Such a game has a mixed Nash equilibrium by the finite-game existence theorem. Every such mixed profile can also be embedded as a behavior profile of the infinite quitting game.
Lemma (Boundary estimate for an exact truncation equilibrium): Assume (eq:normalization). If $\mu\in{\operatorname{NE}}(H_h)$ and $q_i=\mu_i(\infty)$, then in the original infinite quitting game \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-65-74-61} {\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\mu)\le\eta(\mu),\qquad \eta(\mu):=\max_i(-c_i)\prod_{j\ne i}q_j. \end{equation} If $\infty$ is in the support of player $i$'s equilibrium distribution, then the maximal deviation gain for that player is exactly $(-c_i)\prod_{j\ne i}q_j$.
Proof: Every pure deviation from $K_h$ is available in $H_h$ and hence cannot improve player $i$'s payoff. For any finite $t\ge h$, since all opponents' finite planned dates are less than $h$, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-74-61-69-6c-66-69-6e-69-74-65} U_i(t;\mu_{-i})-U_i(\infty;\mu_{-i}) =(-c_i)\prod_{j\ne i}q_j. \end{equation} Since $\infty$ is an available deviation in $H_h$, the equilibrium payoff is at least $U_i(\infty;\mu_{-i})$, which proves the upper bound. When $\infty$ has positive equilibrium probability for player $i$, that action is a best response in $H_h$ and has payoff exactly equal to her equilibrium payoff; the late deviation realizes the bound. ∎

The lemma suggests choosing terminal-game equilibria whose residual never-quit probabilities satisfy \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-62-61-64-63-72-69-74-65-72-69-6f-6e} \inf_{h\ge1}\min_{\mu\in{\operatorname{NE}}(H_h)} \max_i(-c_i)\prod_{j\ne i}\mu_j(\infty)=0. \end{equation} If this condition held, the lemma would yield arbitrarily accurate undiscounted equilibria. It is a sufficient criterion, but it is not necessary. Section sec:counterexample gives a complete three-player refutation of its universal validity.

An exact cyclic subgame-perfect equilibrium

Consider the infinite three-stage periodic behavior profile \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-63-79-63-6c-65} (1/2,0,0),\qquad(0,1/2,0),\qquad(0,0,1/2), \quad\text{repeated forever.} \end{equation} Here the triple at each stage lists quitting probabilities for players $1,2,3$. The associated continuation payoffs at the beginning of the three phases are \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-63-79-63-6c-65-76-61-6c-75-65-73} v^{(1)}=(0,1,0),\quad v^{(2)}=(0,0,1),\quad v^{(3)}=(1,0,0). \end{equation} Indeed, \[ v^{(1)}=\tfrac12 r^{\{1\}}+\tfrac12 v^{(2)}=(0,1,0), \] with the other two recursions following cyclically. At phase $1$, player $1$ is indifferent between quitting and continuing (each yields $0$); player $2$ earns $1$ by continuing and $-1/2$ by quitting; player $3$ earns $0$ by either action. The other phases follow by rotation of player indices.

These one-stage incentive checks are sufficient for arbitrary deviations: under any deviation by one player, the other two players independently quit with probability $1/2$ at their respective positions once per block. Their joint probability of not absorbing the game for $k$ full blocks is $4^{-k}$. Thus any player's payoff under any deviation differs negligibly, as $k\to\infty$, from one determined by the first $k$ blocks. Finite-horizon backward optimality and the one-stage checks establish all best-response inequalities. We have proved:

Proposition: The infinite game $\Gamma_*$ possesses a subgame-perfect undiscounted exact $0$-equilibrium, given by (eq:cycle).

A one-stage uniqueness calculation

We now calculate all Nash equilibria of the terminal games $H_h$ in this example. Consider a one-stage game whose payoff, if all continue, is the common number $v\in[-1,0)$ for each player. Write $x_i$ for player $i$'s probability of quitting. The advantage of quitting instead of continuing for player $1$ is \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-44-31} D_1=x_2-3x_3+x_2x_3-v(1-x_2)(1-x_3), \end{equation} with the analogous cyclic expressions for the other players.
Lemma (Unique one-stage equilibrium): For $-1\le v<0$, the one-stage game has a unique Nash equilibrium, which is symmetric and completely mixed, with \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6f-6e-65-73-74-61-67-65-70-72-6f-62} x_1=x_2=x_3=p(v):=1-\frac1{\sqrt{1-v}}. \end{equation} Its equilibrium payoff vector is $(-p(v),-p(v),-p(v))$. Furthermore, for any continuation payoff vector $v\in{\mathbb{R}}^3$ (not necessarily symmetric), no Nash equilibrium of the one-stage game has any player quit with probability $1$.
Proof: First prove the final assertion. If $x_1=1$, then player $2$'s advantage from quitting is $D_2=2x_3-3<0$, so $x_2=0$. Given $x_1=1,x_2=0$, player $3$'s quitting advantage is $1>0$, so $x_3=1$. But then player $1$'s quitting advantage is $-3<0$, a contradiction. Cyclic relabeling excludes $x_2=1$ and $x_3=1$.

Now assume $v_1=v_2=v_3=v\in[-1,0)$. If $x_1=0$, player $2$'s quitting advantage is $x_3-v(1-x_3)>0$, forcing $x_2=1$, which is impossible. By symmetry every $x_i$ belongs to $(0,1)$. Set the quitting odds $z_i=x_i/(1-x_i)>0$. Dividing the indifference equations $D_i=0$ by the positive products of the other players' continuation probabilities yields \begin{align}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6f-64-64-73} v&=z_2-3z_3-z_2z_3,\\ v&=z_3-3z_1-z_3z_1,\nonumber\\ v&=z_1-3z_2-z_1z_2.\nonumber \end{align} In particular, if some $z_1\ge1$ then the second equation gives $v=z_3(1-z_1)-3z_1\le-3$, contradicting $v\ge-1$. Thus $0<z_i<1$ for all $i$. Put \[ f_v(z)=\frac{v+3z}{1-z},\qquad f_v'(z)=\frac{3+v}{(1-z)^2}>0. \] Equations (eq:odds) assert that $z_2=f_v(z_3)$, $z_3=f_v(z_1)$, and $z_1=f_v(z_2)$. A strictly increasing real map has no nontrivial finite cycle, so $z_1=z_2=z_3$, hence $x_1=x_2=x_3=x$. Equation (eq:D1) now reduces to \[ v=-\frac{x(2-x)}{(1-x)^2}=1-\frac1{(1-x)^2}, \] which uniquely yields (eq:onestageprob). Direct evaluation of the pure-quitting payoff gives $-x$; by indifference, this is the one-stage equilibrium payoff. The fully mixed profile satisfying these equalities is indeed a Nash equilibrium. ∎

All exact terminal equilibria and a positive regret gap

Define recursively \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-70-2d-72-65-63} p_0=1,\qquad p_{k+1}=1-\frac1{\sqrt{1+p_k}},\qquad k\ge0, \end{equation} and put \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-71-72-65-63} q_h:=\prod_{k=1}^{h}(1-p_k). \end{equation}
Theorem (Failure of the universal truncation criterion): For every integer $h\ge1$, the finite terminal timing game $H_h$ associated with $\Gamma_*$ has exactly one Nash equilibrium $\mu^{(h)}$. It has all three players quit independently with probabilities $p_h,p_{h-1},\ldots,p_1$ at the successive stages and has per-player payoff $-p_h$. When interpreted in the infinite quitting game, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-71-67-61-70} {\operatorname{Reg}}_{\Gamma_*}(\mu^{(h)})=q_h^2>\frac14. \end{equation} In particular, criterion (eq:badcriterion) is false even for a game possessing an exact subgame-perfect equilibrium.
Proof: The continuation payoffs after the last available finite date are $(-1,-1,-1)$. By Lemma Lemma (Unique one-stage equilibrium), the one-stage game at the last date has the unique symmetric mixed equilibrium with quitting probability $p_1$ and continuation payoff $-p_1$.

We must justify applying backward induction to all Nash equilibria of $H_h$, rather than just subgame-perfect equilibria. At any continuation history reached with positive probability, a Nash equilibrium induces a one-stage Nash equilibrium when its actual continuation payoffs are inserted at the all-continue action profile. By the final assertion of Lemma Lemma (Unique one-stage equilibrium), no player quits with probability $1$ at such a history. Hence the next all-continue history also has positive probability. Induction from the initial stage shows that every continuation history is reached with positive probability. Consequently, Nash equilibrium behavior is optimal at every stage, and the one-stage uniqueness lemma can be applied backwards. Its recursive formula is exactly (eq:p-rec); this proves existence and uniqueness of the specified finite equilibrium.

Each player's probability of choosing $\infty$ is $q_h>0$. Therefore the terminal action $\infty$ lies in every player's equilibrium support. Lemma Lemma (Boundary estimate for an exact truncation equilibrium), including its equality case, now gives ${\operatorname{Reg}}_{\Gamma_*}(\mu^{(h)})=q_h^2$.

It remains to prove the uniform lower bound. From (eq:p-rec), \[ 1+p_{k-1}=\frac1{(1-p_k)^2},\qquad (1-p_k)(1+p_{k-1})=\frac1{1-p_k}\ge1+p_k. \] Multiplying and telescoping gives \[ q_h\ge\prod_{k=1}^h\frac{1+p_k}{1+p_{k-1}} =\frac{1+p_h}{2}>\frac12, \] because $p_h>0$. Squaring proves (eq:qgap). ∎

Remark (What was actually disproved): The theorem is not a counterexample to approximate-equilibrium existence; Proposition Proposition gives an exact equilibrium for the same game. Nor does it show failure of arbitrary finite-prefix approximations. It disproves the particular program of taking exact Nash equilibria of terminal games with an artificial all-continue terminal payoff and expecting their infinite-game regret to tend to zero.

Finite cyclic prefixes do approximate equilibria

Repeat the three-stage block (eq:cycle) exactly $k$ times, then instruct every player to continue forever. Call this profile $x^{[k]}$. One block survives with probability $1/8$ and has unconditional absorbing-payoff contribution $(0,7/8,0)$. Thus \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-70-72-65-66-69-78-2d-70-61-79-6f-66-66} \gamma(x^{[k]})= \left(-8^{-k},\,1-2\cdot8^{-k},\,-8^{-k}\right). \end{equation} For an unrestricted deviator after the finite prefix, the appropriate terminal best-response value is $\max\{r_i^{\{i\}},c_i\}=0$, not $-1$: such a deviator may quit alone after everyone else has been instructed to continue. For clarity, start the best-response backward induction at $W=(0,0,0)$ after the last prescribed block. At a stage where only player $j$ may quit, with probability $1/2$, the deviator $i=j$ chooses between $0$ and $W_j$; a deviator $i\ne j$ chooses between $\tfrac12 r_i^{\{i,j\}}$ and $\tfrac12 r_i^{\{j\}}+\tfrac12 W_i$. Applying these recursions backwards through the stages with active players $3,2,1$ gives \[ (0,0,0)\xrightarrow{j=3}(1,0,0) \xrightarrow{j=2}(0,0,1) \xrightarrow{j=1}(0,1,0). \] Starting instead from $(0,1,0)$ gives exactly the same three intermediate vectors and ends again at $(0,1,0)$; thus appending whole blocks does not change the best-response payoff vector. Consequently, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-70-72-65-66-69-78-2d-72-65-67} {\operatorname{Reg}}_{\Gamma_*}(x^{[k]})=2\cdot8^{-k}\longrightarrow0. \end{equation} This demonstrates the exact distinction: arbitrary finite-support profiles can approximate an infinite equilibrium even when exact terminal Nash equilibria cannot.

The finite-grid replacement

The obstruction motivates optimizing regret directly over arbitrary finite-support profiles, rather than first imposing the Nash equations of a truncated terminal game. The next result is a quantitative approximation of the global infimum $g(r)$.

Fix $k\ge1$ and set $H=nk$. Let $\mathcal G_{n,k}$ denote the set of all product distributions $\mu=\bigotimes_i\mu_i$ in which each marginal $\mu_i$ is the empirical distribution of $k$ (not necessarily distinct) points in \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-67-72-69-64-2d-73-70-61-63-65} K_H=\{0,1,\ldots,H-1,\infty\}. \end{equation} These are exactly the marginals whose probability masses are integer multiples of $1/k$. There are \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-67-72-69-64-63-6f-75-6e-74} |\mathcal G_{n,k}|=\binom{(n+1)k}{k}^{n} \end{equation} independent profiles. Define \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6d-6b} m_k(r)=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal G_{n,k}}{\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\mu). \end{equation} We stress that (eq:mk) uses unrestricted infinite-game regret.

Theorem (Quantitative finite-grid approximation): For every $n\ge2$, every finite-player quitting-game payoff table $r$, and every positive integer $k$, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-67-72-69-64-2d-6d-61-69-6e} 0\le m_k(r)-g(r)\le\frac{(2n-1)D(r)}k. \end{equation} Equivalently, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-67-72-69-64-2d-69-6e-74-65-72-76-61-6c} \max\!\left\{0,m_k(r)-\frac{(2n-1)D(r)}k\right\} \le g(r)\le m_k(r). \end{equation} No assumption such as (eq:normalization) is needed.
Proof: Step 1: midpoint-quantile rounding. Fix any profile $\mu=\bigotimes_i\mu_i$. For each $i$ and $\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}$, choose the leftmost $t_{i,\ell}\in K$ whose cumulative distribution function reaches $u_\ell=(2\ell-1)/(2k)$, and define \[ \nu_i=\frac1k\sum_{\ell=1}^k\delta_{t_{i,\ell}}. \] A quantile may equal $\infty$. Standard midpoint rounding gives, for every finite $t$, as well as for every left limit, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-63-64-66-2d-63-6f-6e-74-72-6f-6c} \left|F_{\nu_i}(t)-F_{\mu_i}(t)\right|\le\frac1{2k}. \end{equation} The inequality also applies to the total finite mass, by taking $t\to\infty$.

Step 2: the payoff is a three-level function of one stopping time. Fix every stopping time except that of player $j$ and inspect any coordinate $i$. If the earliest among the fixed times is a finite $a$, and $J$ is the set of fixed players quitting at $a$, then as a function of $s=T_j$ the payoff is \[ f(s)=\begin{cases} A=r_i^{\{j\}},&s<a,\\ B=r_i^{J\cup\{j\}},&s=a,\\ C=r_i^J,&s>a. \end{cases} \] Let $\Delta F=F_{\nu_j}-F_{\mu_j}$. The change in the expectation of $f$ is exactly \[ (A-B)\Delta F(a-)+(B-C)\Delta F(a). \] By (eq:D) and (eq:cdf-control), its absolute value is at most $D(r)/k$. If all fixed times equal $\infty$, there are only two payoff levels, and the same bound holds. Averaging over the fixed stopping times preserves the bound.

Step 3: all unilateral deviations are controlled. Replace the $n$ marginals successively. The bound just established gives, simultaneously for all players, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-67-61-6d-6d-61-2d-72-6f-75-6e-64} |\gamma_i(\nu)-\gamma_i(\mu)|\le\frac{nD(r)}k. \end{equation} For any fixed pure deviation $t\in K$, only the other $n-1$ marginals change, and the same estimate is uniform in $t$: \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-55-2d-72-6f-75-6e-64} \sup_{t\in K}\left|U_i(t;\nu_{-i})-U_i(t;\mu_{-i})\right| \le\frac{(n-1)D(r)}k. \end{equation} Combining (eq:gamma-round) and (eq:U-round) yields \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-72-65-67-2d-72-6f-75-6e-64} {\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\nu)\le{\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\mu)+\frac{(2n-1)D(r)}k. \end{equation}

Step 4: date compression. The union of the finite supports of the $\nu_i$ has at most $nk$ points. Let the distinct finite support dates be $s_0<\cdots<s_{L-1}$, with $L\le nk$. Replace the date $s_a$ by $a$ for every player, leaving $\infty$ unchanged, and call the product profile $\widehat\nu$. Every realized order and every tie of planned times is preserved, so \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-63-6f-6d-70-72-65-73-73-2d-67-61-6d-6d-61} \gamma_i(\widehat\nu)=\gamma_i(\nu). \end{equation} For deviations, each new finite quitting time $a<L$ has the same payoff as the original deviation $s_a$; every new finite time $t\ge L$ has the same payoff as the original deviation $s_{L-1}+1$; and the deviation $\infty$ is unchanged. If $L=0$, the same conclusion follows by comparing immediate finite quitting with never quitting. Thus compression may delete old deviation opportunities but cannot create a new payoff level, and \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-63-6f-6d-70-72-65-73-73-2d-72-65-67} {\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\widehat\nu)\le{\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\nu). \end{equation} The profile $\widehat\nu$ belongs to $\mathcal G_{n,k}$. Consequently, for arbitrary $\mu$, \[ m_k(r)\le{\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\widehat\nu) \le{\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\mu)+\frac{(2n-1)D(r)}k. \] Take the infimum over $\mu$ to obtain the upper bound in (eq:grid-main). The lower bound follows from $\mathcal G_{n,k}\subseteq\prod_i\Delta(K)$. ∎

Remark (Neither correlation nor a compactness shortcut): Each rounded marginal remains independent of the others. Date compression is the same deterministic relabeling for every player; it preserves independence. No public signal is introduced. The argument avoids a hidden appeal to continuity at the all-$\infty$ profile by bounding payoff errors before compressing dates.

Exact grid regret and finite nonexistence certificates

For a profile supported on $K_H$, the deviation payoff is constant for finite quitting times $t\ge H$. In full generality, the set of candidate deviations reduces to \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-66-69-6e-69-74-65-2d-64-65-76-69-61-74-69-6f-6e-2d-6c-69-73-74} \{0,1,\ldots,H,\infty\}. \end{equation} Under (eq:normalization), set $q_j=\mu_j(\infty)$. The exact late-deviation identity is \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6c-61-74-65-2d-67-65-6e-65-72-61-6c} U_i(H;\mu_{-i})=U_i(\infty;\mu_{-i}) -c_i\prod_{j\ne i}q_j. \end{equation} Since $-c_i>0$, the late finite deviation weakly dominates never quitting, and therefore \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-66-69-6e-69-74-65-2d-72-65-67-72-65-74-2d-66-6f-72-6d-75-6c-61} {\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\mu)=\max_i\Bigg[ \max\left\{U_i(0;\mu_{-i}),\ldots, U_i(H-1;\mu_{-i}), U_i(\infty;\mu_{-i})-c_i\prod_{j\ne i}q_j\right\} -\gamma_i(\mu)\Bigg]. \end{equation} Importantly, the deviation immediately after the last date cannot be omitted.
Corollary (Finite certificate of failure of approximate equilibrium): Set $C(r)=(2n-1)D(r)$. The following are equivalent: \begin{align}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-66-69-6e-69-74-65-2d-69-66-66} &g(r)>0;\\ &\exists\,k\ge1\text{ such that }m_k(r)>\frac{C(r)}k.\notag \end{align} If an exact calculation establishes $m_k(r)\ge L>C(r)/k$, then \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-63-65-72-74-2d-6c-6f-77} {\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\mu)\ge L-\frac{C(r)}k>0 \quad\text{for \emph{every} infinite strategy profile }\mu. \end{equation}
Proof: Theorem Theorem (Quantitative finite-grid approximation) proves the implication from the second statement to the first and (eq:cert-low). Conversely, if $g(r)>0$, choose $k$ with $C(r)/k<g(r)$; since $m_k(r)\ge g(r)$, the second statement follows. ∎
Proposition (Rational counterexamples would suffice): For two games $r,\widetilde r$ with the same player set, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-4c-69-70-73-63-68-69-74-7a} |g(r)-g(\widetilde r)|\le2\|r-\widetilde r\|_\infty. \end{equation} Thus if a counterexample exists with real payoffs, there is also a counterexample with rational payoffs. Rational approximation may be chosen to preserve the exact singleton-payoff normalization and the strict signs $c_i<0$.
Proof: For fixed $\mu$, replacing $r$ by $\widetilde r$ changes every actual payoff and every pure-deviation payoff by at most $\|r-\widetilde r\|_\infty$. Therefore it changes ${\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\mu)$ by at most twice this amount. Infima over $\mu$ preserve the same Lipschitz bound. Choose a rational table sufficiently close to a hypothetical $r$ with $g(r)>0$. ∎
Corollary (Equivalent universal finite inequalities): Every $n$-player quitting game has undiscounted ${\varepsilon}$-equilibria for every ${\varepsilon}>0$ if and only if \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-75-6e-70-72-6f-76-65-64} m_k(r)\le\frac{(2n-1)D(r)}k \qquad\text{for every payoff table $r$ and every }k\ge1. \end{equation}
Proof: If all such games have $g(r)=0$, Theorem Theorem (Quantitative finite-grid approximation) gives (eq:unproved). Conversely, (eq:unproved) and $0\le g(r)\le m_k(r)$ imply $g(r)=0$ upon letting $k\to\infty$. ∎

The inequalities (eq:unproved) are not proved here. Theorem Theorem (Quantitative finite-grid approximation) gives only $m_k(r)\le g(r)+C(r)/k$. Replacing the right-hand side with $C(r)/k$ without another argument would assume the conclusion of the open conjecture. At fixed $n,k$, each grid profile's regret is the maximum of finitely many linear expressions in the payoff table, with rational coefficients. A complete lower-bound certificate for a fixed rational game is therefore finite and exactly checkable. It may, however, require an astronomically large enumeration.

From finite undiscounted profiles to uniform approximations

The reduction has a direct uniform-equilibrium interpretation. Let ${\operatorname{Reg}}_T(\mu)$ and ${\operatorname{Reg}}_\lambda(\mu)$ be the maximum unilateral gains under the $T$-stage average and $\lambda$-discounted evaluations in (eq:evaluations), respectively.
Theorem (Uniform error bounds for finite-support profiles): If each player's stopping-time distribution is supported on $K_H=\{0,\ldots,H-1,\infty\}$, then \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-75-6e-69-66-6f-72-6d-2d-62-6f-75-6e-64} {\operatorname{Reg}}_T(\mu)\le{\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\mu)+\frac{2D(r)H}{T}, \qquad {\operatorname{Reg}}_\lambda(\mu)\le{\operatorname{Reg}}_r(\mu)+2D(r)H\lambda. \end{equation}
Proof: Under the prescribed profile, either absorption occurs at a stage less than $H$, or nobody ever quits. Therefore its $T$-average payoff differs from the terminal payoff by at most $D(r)H/T$, and the discounted payoff differs by at most $D(r)[1-(1-\lambda)^H]\le D(r)H\lambda$.

The same bounds apply to any pure deviation to a quitting time $t\le H$ or $t=\infty$: absorption then either occurs before $H$ or never occurs. If a deviator instead chooses finite $t>H$, all opponents who quit do so before $H$ and thereby make the late deviation irrelevant. Conditional on all opponents choosing $\infty$, the evaluated payoff is a convex combination of $c_i$ and $r_i^{\{i\}}$, with the weight on $r_i^{\{i\}}$ no larger than it is at time $H$. For either evaluation, such a late deviation has a payoff no greater than the maximum of quitting at $H$ and never quitting. Hence the evaluated best-response payoff exceeds the undiscounted best-response payoff by at most the respective one-sided bound. Subtract the lower bound for the prescribed profile's own evaluated payoff. Mixed deviations are convex combinations of pure deviations, completing the proof. ∎

Corollary (Ordinary and uniform approximate existence are equivalent): For any finite-player quitting game in the sense of Section sec:model, the following are equivalent:
  1. for every ${\varepsilon}>0$ there is an undiscounted ${\varepsilon}$-equilibrium;
  2. for every ${\varepsilon}>0$ there is a uniform ${\varepsilon}$-equilibrium.
Proof: (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii): fix ${\varepsilon}>0$ and an undiscounted profile of regret below ${\varepsilon}/3$. By Theorem Theorem (Quantitative finite-grid approximation), or directly by quantile rounding and date compression, it can be approximated by a finite-support profile whose regret is less than $2{\varepsilon}/3$. Theorem Theorem (Uniform error bounds for finite-support profiles) makes this profile a uniform ${\varepsilon}$- equilibrium for sufficiently large $T$ and small $\lambda$.

(ii)$\Rightarrow$(i): for any fixed behavior profile and any fixed unilateral deviation, bounded convergence gives convergence of the long-run averages to the undiscounted payoff. A uniform ${\varepsilon}/2$- equilibrium satisfies the average-payoff deviation inequality for all large $T$. Passing to the limit separately for each deviation yields an undiscounted ${\varepsilon}/2$-equilibrium, hence an ${\varepsilon}$-equilibrium. ∎

No corresponding assertion about subgame perfection follows just from the quantile approximation: rounding and compression do not preserve incentive constraints at every continuation history.

Exact finite-grid calculations for the cyclic example

The finite-grid theorem is constructive in a theoretical sense. For a rational payoff table, (eq:finite-regret-formula) is a rational expression on every grid profile, so $m_k(r)$ is an exactly computable rational minimum by exhaustive enumeration. A sampled or local numerical minimum is not a certificate of nonexistence.

The exhaustive enumeration has a simple implementation-independent specification. Enumerate, for every player, the $\binom{H+k}{k}$ multisets of $k$ dates from $K_H$. For each product profile and every $i$, compute \begin{align*} \gamma_i(\mu) &=\sum_{(t_1,\ldots,t_n)\in K_H^n} \left(\prod_j\mu_j(t_j)\right) r_i^{S(t_1,\ldots,t_n)},\\ U_i(t;\mu_{-i}) &=\sum_{t_{-i}\in K_H^{n-1}} \left(\prod_{j\ne i}\mu_j(t_j)\right)r_i^{S(t,t_{-i})}, \end{align*} where $S(\cdot)$ is the set of indices attaining the least finite date, and $r^\varnothing=c$ when every date is infinite. Evaluate the $H+2$ candidate deviations in (eq:finite-deviation-list) and minimize (eq:regret). This is a finite exact certificate procedure involving no numerical tolerances.

Absorption paths, the residual obstacle, and conclusions

The absorption-path approach of Ashkenazi-Golan, Krasikov, Rainer, and Solan [AKRS2024] compactifies absorbing behavior profiles using accumulated absorption probability in place of physical time. Its sequential-perfectness conditions retain both jump behavior (positive simultaneous quitting probabilities at discrete events) and continuous components (vanishing quitting rates). The connection between such paths and limits of approximate equilibria depends on the exclusions of the elementary immediate-absorption and never-absorption cases, as specified precisely in [AKRS2024,AKRS2026]. The 2026 APS treatment develops an operator for a restricted class of paths in which one player is active at a time [AKRS2026]; it is not a proof of universal ordinary equilibrium existence.

The main lessons of the consolidated attempt can be stated without ambiguity:

  1. Proposition Proposition (Exact tail discontinuity) pinpoints the discontinuity that makes a naive limit of finite terminal-game equilibria unsafe.
  2. Lemma Lemma (Boundary estimate for an exact truncation equilibrium) is a correct and useful sufficient error estimate, but Theorem Theorem (Failure of the universal truncation criterion) proves that forcing this estimate to vanish along exact terminal-game Nash equilibria is not a viable general argument.
  3. Arbitrary finite-support profiles avoid that obstruction. Theorem Theorem (Quantitative finite-grid approximation) approximates the globally minimal regret with a computable, explicit $O(1/k)$ error bar; the finite certificate Corollary Corollary (Finite certificate of failure of approximate equilibrium) is the corresponding negative- resolution criterion.
  4. Corollary Corollary (Equivalent universal finite inequalities) isolates the unproved universal inequality (eq:unproved). Establishing it for every payoff table, or finding one finite verified violation, would respectively give a positive solution or a genuine counterexample.

Status. No proof of (eq:unproved), and no counterexample meeting Corollary Corollary (Finite certificate of failure of approximate equilibrium), is given here. Thus the general existence problem remains unresolved by the arguments in this manuscript. The finite reductions are presented as transparent mathematical research attempts; no claim is made that they were previously unknown or that they constitute a solution.
