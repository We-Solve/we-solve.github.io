Hidden-Layer Depth Complexity of Piecewise-Linear Functions

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

ReLU is $\sigma(t)=\max\{0,t\}$. Hidden layers are the intermediate affine-plus-activation stages, excluding the final affine output; a continuous piecewise-linear function has finitely many affine regions with continuous agreement across boundaries.

A feed-forward neural network composes affine maps and specified nonlinear activations. ReLU is $\sigma(t)=\max\{0,t\}$. Empirical risk averages a loss on training samples; population risk is its expectation on the target distribution, and generalization compares these risks.

A continuous piecewise-linear function is continuous and agrees with an affine function on each member of a finite polyhedral partition. A difference-of-convex decomposition writes it as $g-h$ with both $g$ and $h$ convex.

Literature qualification and attempted hyperplane-jump lower bound

The current primary Rueß et al. preprint, version 2, gives two-hidden-layer representations of $\max_n$ for $n\le12$; this is attributed work, not a result of this attempt. The catalogue's older summary mentioned a smaller range. We test a direct geometric lower-bound route and locate exactly where it stops.
Proposition: The function $f(x_1,x_2,x_3)=\max\{x_1,x_2,x_3\}$ cannot be represented by a finite-width ReLU network with one hidden layer and real weights, but can be represented with two hidden layers.
Proof: A one-hidden-layer representation, even allowing an additional affine term, has the form $\ell(x)+\sum_{j=1}^r a_j\sigma(w_j^Tx+b_j)$. Zero normals contribute constants and can be absorbed into $\ell$. The only potential gradient jumps are on the finitely many hyperplanes $H_j=\{w_j^Tx+b_j=0\}$. Let $H=\{x_1=x_2\}$. On its open part $U=\{x_1=x_2>x_3\}$ the target has a nonzero gradient jump $e_1-e_2$ when crossing from $x_1<x_2$ to $x_1>x_2$. A finite union of intersections of other hyperplanes with $H$ cannot cover the two-dimensional open set $U$: each distinct intersection is a line or empty. Thus some neurons must have hyperplane exactly $H$. Their combined gradient jump across $H$ is a fixed vector, independent of the crossing point, since each is a scalar ReLU of a multiple of $x_1-x_2$. At points of $H$ away from all other $H_j$, all remaining terms are affine in a neighborhood, and contribute zero jump. The fixed jump therefore must equal $e_1-e_2$ on $U$. But the open part $V=\{x_1=x_2<x_3\}$ also contains points avoiding all other hyperplanes. Near any such point the target is simply $x_3$ and has zero jump across $H$. The same fixed vector would have to be zero, a contradiction. For an explicit two-hidden-layer construction, the first layer produces \[ h=\sigma(x_1-x_2),\quad u_2=\sigma(x_2),\ v_2=\sigma(-x_2),\quad u_3=\sigma(x_3),\ v_3=\sigma(-x_3). \] The second layer produces $q=\sigma(h+u_2-v_2-u_3+v_3)$ and copies the nonnegative $u_3,v_3$ through ReLU units. The affine output is $q+u_3-v_3$. Since $u_i-v_i=x_i$, this equals $\sigma(\max(x_1,x_2)-x_3)+x_3=f$. ∎
Restricting the additional coordinates to $x_3$ yields the same one-hidden-layer obstruction for every $\max_d$ with $d\ge3$. The attempted next induction fails: in a second hidden layer a preactivation is piecewise affine, so its zero set need not be a whole hyperplane with a spatially constant jump. Our own two-layer construction already localizes such jumps. The proof therefore cannot be repeated to show a function needs more than two hidden layers.
