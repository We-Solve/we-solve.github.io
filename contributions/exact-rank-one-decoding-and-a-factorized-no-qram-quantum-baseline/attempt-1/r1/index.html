Exact rank-one syndrome decoding and constant-fiber rank factorizations

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

Fix a basis of $\mathbb F_{q^m}$ over $\mathbb F_q$. Expand $v\in\mathbb F_{q^m}^n$ into an $m\times n$ matrix over $\mathbb F_q$; its rank weight is that matrix's rank, independent of the chosen basis. The target is a vector of rank weight t solving the given linear syndrome equation $Hv=s$.

Attempt and results

Write $K=\mathbb F_{q^m}$ with a fixed effective $\mathbb F_q$ basis. The source requires rank weight exactly $t$, and allows unsatisfiable inputs. We solve the rank-one subcase and test a factorization route for general $t$. These facts are already suggested in the supplied summary; this is a complete rederivation and finite validation, not a new general decoding breakthrough.
Rank one is a linear-algebra decision and construction problem.
A nonzero vector $v\in K^n$ has rank weight one exactly when $v=a u$ for some $a\in K^*$ and nonzero $u\in\mathbb F_q^n$: its entries span a one-dimensional base-field subspace, and conversely such entries span the line generated by $a$. If $s\ne0$, introduce an additional unknown $b\in K$ and solve the $\mathbb F_q$-linear homogeneous system \[ H u=b s,\qquad u\in\mathbb F_q^n,\quad b\in K. \] Expanding in the fixed basis gives $rm$ scalar linear equations in $n+m$ base-field unknowns. A rank-one solution exists exactly when this kernel has an element with $b\ne0$. One direction takes $b=a^{-1}$; in the other, $u\ne0$ follows from $bs\ne0$, and $v=b^{-1}u$ has rank one and $Hv=s$. To decide whether the kernel projection to the $b$ coordinates is nonzero, compute a basis by elimination and inspect its $b$ components. If every basis vector has $b=0$, so does every linear combination; otherwise one such basis vector already supplies a solution. This avoids searching over all nonzero field elements. If $s=0$, rank-one solvability is equivalent to a nonzero $u\in\mathbb F_q^n$ with $Hu=0$: a solution $v=au$ implies this after dividing by $a$, and such a $u$ itself is a solution. This is a second finite kernel computation. Elimination and the required field operations have polynomial arithmetic cost in the explicit dimensions; bit costs depend on the specified finite-field representation and its arithmetic algorithms.
Every exact-rank factorization has the same multiplicity.
Expand a vector into its $m$ by $n$ matrix $V$ over $\mathbb F_q$. For $1\le t\le\min(m,n)$, every rank-$t$ matrix factors as $V=BC$, where $B$ is $m$ by $t$ of column rank $t$ and $C$ is $t$ by $n$ of row rank $t$: choose a basis of the column space of $V$ for $B$ and write each column in that basis. Conversely such a product has rank $t$, since $C$ maps onto $\mathbb F_q^t$ and $B$ is injective. If $B'C'=BC$, their common column space gives a unique $T\in\operatorname{GL}_t(\mathbb F_q)$ with $B'=BT$; injectivity of $B$ then forces $C'=T^{-1}C$. Every such $T$ works and distinct $T$ give distinct pairs. Thus every rank-$t$ matrix has exactly \[ |\operatorname{GL}_t(\mathbb F_q)|=\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}(q^t-q^i) \] full-rank factorizations; the product counts successive independent columns. Enumerating all $q^{t(m+n)}$ factor pairs, rejecting those without both full ranks, and testing the syndrome is therefore a complete finite algorithm. It is not polynomial for variable dimensions. Treating factor pairs as unique errors would miscount the number of solutions by the displayed multiplicity. A quantum search claim would further have to specify coherent preparation, verification cost, and handling of unknown or zero solution count; none is assumed for free here.
Exactness and finite checks.
If $t>\min(m,n)$ no solution can exist. Even for $t=1$, $H=I_n$ and $s=0$ force $v=0$, so an at-most-one decoder returning zero fails the exact-one task. The accompanying Python script exhaustively checked all 64 one-row, length-two syndrome instances over $\mathbb F_4=\mathbb F_2[z]/(z^2+z+1)$, comparing the rank-one kernel criterion with all 16 vectors per instance. It also checked all 252 full-rank factor pairs for $2$ by $3$ rank-two matrices over $\mathbb F_2$: there are 42 distinct matrices, each occurring six times. These finite checks passed and are illustrative validation of the proofs, not evidence of a general decoding improvement.
