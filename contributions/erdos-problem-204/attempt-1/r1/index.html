Erdős Problem #204

1. FORMAL RESTATEMENT

Can one choose a residue class $a_d\pmod d$ for every divisor $d>1$ of some integer $n$, covering all integers, such that two distinct classes can overlap only when their moduli are coprime?

2. LITERATURE AND SCOPE

The answer is no, by Adenwalla. We reconstruct his elementary prime-divisor obstruction and density argument. The proof below does not invoke the minimum-modulus theorem for covering systems and makes no claim of novelty.

3. A NECESSARY FACTORISATION RESTRICTION

Let $p$ be the least prime dividing $n>1$. The assumed overlap restriction implies \[ \omega(n/p)\le p-1, \tag{1} \] where $\omega$ counts distinct prime factors. Indeed, if distinct primes $q_1,\ldots,q_p$ divide $n/p$, all the moduli $p,pq_1,\ldots,pq_p$ are distinct divisors of $n$. Any two have gcd $p$, including when one $q_i$ equals $p$. Two residue classes whose moduli have gcd $p$ intersect exactly when their residues agree modulo $p$, by the Chinese remainder theorem. Thus these $p+1$ residues would all have to be different modulo $p$, which is impossible.

Write $n=p^a\prod_{i=1}^s q_i^{b_i}$, with $p<q_1<\cdots<q_s$ prime. If $a=1$, $1$ gives $s\le p-1$; if $a\ge2$, it gives $s\le p-2$.

4. DENSITY BELOW ONE IN ALL CASES

Any union of the chosen classes has density at most \[ \sum_{d\mid n,\ d>1}\frac1d =\left(\sum_{j=0}^{a}p^{-j}\right) \prod_{i=1}^s\left(\sum_{j=0}^{b_i}q_i^{-j}\right)-1. \tag{2} \] This follows by counting residues modulo $n$; the geometric product lists its divisors.

If $a\ge2$, we bound each finite geometric series strictly by its infinite one. Since $q_i\ge p+i$, \[ \sum_{d\mid n,d>1}\frac1d <\frac p{p-1}\prod_{i=1}^s\frac{q_i}{q_i-1}-1 \le\frac p{p-1}\prod_{i=1}^s\frac{p+i}{p+i-1}-1 =\frac{p+s}{p-1}-1\le1. \] Strictness holds even when $s=0$ because the $p$-series is finite.

Suppose $a=1$ and $p\ge3$. If $s=0$, $2$ is $1/p<1$. Otherwise $p$ and each $q_i$ are odd, so $q_i\ge p+1+i$. Therefore \[ \sum_{d\mid n,d>1}\frac1d <\frac{p+1}{p}\prod_{i=1}^s\frac{p+1+i}{p+i}-1 =\frac{p+s+1}{p}-1\le1. \]

The only remaining case has $p=2,a=1,s\le1$. The number $n=2$ clearly cannot work. Otherwise $n=2q^b$ with odd prime $q$. The moduli are $2,q^j,2q^j$ for $1\le j\le b$. The only coprime pairs of distinct moduli are $(2,q^j)$. Each such pair necessarily intersects with density $1/(2q^j)$ by CRT. All other pairwise intersections are empty by the hypothesis, and all triple intersections are empty. Inclusion--exclusion therefore gives the exact union density \[ \delta=\frac12+\sum_{j=1}^b\frac1{q^j} +\sum_{j=1}^b\frac1{2q^j} -\sum_{j=1}^b\frac1{2q^j} =\frac12+\sum_{j=1}^b\frac1{q^j} <\frac12+\frac1{q-1}\le1. \] This handles the case where the simple union bound alone might exceed one. For $n=1$ the family is empty and also cannot cover.

5. FINAL AND CHECKS

SOLVED: no such integer $n$ exists. Every hypothetical admissible family falls into one of the cases above and has density strictly less than one, whereas a finite periodic covering has density one. The proof includes prime powers, the even exceptional family, and the possibility $q_i=p$ in the obstruction. It is a full reconstruction of the known proof, with only elementary CRT and inclusion--exclusion as inputs.
