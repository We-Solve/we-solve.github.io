Inscribed extensions: slack factorization and why small ordinary rank does not suffice

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

An inscribed polytope has all its vertices on one Euclidean sphere. An extension of $P$ is a polyhedron $Q$ together with an affine map projecting $Q$ onto $P$. Extension complexity is the minimum number of inequalities in any such description of $Q$; equations do not count as inequalities. The dimension $d$ is fixed and the constant in $O(\sqrt n)$ may depend on $d$.

Statement recorded in the supplied source.

Verbatim supplied catalogue question

For every fixed $d\geq2$, does every $d$-dimensional polytope with $n$ vertices on a common sphere have extension complexity $O(\sqrt n)$?

Attempt through slack matrices

The primary report asks for every fixed dimension and every inscribed polytope, rather than only a random inscribed polytope. We examine whether the low algebraic rank of its slack matrix proves a short extension. The construction below identifies the extra positivity condition needed and gives a complete obstruction to omitting it.
A nonnegative factorization gives an extension.
Let $P=\operatorname{conv}\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\} =\{x:Ax\le b\}$ be a full-dimensional polytope and put $S_{ij}=b_i-a_i^Tv_j$. Suppose $S=TU$, where both factors are entrywise nonnegative and $T$ has $q$ columns. Define \[ Q=\{(x,y):Ax+Ty=b,\ y\in\mathbb R^q_{\ge0}\}. \] Every projected point of $Q$ satisfies $Ax\le b$, since $Ty\ge0$, and is therefore in $P$. Conversely each vertex $v_j$ lifts with $y=U_{*,j}$ because $Av_j+TU_{*,j}=b$. Convex combinations of these lifts give every point of $P$. Thus the projection is exactly $P$, using only $q$ inequalities besides equations. Finding a nonnegative factorization of inner size $O(\sqrt n)$ would therefore supply the desired bound.

Ordinary rank is much easier: writing rows $(b_i,-a_i^T)$ and columns $(1,v_j)^T$ shows $\operatorname{rank}S\le d+1$. Those factors need not be nonnegative. Discarding that requirement invalidates the implication $Ty\ge0$, so the same formula no longer proves a valid extension.

A complete general lower bound showing the distinction.
The one-vertex case has the trivial bound $1\le2^q$. For $n\ge2$, any extension of a polytope with $n$ vertices described by $q$ inequalities has $n\le2^q$. Here equations may be present and the extension may be unbounded. To prove the claim, for each vertex $v$ choose an objective on $P$ uniquely maximized at $v$. Such an objective exists directly: the compact convex hull of the other vertices does not contain $v$; let $z$ be its closest point to $v$. Differentiating squared distance along the segment from $z$ to any other point $u$ of that hull gives $(v-z)\cdot(u-z)\le0$. Hence the vector $v-z$ strictly separates $v$ from that hull. Pulling this objective back to the extension gives a nonempty exposed face $F_v$ projecting to $v$. Different vertices give different faces.

Every nonempty face of a polyhedron is determined by which of its $q$ defining inequalities hold identically as equalities. For a direct verification in the present exposed-face case, choose a point $x$ in the face at which exactly those common equalities are tight. If every inequality is identically tight, choose any point of the nonempty face. Otherwise obtain such a point by averaging, for each inequality not identically tight, a face point with positive slack. If $y$ in the polyhedron satisfies all the common equalities, then for sufficiently small $t>0$ the point $z=x+t(x-y)$ is feasible: common equalities are preserved and each remaining positive slack at $x$ allows a small perturbation. Since $x$ is a strict convex combination of $y,z$, and the defining linear objective is maximal at $x$, both $y,z$ maximize it too. Thus $y$ lies in the face, proving the characterization. There are at most $2^q$ subsets of inequalities, which proves $n\le2^q$ and $q\ge\log_2 n$.

Regular $n$-gons have all vertices on a circle and ordinary slack rank at most three, yet this bound makes their extension complexities unbounded as $n$ grows. Consequently small ordinary rank, even with the inscribed hypothesis, does not imply an extension with that many inequalities. This does not refute an $O(\sqrt n)$ extension, which is much larger than the lower bound just proved.

The missing step and a baseline construction.
Every $n$-vertex polytope has an $n$-inequality extension using $\lambda_j\ge0$, $\sum_j\lambda_j=1$ and $x=\sum_j\lambda_jv_j$, by the definition of convex hull. The source's random-polytope and planar results do not turn this into a worst-case fixed-dimensional bound by themselves. The route above requires a nonnegative factorization exploiting the common sphere or another explicit construction with the desired size; neither is supplied here.
