Attempted inference from an integrality-gap bound

This fragment emphasizes that a gap-two theorem for an LP did not settle Fiorini's extreme-point question. We test the tempting general inference that a covering relaxation with integrality gap at most two must have a coordinate at least one half at every extreme point. The following abstract covering polyhedron disproves that inference; it is explicitly not asserted to be an FVS strong-density polyhedron. Let $q=(1/3,1/3,1/3,1/3)$ and let $\mathcal I$ be the six incidence vectors of two-element subsets of $\{1,2,3,4\}$. Set \[ Q=\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal I\cup\{q\})+\mathbb R_{\ge0}^4. \]
Proposition: The point $q$ is an extreme point with every coordinate below $1/2$, while for every nonnegative cost vector the minimum integral cost over $Q$ is at most $3/2$ times the minimum fractional cost.
Proof: The all-ones objective has unique minimizer $q$: its value there is $4/3$, while every vector in $\mathcal I$ has value two and a nonzero nonnegative added vector increases the value. A unique minimizer is extreme, because a nontrivial convex decomposition into feasible points would make both endpoints minimizers. Every integral vector of $Q$ has at least two coordinates at least one. Indeed, if its convex representation assigns positive weight to $q$, all four coordinates are positive, hence are at least one when integral. If that weight is zero, the coordinate sum before the nonnegative addition is two, and a vector with only one positive integral coordinate cannot dominate a convex combination of two-element incidence vectors, each of whose coordinates is at most one. Therefore the integral optimum is $I=c_{(1)}+c_{(2)}$, the sum of the two smallest costs, attained by the corresponding incidence vector. The fractional optimum is $\min\{I,\tfrac13\sum_i c_i\}$. Since $c_{(1)}+c_{(2)}\le\tfrac12\sum_i c_i$, in either case $I$ is at most $3/2$ times that optimum. This remains true if the optimum is zero, by the same inequality. ∎
Thus the numerical integrality-gap assertion by itself cannot supply the desired extreme-point coordinate. Specific structure of the strong-density inequalities is essential; the constructed $Q$ is only an obstruction to the logical shortcut.
