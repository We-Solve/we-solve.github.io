Research attempt: A lattice-basis verification trap for a proposed Markov-basis interface

Let $A=(1,1,1)$ and, for $b\in\mathbb N$, let $\mathcal F_b=\{x\in\mathbb N^3:x_1+x_2+x_3=b\}$. An allowed move $m\in\ker_{\mathbb Z}A$ can be added or subtracted only if the resulting vector remains nonnegative. A Markov basis in this example means a finite move set whose resulting graph is connected on every nonempty fiber.

Suppose a backend returns \[ m_1=(1,-1,0),\qquad m_2=(1,-2,1). \] Both lie in the integer kernel, and they even form an integer-lattice basis of that kernel. To prove spanning, take $z=(a,b,c)$ with $a+b+c=0$. Then \[ z=(a-c)m_1+c m_2, \] since the second coordinate becomes $-a-c=b$. Independence follows because the third coordinate of $\alpha m_1+\beta m_2=0$ forces $\beta=0$, then the first forces $\alpha=0$.

Nevertheless the fiber $\mathcal F_1=\{e_1,e_2,e_3\}$ is disconnected. The move $m_1$ joins $e_1$ to $e_2$ and cannot touch $e_3$ while remaining nonnegative. Either sign of $m_2$ requires at least two units of mass before application: its positive part and negative part each have total two. More explicitly, applying $m_2$ requires $x_2\ge2$, and applying $-m_2$ requires $x_1,x_3\ge1$, both impossible when the total is one. Thus $e_3$ is isolated. Checking integer-kernel membership or even lattice generation does not certify the promised Markov property.

For comparison the moves $u=e_1-e_3$ and $v=e_2-e_3$ do connect every fiber. Given $x\in\mathcal F_b$, repeatedly subtract $u$ while $x_1>0$ and subtract $v$ while $x_2>0$. Each subtraction transfers one unit to coordinate three and stays nonnegative, ending at $(0,0,b)$. Reversing the corresponding path for another point connects any two points. This proves the complete Markov property for this selected matrix and supplies a positive control for a small interface test.

The failed route is therefore exact: a service can return a mathematically correct lattice basis and still fail its stated fiber-connectivity purpose. A test at $b=1$ detects this particular error, but testing finitely many fibers without a separate theorem does not certify all fibers for arbitrary matrices.
