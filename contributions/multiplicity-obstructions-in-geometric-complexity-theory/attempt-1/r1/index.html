Multiplicity obstructions: an explicit small calculation and the orbit-closure gap

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$. Specialized notation and index ranges follow the cited source and the definitions below.

In the usual GCT setting, the general linear group $G=\operatorname{GL}(V)$ acts on a space of polynomials, with orbit $Gp$ and Zariski closure $\overline{Gp}$. Its coordinate ring is graded by polynomial degree. For an irreducible polynomial representation indexed by a partition $\lambda$, $\operatorname{mult}_\lambda\mathbb C[\overline{Gp}]_d$ is its multiplicity in the degree-$d$ part. Inclusion $\overline{Gp}\subseteq\overline{Gq}$ induces a surjection of coordinate rings in the reverse direction, so a larger multiplicity on the left obstructs such inclusion.

A full binary-quadratic auxiliary calculation

This subsection uses a deliberately different, explicitly labeled pair. Let $V$ be the space of complex symmetric matrices $Q=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, equivalently binary quadratics $ax^2+2bxy+cy^2$. Let $GL_2$ act by $Q\mapsto gQg^T$. Take $p=I$ and $q=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$.

The orbit closure of $p$ is all of $V$. To see this, every invertible complex symmetric matrix is congruent to $I$. Indeed its symmetric bilinear form has a vector with nonzero self-pairing: otherwise $B(u+v,u+v)-B(u,u)-B(v,v)=2B(u,v)$ makes the whole form zero. Scale that vector to self-pairing one using a complex square root, take its orthogonal complement, and repeat in dimension one. This constructs the congruence for an invertible form. Invertible symmetric matrices are dense, because $Q+tI$ is invertible except at the finitely many roots of its monic determinant polynomial. A polynomial vanishing on the orbit consequently vanishes on all $V$ by continuity.

The orbit closure of $q$ is $Y=\{ac-b^2=0\}$. Every nonzero rank-one symmetric form is congruent to $q$, by the same orthogonal decomposition on its nonzero part, and zero is a limit under scalar matrices. The displayed determinant equation defines a closed set containing that orbit and no rank-two form, proving the claim.

Coordinate functions carry the contragredient action $(g\cdot F)(Q)=F(g^{-1}Qg^{-T})$. The polynomial $\Delta=ac-b^2$ of coordinate degree two spans the character $g\mapsto\det(g)^{-2}$. In the conventional dual Schur notation this is the dual of the partition $\lambda=(2,2)$; we spell out the character to avoid a hidden sign convention for coordinate rings.

Its multiplicity in $\mathbb C[V]_2$ is exactly one. A diagonal $g=\operatorname{diag}(t,s)$ acts on $a,b,c$ by factors $t^{-2},t^{-1}s^{-1},s^{-2}$. Therefore a degree-two polynomial with weight $t^{-2}s^{-2}$ is a combination $Aac+Bb^2$. For $g=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&z\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, substitution sends $(a,b,c)$ to $(a-2zb+z^2c,b-zc,c)$. Invariance under this determinant-one matrix forces $A+B=0$ by the coefficients of $zbc$ and $z^2c^2$. Conversely $\Delta$ has the required character under every $g$, by multiplicativity of determinant. The character space is precisely its one-dimensional span.

In $\mathbb C[Y]_2$ the same multiplicity is zero. The degree-two relations vanishing on $Y$ are exactly multiples of $ac-b^2$: substitute $(a,b,c)=(u^2,uv,v^2)$ into the six quadratic monomials. Their images are $u^4,u^3v,u^2v^2,u^2v^2,uv^3,v^4$, with just the indicated one-dimensional relation. The weight $t^{-2}s^{-2}$ subspace of the quotient is therefore spanned by the common class of $ac$ and $b^2$. But the unipotent substitution sends the class of $ac$ to that of $ac-2zbc+z^2c^2$, which is not fixed for all $z$; the classes of $bc$ and $c^2$ are nonzero and independent by the same substitution test. Thus no nonzero vector in that weight space has the character, proving the multiplicity zero. This gives the fully checked auxiliary pair $(\lambda,d)=((2,2),2)$ with multiplicities one and zero. It is a simple known occurrence obstruction, not the source's nonoccurrence multiplicity construction.

Testing the source power-sum/product route directly

For the concrete source case $m=D=3$, the Fermat cubic $p=x^3+y^3+z^3$ cannot be a product of three homogeneous linear forms. Its gradient $(3x^2,3y^2,3z^2)$ vanishes only at zero. If a cubic is a product $l_1l_2l_3$, there is a nonzero common zero of $l_1,l_2$ because two homogeneous linear equations in three unknowns have a nonzero solution. At that point every term in the product-rule gradient vanishes. Thus such a product has a nonzero singular point, unlike $p$. Since every element in the $GL_3$ orbit of $xyz$ is such a product, this proves ordinary orbit nonmembership for the source pair.

This argument alone does not compute any coordinate-ring multiplicity, and it has not supplied the algebraic closedness or a separating polynomial needed to promote orbit nonmembership to the exact orbit- closure multiplicity claim. The latter separation is known in the primary source, but is not newly proved by this gradient calculation. For another boundary check, the analogous binary degree-two power sum is already a product: $x^2+y^2=(x+iy)(x-iy)$. The two linear forms are independent, so this power sum lies in the $GL_2$ orbit of $xy$. The auxiliary rank-one quadratic pair above therefore must not be confused with the source's degree-two power-sum/product pair.

Why symmetry data for an orbit need an extension argument

Consider the elementary action of $\mathbb C^*$ on the affine line by multiplication. The orbit of 1 is $\mathbb C^*$ and its closure is $\mathbb C$. Their coordinate rings are respectively $\mathbb C[t,t^{-1}]$ and $\mathbb C[t]$: on the punctured line, inverting $t$ is allowed, whereas a polynomial regular on the full line cannot have a negative power. For every integer $r$, the monomial $t^r$ has coordinate-action character $a\mapsto a^{-r}$ on the orbit; only $r\ge0$ occur in the closure's coordinate ring. In particular the orbit's vector $t^{-1}$ does not extend across zero.

The injection of a closure's coordinate ring into that of its dense orbit therefore provides upper bounds from orbit multiplicities, not automatic lower bounds in the closure. A strategy comparing symmetry- group invariant dimensions for two orbits needs an extension argument on the side where a closure lower bound is required. This example identifies the precise missing implication; it does not refute the special extension theorems proved in the cited GCT work.
