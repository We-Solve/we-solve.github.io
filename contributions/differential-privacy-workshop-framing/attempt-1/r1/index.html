Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$. Specialized notation and index ranges follow the cited source and the definitions below.

Fix a neighboring-dataset relation, such as changing one row. A randomized mechanism $M$ is $(\epsilon,\delta)$-differentially private if $\Pr[M(D)\in E]\le e^\epsilon\Pr[M(D^{\prime})\in E]+\delta$ for every neighboring $D,D^{\prime}$ and measurable output event $E$, where $\epsilon\ge0$ and $0\le\delta<1$. A statistical query $q:\mathcal X\to[0,1]$ has empirical answer $q(D)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n q(x_i)$. An $\alpha$-accurate release approximates the requested answers within $\alpha$; the query class and probability of success must also be specified. Complexity depends on dataset size, dimension, query access, and these privacy and accuracy parameters.

Randomization is necessary even before computational questions

The source record is broad workshop framing and is retained as such. We test a concrete proposed simplification: use a deterministic release to avoid the complexity of randomization. Assume finite databases of a fixed size over a finite nonempty universe, with one-row replacement as adjacency and pure differential privacy at some finite $\varepsilon\ge0$.
Proposition: Every deterministic pure-private mechanism on this database space is constant. For one binary record, a randomized binary estimate has worst-case probability of being correct at most $e^\varepsilon/(1+e^\varepsilon)$, and randomized response attains this value.
Proof: If neighboring databases $D,D'$ had different deterministic outputs, the event consisting of the output on $D$ would have probability one on $D$ and zero on $D'$, contradicting the privacy inequality. Any two fixed-size databases are joined by changing their rows one at a time, so the output is constant throughout the space. For a binary estimate let $p$ be its worst-case correct probability. Then $\Pr(M(1)=1)\ge p$ and $\Pr(M(0)=1)\le1-p$. Privacy for the event $\{1\}$ gives $p\le e^\varepsilon(1-p)$, hence the upper bound. Randomized response outputs the true input with probability $a=e^\varepsilon/(1+e^\varepsilon)$ and its complement with probability $1-a$. For either singleton output, the likelihood ratio between the two inputs is either $a/(1-a)=e^\varepsilon$ or its reciprocal. Summing over outputs verifies privacy for every event. Both inputs have success probability $a$, proving attainment. ∎
