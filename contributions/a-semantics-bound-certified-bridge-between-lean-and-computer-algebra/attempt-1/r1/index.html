An exact certificate route from a CAS to a checker

For one sharply specified interoperability task, let $K$ be a field and $R=K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ with exact coefficient operations. Given $g_1,\ldots,g_s,f\in R$, an untrusted computer algebra system may propose coefficients $q_1,\ldots,q_s\in R$ certifying that $f$ belongs to the ideal generated by the $g_i$.
Proposition: Checking the polynomial identity $f=\sum_iq_ig_i$ is a sound certificate of ideal membership. At every common zero $a$ of the $g_i$ in any field extension of $K$, such a certificate also proves $f(a)=0$.
Proof: By definition the generated ideal consists of finite sums $\sum_i r_i g_i$ with $r_i\in R$. The checked coefficients exhibit such a sum. Polynomial evaluation at $a$ respects addition and multiplication, so the identity gives $f(a)=\sum_i q_i(a)g_i(a)=0$. To check the identity algebraically, expand both sides using distributivity, collect coefficients of each monomial, and compare them in $K$. Polynomials are finite coefficient functions on exponent vectors, so agreement of every coefficient is exactly equality in $R$. ∎
For $R=\mathbb Q[x,y]$, take $g_1=x^2-y$, $g_2=xy-1$, and $f=x^3-1$. The certificate $(q_1,q_2)=(x,1)$ works because \[ x(x^2-y)+(xy-1)=x^3-1. \] This makes one bridge technically concrete, but it cannot simply be reused to certify a different claim. For example, verifying that a returned factor list multiplies to $x^2-1$ does not certify irreducibility: the one-item list $(x^2-1)$ passes the multiplication test even though $(x-1)(x+1)$ is a nontrivial factorization over $\mathbb Q$.
