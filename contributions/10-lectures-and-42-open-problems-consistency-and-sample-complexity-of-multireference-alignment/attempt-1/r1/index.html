Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

Fix a signal length $L$ and unknown $u\in\mathbb{R}^L$. A cyclic shift acts by $(R_\ell u)_j=u_{j-\ell\bmod L}$. In the standard uniform-shift Gaussian model, independent observations are \[ y_i=R_{\ell_i}u+\sigma\xi_i,\qquad \ell_i\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{Z}/L\mathbb{Z}),\quad \xi_i\sim N(0,I_L), \] with independent shifts and noise, and noise level $\sigma>0$. The signal is identifiable only up to a common cyclic shift; estimation error is therefore $\min_\ell\|\widehat u-R_\ell u\|_2$.

For the unitary discrete Fourier transform $\widehat u_k=L^{-1/2}\sum_j u_j e^{-2\pi i jk/L}$, the power spectrum is $(|\widehat u_k|^2)_k$; shifts leave it invariant. The noise contribution to the empirical power spectrum must be removed or otherwise fixed before judging phase consistency. Consistency means that the orbit error tends to zero in probability as the number of observations tends to infinity at fixed $L$ and $\sigma$. Sample complexity asks how many observations guarantee a specified error and success probability, as a function of signal class, $L$ and $\sigma$.

Two distinct likelihood conventions matter. A joint/profile estimator optimizes over unknown shifts as incidental parameters, minimizing $\sum_i\min_\ell\|y_i-R_\ell u\|_2^2$. A marginalized maximum-likelihood estimator instead maximizes \[ \prod_i\left(\frac1L\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1} \exp\left[-\frac{\|y_i-R_\ell u\|_2^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]\right). \] These objectives need not have the same statistical behavior; the source lecture discussion refers to the quasi-MLE as well as the MLE.

Research attempt: Compact-family marginalized likelihood consistency and profile bias

Fix $L\ge1$ and $\sigma>0$. For a signal $v$ let $p_v$ be its uniform-shift Gaussian density. Distinct shift orbits have distinct densities. To see this, suppose $p_v=p_u$. Multiplication by $\exp(\|y\|^2/(2\sigma^2))$ gives equality between two finite sums \[ \sum_{s\in\{R_\ell u\}_{\ell=0}^{L-1}} e^{-\|s\|^2/(2\sigma^2)}e^{y\cdot s/\sigma^2}, \] where the sum is a multiset, and the analogous sum for $v$. Choose a real direction $w$ giving distinct dot products on every distinct point in the union of the two supports. Such $w$ exists because finitely many proper hyperplanes cannot fill $\mathbb R^L$ (one may take $w=(1,t,\ldots,t^{L-1})$ outside the finitely many roots of the corresponding nonzero polynomials). Substitute $y=tw$ and let $t\to+\infty$ after dividing by the largest exponential. Its coefficient must agree on both sides; subtract it and repeat. Thus the supports coincide, so $v$ is a shift of $u$. This includes periodic and zero signals.

For a fixed finite candidate list $v_1,\ldots,v_m$, including the true orbit, put $Z_v(Y)=\log(p_v(Y)/p_u(Y))$. A finite Gaussian mixture with bounded centers satisfies $|Z_v(y)|\le C_v+D_v\|y\|$: factor the common Gaussian term, and bound each logarithm of a finite exponential sum between its largest linear exponent and that exponent plus $\log L$, retaining the constant norm terms. Hence $Z_v$ has finite second moment under $p_u$. Using $\log t\le t-1$ and integration gives $E_u Z_v\le0$, with equality only when $p_v/p_u=1$ almost everywhere. Continuity and the preceding argument make the inequality strict for a wrong orbit. Set $K_v=-E_u Z_v>0$ and $s_v^2=\operatorname{Var}_u Z_v$. Independence yields \[ \Pr_u\!\left\{\sum_{i=1}^N Z_v(Y_i)\ge0\right\} \le\frac{s_v^2}{NK_v^2}. \] This follows by applying Markov to the squared centered sample average. Summing over the finitely many wrong candidates proves consistency on that list, with an explicit, though implicit-in-$K_v$, finite-list error bound.

The final audit suggested carrying this argument through the compact parameter family, and that step can in fact be completed. The conclusion is consistency of a global marginalized MLE at fixed $L,\sigma$, with the true power spectrum fixed exactly. This is known statistical material rederived here, not a new solution of the source's bundled question.

Let \[ \Theta=\{v\in\mathbb R^L: |\widehat v_k|=|\widehat u_k| \text{ for every }k\},\qquad R=\|u\|. \] The unitary Fourier transform preserves norm, so every member has norm $R$. The set is closed and bounded, hence compact, and contains the true orbit. When $R=0$ it contains only zero, making consistency immediate. Assume $R>0$. Write $f_v(y)=\log p_v(y)-\log p_u(y)$ and $G(y)=(\|y\|+R)/\sigma^2$. Differentiating the finite Gaussian mixture gives \[ \nabla_v\log p_v(y) =\sigma^{-2}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1} w_\ell(v,y)R_{-\ell}y-v\right), \quad w_\ell\ge0,\quad\sum_\ell w_\ell=1. \] Its norm is at most $G(y)$ throughout the ball $\|v\|\le R$. Integrating on the segment between two points of $\Theta$ (which stays in that ball even when it leaves $\Theta$) proves \[ |f_v(y)-f_w(y)|\le G(y)\|v-w\|,\qquad |f_v(y)|\le2RG(y). \] The Gaussian observation has finite second moment, so $E_uG^2<\infty$.

Here is the uniform convergence argument explicitly. Let $P_N$ denote the empirical average and $P$ expectation under $p_u$. For any fixed $h>0$, compactness gives a finite $h$-net $v_1,\ldots,v_M$ in $\Theta$. The displayed Lipschitz bound gives \[ \sup_{v\in\Theta}|P_Nf_v-Pf_v| \le\max_{j\le M}|P_Nf_{v_j}-Pf_{v_j}|+h(P_NG+PG). \] For each net point, variance of its sample average is its finite variance divided by $N$; the same holds for $G$. Markov's inequality on squared deviations and a finite union bound make the maximum tend to zero in probability and make $P_NG\to PG$ in probability. Given a desired positive tolerance, first choose $h$ so that $3hPG$ is smaller than half that tolerance, then take $N$ large. This proves the supremum tends to zero in probability. The supremum is measurable, since continuous functions on compact $\Theta$ have the same supremum over a fixed countable dense subset.

The population objective $m(v)=Pf_v$ is continuous by the same Lipschitz bound after expectation. The previously proved density identifiability and strict logarithm inequality say $m(v)=0$ exactly on the orbit of $u$, and $m(v)<0$ elsewhere. For each $\epsilon>0$ the set \[ K_\epsilon=\{v\in\Theta:\min_\ell\|v-R_\ell u\|\ge\epsilon\} \] is compact. If nonempty, its maximum population objective is a strictly negative number $-c_\epsilon$. On the event that the uniform empirical error is less than $c_\epsilon/2$, every member of $K_\epsilon$ has strictly negative empirical objective, while $f_u$ is identically zero. Therefore no global maximizer lies in $K_\epsilon$. Global maximizers exist because the sample objective is continuous on compact $\Theta$. Every measurable choice of a global maximizer is consequently orbit-consistent in probability. In fact the event just proved confines all maximizers simultaneously; it does not depend on a particular tie rule.

This proof covers zero and periodic signals as well as generic signals at fixed $L$ and positive fixed $\sigma$. It assumes the true Fourier magnitudes are imposed exactly. Estimating those magnitudes from the data creates a moving constraint set and needs an additional stability argument. The proof also does not locate a global maximizer computationally, give sharp high-noise rates, or analyze profile likelihood.

A second tempting extension identifies marginalized likelihood with profile alignment. It fails before any phase conclusion: for $L=2$, true signal $(a,-a)$, $a>0$, and unrestricted fitted contrast $c\ge0$, each centered observation has contrast $d=\pm a+\zeta$ with nondegenerate Gaussian $\zeta$. Its profiled squared contrast residual is $2(|d|-c)^2$, so the population minimizer is $c=E|a+\zeta|>a$. Indeed $E|a+\zeta|-a=2E[-(a+\zeta)\mathbf1_{a+\zeta<0}]>0$. This is an actual bias calculation, but fixing the power spectrum forces $c=a$ in this example. It therefore does not refute the source's power-fixed phase-consistency question.

Scope and remaining gaps

This is standard statistical material, without a novelty claim. The original also asks about a profile/quasi-likelihood interpretation and sample complexity.
