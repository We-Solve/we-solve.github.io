Erdős Problem #349: floors of geometric sequences

1. Statement and indexing

For which $t,\alpha>0$ is the set of positive distinct values \[ A(t,\alpha)=\{\lfloor t\alpha^n\rfloor:n=0,1,2,\ldots\}\setminus\{0\} \] complete, meaning that its finite subset sums contain every sufficiently large integer? A convention starting at $n=1$ is obtained by replacing $t$ by $t\alpha$. Finite initial terms can affect completeness, so this shift must not be dismissed as harmless. Repeated values cannot be used repeatedly in the set interpretation of the question. The following boundary classification and positive family leave the general problem unresolved.

2. Two incomplete ranges

For $0<\alpha\le1$ there are only finitely many positive distinct values, so their subset sums form a finite set. For $\alpha>2$, the number of indices whose positive value is at most $X$ is at most $\log_\alpha X+O_{t,\alpha}(1)$. Only these indices can contribute to a sum at most $X$. Even allowing repetition of equal values at different indices gives at most \[ O_{t,\alpha}(X^{\log_\alpha 2})=o(X) \] different sums. The subset-sum set has density zero, hence is not complete.

3. Exact classification at $\alpha=2$

Put $a_j=\lfloor t2^j\rfloor$ and $\varepsilon_j=a_{j+1}-2a_j\in\{0,1\}$. Telescoping gives \[ \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_j =a_n-a_0-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\varepsilon_j. \tag{1} \] If $a_0+\sum_{j<n}\varepsilon_j\ge2$ for some $n$, it remains so for every larger $n$. Then \[ \sum_{j<n}a_j+1<a_n. \] The integer on the left is not representable even using indexed terms: all earlier terms together are too small, and later positive terms are too large. These holes tend to infinity, proving incompleteness also for distinct values.

Otherwise $a_0+\sum_{j<n}\varepsilon_j\le1$ for every $n$. Since $t>0$, either $a_0=1$ and all $\varepsilon_j=0$, or $a_0=0$ and exactly one $\varepsilon_j$ equals $1$. The possibility of no such digit in the latter case would force all $a_j=0$ and hence $t=0$. Taking $t=\lim_{j\to\infty}2^{-j}a_j$ shows precisely \[ t=2^{-s}\qquad(s=0,1,2,\ldots). \] Conversely these parameters give the positive distinct values $1,2,4,\ldots$, whose subset sums contain all nonnegative integers. Therefore \[ \boxed{A(t,2)\text{ is complete}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ t=2^{-s}\text{ for an integer }s\ge0.} \]

4. A complete proof of a positive family below $2$

We prove that $A(1,\alpha)$ is strongly complete whenever $1<\alpha\le5/3$. List its positive distinct values as $b_1<b_2<\cdots$. They begin with $b_1=1,b_2=2$. To see the second value explicitly, if $\alpha\le\sqrt2$, the first power leaving $[1,2)$ is less than $2\alpha\le2\sqrt2<3$. If $\sqrt2<\alpha\le5/3$, the power $\alpha^2$ lies in $[2,25/9]$, while $1,\alpha$ have floor $1$.

For any attained value $a\ge2$, consider its last occurrence before the next distinct value $b$. The corresponding real power is less than $a+1$, so \[ b<\alpha(a+1)\le \frac53(a+1)\le2a+1. \] As $b$ is an integer, $b\le2a$. Thus $b_{i+1}\le2b_i$ for every $i$. Writing $S_i=b_1+\cdots+b_i$, induction gives \[ S_i\ge2b_i-1: \quad S_{i+1}\ge2b_i-1+b_{i+1}\ge2b_{i+1}-1. \] It follows that $b_{i+1}\le S_i+1$. Starting with the represented interval $[0,1]$, the intervals $[0,S_i]$ and $[b_{i+1},S_i+b_{i+1}]$ therefore meet or are adjacent. Induction proves that every nonnegative integer is a subset sum of distinct values $b_i$.

More generally, $t=\alpha^{-s}$ for an integer $s\ge0$ contains this entire sequence among the indices $n\ge s$, so is also complete. This is a statement about a specified family of parameters, not about all $t$ in that range of $\alpha$.

5. Assessment and provenance

The missing part is a classification of arbitrary $t>0$ when $1<\alpha<2$, including the conjectural universal completeness below the golden ratio. Neither the doubling boundary nor the proved $t=1$ family supplies such a classification. In particular modular tests for a finite prefix do not decide it.
