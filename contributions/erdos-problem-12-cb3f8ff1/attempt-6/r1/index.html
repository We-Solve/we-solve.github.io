Round 2

1) ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE

I pursue path (C) on the natural square-based class generated by the Round-1 example. More precisely, I prove an exact structural criterion for sets of the form \[ A_S:=\{s^2:s\in S\}, \] where every prime factor of every $s\in S$ is congruent to $3\pmod 4$. This gives two strictly new consequences beyond Round 1:

  1. a denser explicit construction than the prime-square family, namely semiprime-squares, with \[ |A\cap[1,N]|\gg \frac{\sqrt N\,\log\log N}{\log N}, \]
  2. a fatal obstruction to the Round-1 square strategy for Q1: every set in this entire square-based class has size $o(\sqrt N)$, so no such construction can prove a positive answer to Q1.
Globally, Q1--Q3 remain unresolved.

2) ROUND-1 FOUNDATION USED

I use the following Round-1 ingredients without reproving them.

  • Lemma 12.1 (residue-class obstruction). For fixed $a\in A$, among larger elements of $A$ one cannot occupy both residues $r$ and $-r\pmod a$. In particular, in residue class $0\pmod a$, there cannot be two distinct larger multiples of $a$.
  • Lemma 12.2 (prime-square example). The proof there uses the key modular fact that for a prime $p\equiv 3\pmod 4$, the congruence $x^2\equiv -1\pmod p$ has no solution.
The new work below strengthens the modular idea from Lemma 12.2 to an exact $p$-adic statement and then converts the residue-$0$ obstruction from Lemma 12.1 into a full characterization of the square-based class.

3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)

The genuinely new tool is the following $p$-adic strengthening of the Round-1 congruence argument.

New tool. If $p\equiv 3\pmod 4$ is prime, then for all integers $x,y$, \[ \nu_p(x^2+y^2)=2\min\left(\nu_p(x),\nu_p(y)\right). \] Equivalently: if $p^{2e}\mid x^2+y^2$, then $p^e\mid x$ and $p^e\mid y$.

This yields an exact reduction for the whole natural class $A_S=\{s^2:s\in S\}$ with all prime factors of $S$ in $3\pmod 4$:

$A_S$ satisfies $(*)$ if and only if every $s\in S$ divides at most one larger element of $S$.
Thus the square-based problem becomes a purely multiplicative divisibility problem on $S$.

4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)

The main gap after Round 1 was this: the prime-square construction is rigorous but too sparse to address Q1, and Round 1 had no mechanism either to enlarge it substantially or to prove that the whole strategy must fail.

The Round-2 plan is therefore:

  1. strengthen the local modular obstruction from primes to exact prime-power divisibility;
  2. derive a necessary-and-sufficient criterion for all square families built from $3\pmod 4$ primes;
  3. use that criterion in two opposite directions:
    • construct a denser family than prime squares (semiprime-squares), and
    • prove that every construction in this entire square-based class still has cardinality $o(\sqrt N)$, so this line of attack cannot settle Q1 positively.

5) WORK (ROUND-2)

Notation. Let \[ \mathcal P_3:=\{p\text{ prime}: p\equiv 3\pmod 4\}. \] For a set $S\subseteq\mathbb N$, write \[ A_S:=\{s^2:s\in S\}. \]

Lemma 12.3 ($p$-adic valuation of a sum of two squares for $p\equiv 3\pmod 4$). Let $p\in\mathcal P_3$ and let $x,y\in\mathbb Z$. Then \[ \nu_p(x^2+y^2)=2\min\left(\nu_p(x),\nu_p(y)\right). \]

Proof. Set \[ t:=\min\left(\nu_p(x),\nu_p(y)\right). \] Write \[ x=p^t x_1,\qquad y=p^t y_1, \] where at least one of $x_1,y_1$ is not divisible by $p$. Then \[ x^2+y^2=p^{2t}(x_1^2+y_1^2). \] So it suffices to show that $p\nmid x_1^2+y_1^2$.

If exactly one of $x_1,y_1$ is divisible by $p$, then clearly $x_1^2+y_1^2\not\equiv 0\pmod p$. Thus the only remaining case is $p\nmid x_1y_1$ and \[ x_1^2+y_1^2\equiv 0\pmod p. \] Then \[ (x_1y_1^{-1})^2\equiv -1\pmod p, \] which is impossible because $p\equiv 3\pmod 4$ and hence $-1$ is not a quadratic residue modulo $p$. Therefore $p\nmid x_1^2+y_1^2$, and so \[ \nu_p(x^2+y^2)=2t=2\min\left(\nu_p(x),\nu_p(y)\right). \] $\square$

Corollary 12.4 (divisibility forced by square-divisibility). Let $u,v,w\in\mathbb N$, and assume every prime divisor of $u$ lies in $\mathcal P_3$. If \[ u^2\mid (v^2+w^2), \] then \[ u\mid v\qquad\text{and}\qquad u\mid w. \]

Proof. Write \[ u=\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{e_i} \] with each $p_i\equiv 3\pmod 4$. From $u^2\mid v^2+w^2$ we obtain \[ \nu_{p_i}(v^2+w^2)\ge 2e_i \qquad (1\le i\le r). \] By Lemma 12.3, \[ 2\min\left(\nu_{p_i}(v),\nu_{p_i}(w)\right)=\nu_{p_i}(v^2+w^2)\ge 2e_i, \] so \[ \min\left(\nu_{p_i}(v),\nu_{p_i}(w)\right)\ge e_i. \] Hence $p_i^{e_i}\mid v$ and $p_i^{e_i}\mid w$ for every $i$, therefore $u\mid v$ and $u\mid w$. $\square$

Theorem 12.5 (exact criterion for the $3\pmod 4$ square family). Let $S\subseteq\mathbb N$, and assume every prime divisor of every $s\in S$ lies in $\mathcal P_3$. Then the set \[ A_S=\{s^2:s\in S\} \] satisfies property $(*)$ if and only if every $s\in S$ divides at most one larger element of $S$.

Proof.

Necessity. Assume $A_S$ satisfies $(*)$. Suppose, toward contradiction, that some $s\in S$ divides two distinct larger elements $t,u\in S$ with $t>s$ and $u>s$. Then in $A_S$ the three elements \[ a=s^2,\qquad b=t^2,\qquad c=u^2 \] are distinct, satisfy $b>a$ and $c>a$, and also \[ a=s^2\mid t^2+u^2=b+c, \] contradicting $(*)$. Hence each $s\in S$ divides at most one larger element of $S$.

Sufficiency. Assume now that each $s\in S$ divides at most one larger element of $S$. Suppose, toward contradiction, that $A_S$ fails $(*)$. Then there exist distinct $a,b,c\in A_S$ with $b>a$, $c>a$, and \[ a\mid (b+c). \] Write \[ a=s^2,\qquad b=t^2,\qquad c=u^2 \] with $s,t,u\in S$. Since $b>a$ and $c>a$, we have $t>s$ and $u>s$. Also \[ s^2=a\mid (b+c)=t^2+u^2. \] By Corollary 12.4, this implies $s\mid t$ and $s\mid u$. Thus $s$ divides two distinct larger elements of $S$, contradicting the hypothesis on $S$. Therefore $A_S$ satisfies $(*)$. $\square$

Corollary 12.6 (primitive-basis corollary). Let $S\subseteq\mathbb N$ be primitive (that is, no element of $S$ divides another distinct element of $S$), and assume every prime divisor of every $s\in S$ is congruent to $3\pmod 4$. Then $A_S=\{s^2:s\in S\}$ satisfies $(*)$.

Proof. In a primitive set, no $s\in S$ divides any larger element of $S$, so certainly no $s\in S$ divides two larger elements. Apply Theorem 12.5. $\square$

Proposition 12.7 (a denser explicit construction than prime squares). Define \[ S_2:=\{pq: p<q,\ p,q\in\mathcal P_3\} \] and \[ A_2:=A_{S_2}=\{(pq)^2: p<q,\ p,q\in\mathcal P_3\}. \] Then $A_2$ satisfies $(*)$.

Proof. Every element of $S_2$ is squarefree with exactly two prime factors. Hence if $m,n\in S_2$ and $m\mid n$, then $m=n$ by unique factorization. So $S_2$ is primitive. Now apply Corollary 12.6. $\square$

Proposition 12.8 (quantitative size of the semiprime-square family). There exists $c>0$ such that for all sufficiently large $x$, \[ |S_2\cap[1,x]|\ge c\,\frac{x\log\log x}{\log x}. \] Consequently, there exists $c'>0$ such that for all sufficiently large $N$, \[ |A_2\cap[1,N]|\ge c'\,\frac{\sqrt N\,\log\log N}{\log N}. \]

Proof. Let \[ \pi_3(y):=|\{p\le y: p\in\mathcal P_3\}|. \] By the prime number theorem in the progression $3\pmod 4$, there exist constants $c_0,C_0>0$ and $y_0$ such that for all $y\ge y_0$, \[ c_0\frac{y}{\log y}\le \pi_3(y)\le C_0\frac{y}{\log y}. \]

Fix $x$ large. For every prime $p\in\mathcal P_3$ with $p\le x^{1/3}$ and every prime $q\in\mathcal P_3$ with \[ x^{1/3}<q\le \frac{x}{p}, \] we have $p<q$ and $pq\le x$, so $pq\in S_2\cap[1,x]$. Moreover, because $q>x^{1/3}\ge p$, each such semiprime is counted exactly once by its smaller prime $p$. Therefore \[ |S_2\cap[1,x]| \ge \sum_{\substack{p\le x^{1/3}\\ p\in\mathcal P_3}} \left(\pi_3(x/p)-\pi_3(x^{1/3})\right). \] For $p\le x^{1/3}$ we have $x/p\ge x^{2/3}$, so for large $x$, \[ \pi_3(x/p)\ge c_0\frac{x/p}{\log(x/p)}\ge c_0\frac{x}{p\log x}. \] Also, \[ \pi_3(x^{1/3})\le C_1\frac{x^{1/3}}{\log x} \] for some absolute $C_1$. Hence \[ |S_2\cap[1,x]| \ge c_0\frac{x}{\log x} \sum_{\substack{p\le x^{1/3}\\ p\in\mathcal P_3}}\frac1p - C_1\frac{x^{1/3}}{\log x}\,\pi_3(x^{1/3}). \] Using again $\pi_3(x^{1/3})\ll x^{1/3}/\log x$, the error term is \[ \ll \frac{x^{2/3}}{(\log x)^2}. \] So it remains to lower-bound the reciprocal prime sum. By partial summation, \[ \sum_{\substack{p\le y\\ p\in\mathcal P_3}}\frac1p = \frac{\pi_3(y)}{y}+ \int_{2}^{y}\frac{\pi_3(t)}{t^2}\,dt. \] Applying the lower bound $\pi_3(t)\ge c_0 t/\log t$ for $t\ge y_0$ gives, for large $y$, \[ \sum_{\substack{p\le y\\ p\in\mathcal P_3}}\frac1p \ge c_2\log\log y \] for some $c_2>0$. Taking $y=x^{1/3}$, we obtain \[ \sum_{\substack{p\le x^{1/3}\\ p\in\mathcal P_3}}\frac1p \ge c_3\log\log x \] for large $x$ (since $\log\log x^{1/3}=\log\log x+O(1)$). Substituting into the previous estimate yields \[ |S_2\cap[1,x]| \ge c\,\frac{x\log\log x}{\log x} \] for all sufficiently large $x$.

Finally, \[ |A_2\cap[1,N]|=|S_2\cap[1,\sqrt N]| \ge c'\,\frac{\sqrt N\,\log\log N}{\log N} \] for all sufficiently large $N$, after adjusting constants. $\square$

Proposition 12.9 (fatal obstruction to the Round-1 square strategy for Q1). Let \[ \mathcal T:=\{n\in\mathbb N: \text{every prime divisor of $n$ is congruent to }3\pmod 4\}. \] Then \[ |\mathcal T\cap[1,x]|=o(x). \] Consequently, for every set $S\subseteq\mathcal T$ one has \[ |A_S\cap[1,N]|=o(\sqrt N). \] In particular, no construction of the form $A_S=\{s^2:s\in S\}$ with all prime factors of $S$ lying in $3\pmod 4$ can answer Q1 positively.

Proof. Fix $Y\ge 3$, and define \[ M_Y:=2\prod_{\substack{p\le Y\\ p\equiv 1\, (4)}}p. \] Every $n\in\mathcal T$ is coprime to $M_Y$. Hence modulo $M_Y$, such an $n$ can lie only in residue classes that are nonzero modulo $2$ and nonzero modulo every prime $p\le Y$ with $p\equiv 1\pmod 4$. Therefore the number of admissible residue classes modulo $M_Y$ is exactly \[ R_Y:=\prod_{\substack{p\le Y\\ p\equiv 1\, (4)}}(p-1), \] since modulo $2$ there is just one admissible class, namely $1$. Thus, for every $x$, \[ |\mathcal T\cap[1,x]| \le R_Y\left(\frac{x}{M_Y}+1\right) = \frac{R_Y}{M_Y}x+R_Y. \] Now \[ \frac{R_Y}{M_Y} = \frac12\prod_{\substack{p\le Y\\ p\equiv 1\, (4)}}\left(1-\frac1p\right). \] By the prime number theorem in the progression $1\pmod 4$, the reciprocal sum \[ \sum_{\substack{p\equiv 1\, (4)}}\frac1p \] diverges. Hence \[ \prod_{\substack{p\le Y\\ p\equiv 1\, (4)}}\left(1-\frac1p\right)\longrightarrow 0 \qquad (Y\to\infty). \] So given $\varepsilon>0$, we may choose $Y$ so large that \[ \frac{R_Y}{M_Y}<\varepsilon. \] Then for this fixed $Y$, \[ \frac{|\mathcal T\cap[1,x]|}{x} \le \varepsilon+\frac{R_Y}{x}. \] Letting $x\to\infty$ gives \[ \limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{|\mathcal T\cap[1,x]|}{x}\le \varepsilon. \] Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $|\mathcal T\cap[1,x]|=o(x)$.

Now if $S\subseteq\mathcal T$, then \[ |A_S\cap[1,N]|=|\{s\in S:s^2\le N\}|=|S\cap[1,\sqrt N]|\le |\mathcal T\cap[1,\sqrt N]|=o(\sqrt N). \] This proves the obstruction claim. $\square$

6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION

I now check the new work against the main failure modes.

  • Prime-power issue. The Round-1 argument only worked modulo $p$ for prime squares. Lemma 12.3 fixes the exact missing point: it controls prime powers $p^{2e}$, so Corollary 12.4 applies to arbitrary $u$ whose prime factors all lie in $3\pmod 4$, not just to primes.

  • Necessity versus sufficiency in Theorem 12.5. Necessity is immediate from the residue-$0$ case of Round-1 Lemma 12.1: if $s$ divides two larger elements $t,u$, then $s^2\mid t^2+u^2$. The nontrivial direction is sufficiency; this is exactly what Corollary 12.4 supplies. So the criterion is genuinely biconditional.

  • Distinctness of the semiprimes. In Proposition 12.7, $S_2$ is primitive because every element is squarefree with exactly two prime factors. Thus divisibility between distinct elements is impossible by unique factorization; there is no hidden repeated-prime pathology.

  • Double counting in Proposition 12.8. The lower bound counts semiprimes $pq$ by the smaller prime $p$ under the extra restriction $q>x^{1/3}$. Since $p\le x^{1/3}<q$, each semiprime is counted exactly once. So the lower bound is rigorous.

  • Scope of the obstruction in Proposition 12.9. The conclusion $|A_S\cap[1,N]|=o(\sqrt N)$ is proved only for the restricted class $A_S=\{s^2:s\in S\}$ with all prime factors of $S$ in $3\pmod 4$. This is not a disproof of Q1. It is a disproof only of the natural Round-1 strategy class generated by the prime-square example.

  • Interaction with Round-1 results. There is no contradiction with Lemma 12.2. Prime squares correspond to the special case $S=\mathcal P_3$, where indeed each element divides no larger element. Thus Theorem 12.5 strictly extends Round 1 rather than replacing it.

7) FINAL

UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED)

The global questions Q1--Q3 remain open in this round, but the investigation has advanced in two mathematically substantive ways beyond Round 1.

  1. Exact structural reduction of the whole Round-1 square strategy. For sets of the form $A_S=\{s^2:s\in S\}$ with all prime factors of $S$ in $3\pmod 4$, property $(*)$ is equivalent to the purely multiplicative condition that each $s\in S$ divides at most one larger element of $S$ (Theorem 12.5). This is a complete characterization inside that natural class.

  2. A denser explicit family and a barrier. The semiprime-square family \[ A_2=\{(pq)^2: p<q,\ p\equiv q\equiv 3\pmod 4\} \] satisfies $(*)$ and obeys \[ |A_2\cap[1,N]|\gg \frac{\sqrt N\,\log\log N}{\log N}, \] which is a strict improvement on the prime-square example from Round 1. At the same time, Proposition 12.9 proves that every construction in this entire $3\pmod 4$ square class has size $o(\sqrt N)$, so this strategy can never yield a positive answer to Q1.

The sharp remaining gap is therefore clearer than after Round 1: any eventual positive answer to Q1 must come from a construction outside the whole natural class \[ A=\{s^2:s\in S,\ \text{all prime factors of $s$ are }3\pmod 4\}, \] and any negative answer must rule out such completely different constructions.
