Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$. Specialized notation and index ranges follow the cited source and the definitions below.

A splittable flow may route one demand along several paths; an unsplittable flow routes its entire demand on one path. $d_{\max}=\max_i d_i$, $x_e,y_e$ are edge loads, and $c(x)=\sum_e c_e x_e$.

Verbatim supplied catalogue question

Given a single-source network with capacities $u$, costs $c$, source $s$, sinks $t_i$, and demands $d_i$ satisfying the no-bottleneck condition $d_{\max}\leq u_e$, does every splittable flow $x$ satisfying the demands admit an unsplittable flow $y$ satisfying the same demands such that $y_e\leq x_e+d_{\max}$ for every edge $e$ and $c(y)\leq c(x)$?

Known result and scope

The answer to the recorded universal assertion is negative. The preserved definition already identifies the July 2026 counterexample. The primary AFP entry credits the finite example to Dmitry Rybin and its Isabelle formalization to Arthur Freitas Ramos, David Barros Hulak, and Ruy Jose Guerra Barretto de Queiroz. We reproduce a self-contained elementary proof below, with capacities enlarged to satisfy the catalogue's explicit no-bottleneck hypothesis. This is a rederivation of a published result, not a newly solved open problem. The external Isabelle development was read; Isabelle was not run in this task.

Explicit instance and complete proof

Take vertices $s,u,v,w,t_1,t_2,t_3$ and exactly the following nine directed arcs. The rows give cost per unit, the fractional load to be constructed, and our capacity, respectively: \[ \begin{array}{c|rrrrrrrrr} e&st_1&st_2&su&uv&ut_3&vt_1&vw&wt_2&wt_3\\\hline c_e&2&3&0&0&2&0&0&0&0\\ x_e&10&6&24&14&10&5&9&4&5\\ u_e&15&15&24&15&15&15&15&15&15 \end{array} \] The demands at $t_1,t_2,t_3$ are $(15,10,15)$, so $d_{\max}=15$. This graph is acyclic; all arcs go forward through the displayed junction chain or into terminal sinks. Each terminal has exactly two source paths: \[ \begin{array}{c|l|l|c|c} i& P_i\text{ (paid)}&Q_i\text{ (free)}&\text{load on }P_i&\text{load on }Q_i\\\hline 1&s,t_1&s,u,v,t_1&10&5\\ 2&s,t_2&s,u,v,w,t_2&6&4\\ 3&s,u,t_3&s,u,v,w,t_3&10&5 \end{array} \] To check completeness, a path to $t_1$ must enter either from $s$ or $v$; reaching $v$ requires $s,u,v$. A path to $t_2$ enters from $s$ or $w$; reaching $w$ requires $s,u,v,w$. A path to $t_3$ enters from $u$ or $w$, with those same uniquely determined prefixes. Terminal vertices have no outgoing edges, so no other route or detour is possible.

The path amounts in each row sum to its demand. Summing the amounts through arcs gives the stated vector $x$: in particular $x_{su}=5+4+10+5=24$, $x_{uv}=5+4+5=14$, and $x_{vw}=4+5=9$; the remaining six arcs have their individual route amounts. All loads are nonnegative and at most the listed capacities. Also every capacity is at least 15, verifying the no-bottleneck condition on every edge, including the edges carrying small fractional loads. The cost is \[ c(x)=2\cdot10+3\cdot6+2\cdot10=58. \]

Any unsplittable routing chooses either $P_i$ or $Q_i$ for each $i$. Routing a full demand on any $P_i$ costs exactly 30: the three products are $15\cdot2$, $10\cdot3$, and $15\cdot2$. Each $Q_i$ costs zero. Suppose a routing satisfies $y_e\le x_e+15$ on all arcs. It cannot choose $Q_1,Q_2$ simultaneously: those two demands together with demand 3 (whose two routes both use $su$) give $y_{su}=15+10+15=40>24+15=39$. It cannot choose $Q_1,Q_3$ simultaneously, because then $y_{uv}\ge15+15=30>14+15=29$. It cannot choose $Q_2,Q_3$ simultaneously, because then $y_{vw}=10+15=25>9+15=24$. Thus at most one of the three demands can use a free route, and at least two must use paid routes. Every routing satisfying all the additive bounds therefore has \[ c(y)\ge60>58=c(x). \] This contradicts cost preservation and proves that the requested $y$ does not exist. The proof covers non-strict congestion inequalities exactly as printed, and would also rule out a stricter inequality or an additional requirement $y\le u$.

Why the capacity adjustment preserves the counterexample.
The AFP's displayed capacities equal its fractional loads, some below 15. Our capacities are $u_e=\max\{x_e,15\}$. Changing them introduces no new arc or path, leaves the feasible fractional flow and its cost unchanged, and meets $d_{\max}\le u_e$. The required target bound still uses the original $x_e+15$, not $u_e+15$. Consequently the same three strict violations above apply. Replacing the target by $u_e+15$ at this step would be an invalid weakening; no such replacement is made.

Attempted rounding route and adversarial audit

Independently choosing the two paths according to the displayed fractional proportions preserves expected edge loads and expected cost 58, but may violate the additive bounds. The complete inequality above proves a stronger obstruction: no probability distribution supported entirely on bound-feasible unsplittable routings can have expected cost 58, since every such routing costs at least 60. Thus trying to preserve those expectations by a correlated rounding supported only on admissible routings cannot work on this instance. This explains exactly where an expectation-based proof of the old conjecture must fail, rather than merely reporting an unsuccessful rounding experiment.

An exact Python checker reconstructs every source-to-terminal path directly from the nine-arc graph, verifies that there are exactly two per terminal, computes all fractional loads and the enlarged capacities, and enumerates all $2^3=8$ unsplittable routings. Their minimum cost under the additive bounds is 60. The saved program and JSON use integer arithmetic only. The root Codex GPT-6 agent independently checked the same primary graph, the three inequalities, the capacity adjustment, and a separate DFS/enumeration program.
