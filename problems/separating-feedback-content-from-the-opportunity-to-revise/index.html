Attempt: more feedback as more useful information

The source asks how feedback improves learning without choosing a treatment or outcome. I tested a restricted binary-decision model. A latent answer $H$ is equally likely to be 0 or 1, and a feedback bit $S$ equals $H$ with probability $q$, with symmetric conditional errors. Loss is one for an incorrect subsequent answer and zero otherwise. This measures immediate decision accuracy, not transfer or motivation.
Proposition: Blindly following the feedback has error $1-q$ and can be worse than using no feedback. A learner who knows the channel can attain error $\min(q,1-q)\le1/2$; allowing optional use of additional information cannot worsen the optimal risk.
Proof: Following $S$ fails exactly when $S\ne H$, with probability $1-q$. Without information every constant or independent randomized answer has error $1/2$ under the balanced prior. If $q<1/2$, blind following is worse. By symmetry and Bayes' rule, conditional on either signal, the probability that its label is correct is $q$. The optimal response therefore follows it when $q\ge1/2$ and flips it when $q<1/2$, giving error $\min(q,1-q)$. A randomized mixture cannot improve on the smaller of the two conditional losses, proving optimality. More generally, the set of policies with access to a signal includes every policy that ignores it; minimizing over the larger policy set cannot increase risk. ∎

Verification and remaining gaps

At $q=1/4$, blind following has error $3/4$, the no-feedback baseline $1/2$, and calibrated use $1/4$. Thus the mathematical value of optional information does not imply that actual feedback recipients interpret or use it optimally. This is the exact unsupported step in translating "more information" into a learning-improvement claim. The channel quality, learner policy, later transfer, revision opportunities and empirical comparison remain unspecified. No pedagogical efficacy theorem is asserted.
