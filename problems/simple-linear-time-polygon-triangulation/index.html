Simple Linear-Time Polygon Triangulation

Status Open · unsolvedmath.comLLM status Open unverified

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
$\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, and $\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$ denote the integers, rationals, reals and complex numbers. Any other conventions are specified locally.

A simple polygon has a non-self-intersecting polygonal boundary with $n$ vertices. A triangulation partitions its interior into triangles by noncrossing diagonals, using its vertices. Linear time means $O(n)$ running time. Simplicity of the algorithm is the qualitative criterion used in the source.

Statement recorded in the supplied source.
Find a deterministic linear-time triangulation algorithm for arbitrary simple polygons that is significantly simpler than Chazelle's. The related question recorded with it asks for a simple linear-time algorithm computing a shortest path within a simple polygon without first using a more complicated triangulation algorithm. [S2]

Research attempt: Auditing fan triangulation and ear-scan costs

The first attempted linear algorithm was to choose one boundary vertex and draw a fan. For a convex polygon $v_0,\ldots,v_{n-1}$ this works: each diagonal $v_0v_i$ lies in the polygon by convexity, and the triangles $(v_0,v_i,v_{i+1})$, $1\le i\le n-2$, have disjoint interiors and exhaust it. One proof is to intersect every ray from $v_0$ with the convex polygon: its intersection is an interval, whose outer endpoint lies on exactly one opposite edge except at finitely many directions. Thus construction and explicit output cost $O(n)$ arithmetic operations. For two points of a convex polygon, their straight segment is also a shortest internal path: every rectifiable competitor has length at least the Euclidean distance, by applying the triangle inequality to its polygonal approximations.

The fan inference fails on this explicit simple polygon, in boundary order: \[ (0,0),(4,0),(4,4),(3,4),(3,1),(1,1),(1,4),(0,4). \] Its interior is the outer rectangle with the upper open rectangular notch $1<x<3$, $1<y<4$ removed. The proposed diagonal from $(0,0)$ to $(4,4)$ passes through $(2,2)$, outside the polygon. Its boundary edges have disjoint relative interiors, so this is a valid simple-polygon counterexample to the fan algorithm, rather than a self-intersecting input. A shortest-path shortcut through the notch likewise fails the domain constraint.

The next attempt was ear removal with an explicit emptiness scan: when $m$ vertices remain, select a convex candidate, test all other $m-3$ vertices for membership in its triangle, and remove it if empty. On a strictly convex polygon every such candidate succeeds, but the specified verifier still makes exactly \[ \sum_{m=4}^n(m-3)=\frac{(n-3)(n-2)}2 \] point-in-triangle tests. The input family can be the vertices of any strictly convex $n$-gon. Thus constant amortized time per removed ear has not been proved: even a sequence of successful ears can require quadratic verification under the chosen implementation. This is a running-time counterexample to that concrete implementation, not a lower bound for ear methods with more careful maintenance.

The two failures locate the remaining operation: cheaply certifying that a candidate diagonal is internal in arbitrary nonconvex geometry. Neither the convex fan proof nor the successful-ear count pays for that operation. A replacement visibility structure would require its own preprocessing and update analysis before an $O(n)$ total bound could be asserted.

Verification and adversarial audit

The notch example was checked by its coordinate intervals and by the point $(2,2)$ on the proposed diagonal. The exact quadratic sum was evaluated symbolically. Triangles ($n=3$) give zero ear-removal tests, consistently. Collinear boundary vertices and shortest paths in nonconvex polygons are not claimed handled by the convex special case.
