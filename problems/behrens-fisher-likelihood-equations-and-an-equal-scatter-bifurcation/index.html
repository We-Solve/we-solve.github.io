Verbatim supplied catalogue question

Problem 24 (Behrens-Fischer Problem). Solve the maximum likelihood equations for the mixture of two multivariate Gaussians with the same mean and different covariance matrices.

Retaining the mixture formulation

The source calls its problem Behrens--Fischer but explicitly says mixture of two multivariate Gaussians. A labelled two-sample common-mean model has a different likelihood. This attempt uses the literal latent-mixture density specified in the imported definition, for observations $y_1,\ldots,y_n\in\mathbb R^d$, $n,d\ge1$: \[ f(y)=w\varphi_{\mu,\Sigma_1}(y)+(1-w)\varphi_{\mu,\Sigma_2}(y), \quad 0<w<1,\quad\Sigma_1,\Sigma_2\succ0. \] The target asks to solve likelihood equations, so an obstruction to a finite global maximum alone cannot count as a full solution.
Unbounded likelihood even with a common mean.
Fix $w=1/2$, $\mu=y_1$, $\Sigma_2=I_d$, and $\Sigma_1=\varepsilon^2 I_d$ for $0<\varepsilon<1$. The covariances are positive definite and distinct. At $y_1$ the first component contributes $\tfrac12(2\pi)^{-d/2}\varepsilon^{-d}$. At every other observation the second component supplies the strictly positive lower bound $\tfrac12(2\pi)^{-d/2}\exp(-\|y_i-y_1\|^2/2)$, independent of $\varepsilon$. Multiplying the bounds gives \[ \prod_{i=1}^n f(y_i)\ \ge C\varepsilon^{-d}\longrightarrow\infty \] for a constant $C>0$ determined by the data. For $n=1$ the empty product in $C$ is one. Every fixed admissible parameter has a finite likelihood, so no finite global maximizer exists in this unconstrained model. Common means do not prevent the usual component-collapse route. This proof does not say that stationary points cannot exist.
Interior stationary equations derived explicitly.
Let $r_i=y_i-\mu$ and define the responsibilities \[ \tau_i=\frac{w\varphi_{\mu,\Sigma_1}(y_i)}{f(y_i)},\quad N_1=\sum_i\tau_i,\quad N_2=n-N_1. \] They satisfy $0<\tau_i<1$ and $N_1,N_2>0$. Differentiating a log sum shows that the derivative with respect to a component's parameters is weighted by its responsibility. For a Gaussian, differentiating $-\tfrac12\log\det\Sigma-\tfrac12r^T\Sigma^{-1}r$ gives mean score $\Sigma^{-1}r$ and covariance differential $\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}[(\Sigma^{-1}rr^T\Sigma^{-1} -\Sigma^{-1})\,d\Sigma]$. The inverse identity follows by differentiating $\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}=I$, and the log determinant identity follows from $\det(I+tB)=1+t\operatorname{tr}B+O(t^2)$, whose linear terms in the determinant expansion are its diagonal entries. Thus every interior stationary point obeys \[ w=N_1/n,\qquad \Sigma_1=N_1^{-1}\sum_i\tau_i r_i r_i^T,\qquad \Sigma_2=N_2^{-1}\sum_i(1-\tau_i)r_i r_i^T, \] \[ (N_1\Sigma_1^{-1}+N_2\Sigma_2^{-1})\mu =\Sigma_1^{-1}\sum_i\tau_i y_i +\Sigma_2^{-1}\sum_i(1-\tau_i)y_i. \] For the first equation the weight derivative is $\sum_i[\tau_i/w-(1-\tau_i)/(1-w)]=0$. For the covariance equations multiply the zero covariance score on both sides by the positive-definite covariance matrix. The mean equation is the rearranged sum of the two weighted mean scores. Conversely these equations, together with the definition of $\tau_i$ and the open-domain conditions, make every first derivative zero by the same formulas. They are therefore equivalent to interior stationarity, but remain implicit because $\tau_i$ depends on all unknowns through Gaussian exponentials.
A check of a tempting symmetry reduction.
For scalar data consisting of $-a,+a$ with $a>0$, impose a candidate common mean $\mu=0$. Both squared residuals are then $a^2$. The two covariance equations force $\Sigma_1=\Sigma_2=a^2$ at any interior stationary point with that mean, independently of the responsibilities. Hence an assumed symmetric mean does not produce a stationary solution with distinct variances in this example. The equal-covariance point may be considered if coinciding components are allowed, but it is excluded by a strict distinct-covariance reading. Neither this check nor iterating the implicit equations classifies all other stationary means.
