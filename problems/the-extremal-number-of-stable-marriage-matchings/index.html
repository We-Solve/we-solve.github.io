The extremal number of stable-marriage matchings

LLM status Open unverified

C78 — Bargaining Theory - Matching Theory Economics catalogue openaigpt 6 astra pro 12% cataloguednot recordedOpen

Statement

MM-12 |  Matching markets and allocation

Published question

Definitions and assumptions

A complete strict stable-marriage instance $I$ has disjoint sets $U,W$ of size $n$, and every agent strictly ranks every member of the other set. A matching is a bijection between the two sides. It is stable if no unmatched pair strictly prefers one another to their respective partners. Let $\mathcal S(I)$ be the stable matchings and define \[ x_n=\max\{|\mathcal S(I)|:I\text{ is a complete strict instance of size }n\}, \qquad \gamma=\limsup_{n\to\infty}x_n^{1/n}. \] The maximum exists because only finitely many preference profiles have a given size; matchings are counted as distinct sets of pairs, without quotienting by relabeling.

Formal question

Determine $x_n$ exactly or obtain sharp asymptotic bounds. In particular, determine the optimal exponential base $\gamma$ and the finer multiplicative growth of $x_n$. Matching lower and upper exponential bounds would settle the growth-rate version even without a closed formula for each finite $n$.

Scope and status

The 2019 survey states the extremal counting problem explicitly. Its numerical upper-bound discussion is historical: Palmer and Pálvölgyi subsequently proved $x_n=O(3.55^n)$ in FOCS 2021. That bound does not determine the exact exponential base. The limsup is an unambiguous formulation; indeed independent blocks whose agents rank their own block first give $x_{n+m}\ge x_nx_m$, so superadditivity of $\log x_n$, together with an exponential upper bound, implies that the displayed limsup is a limit. This is a worst-case extremal problem, distinct from the expected count for random preferences or complexity of counting one instance's stable matchings.

WORK

Lemma (Existence and elementary bounds): For every $n\ge0$, $1\le x_n\le n!$.
Proof: For $n=0$ use the declared convention. For $n\ge1$, repeatedly let an unmatched man propose to his most-preferred woman not yet proposed to. A woman keeps her preferred proposal, rejecting any other. There are at most $n^2$ proposals. A man cannot be rejected by all women while some woman has never received a proposal: every rejection leaves that woman holding another man, which would require $n$ other held men. Thus the process ends with a perfect matching. If a man prefers a woman to his terminal partner, he proposed to her earlier; she rejected him or subsequently traded up, and therefore prefers her terminal partner to him. No such pair blocks. Finally there are only $n!$ bijections. ∎
Lemma (Exact block multiplication): If $I$ and $J$ have sizes $n$ and $m$, form a combined instance in which every agent ranks all members of the opposite side of its own block before all outsiders, retaining the original within-block rankings. Then \[ |\mathcal S(I\oplus J)|=|\mathcal S(I)|\,|\mathcal S(J)|. \] Consequently $x_{n+m}\ge x_nx_m$.
Proof: If a man in a balanced block is matched outside it, counting internal matching edges shows that a woman of that block is also matched outside it. That man and woman prefer one another to their outside partners, and block. Hence every stable matching stays inside each block. Its restrictions must be stable, because an internal blocking pair would remain blocking. Conversely the union of two internal stable matchings has no internal blocking pair, and an agent never prefers an outsider to an internal partner. This proves a bijection between stable matchings and pairs of internal stable matchings. Maximizing gives the inequality. ∎
Theorem (The limiting base exists): The limsup in the input is a limit and \[ \gamma=\sup_{k\ge1}x_k^{1/k},\qquad x_n\le\gamma^n\quad(n\ge1). \] Using the imported exponential upper bound, $\gamma\le3.55$ and even $x_n\le3.55^n$ for every $n$.
Proof: Put $a_n=\log x_n$, $a_0=0$. Fix $k$ and write $n=qk+r$, $0\le r<k$. Lemma Lemma (Exact block multiplication) gives $a_n\ge q a_k+a_r\ge q a_k$. Therefore $\liminf a_n/n\ge a_k/k$. Taking the supremum over $k$, while $a_n/n\le\sup_k a_k/k$ for each $n$, proves equality and existence of the limit, possibly infinite before using an upper bound. The imported bound makes the limit at most $\log3.55$. For an alternative direct removal of its additive constant, fix $n$ and apply $x_n^r\le x_{rn}\le3.55^{rn}+C$. Taking $r$th roots and letting $r\to\infty$ proves $x_n\le3.55^n$. ∎
Lemma (Orientation on one alternating cycle): On every nontrivial alternating cycle of two stable matchings, all men on that cycle prefer the same one of the two matchings.
Proof: Let the two matchings be $A,B$, and suppose man $i$ prefers $A(i)$ to $B(i)$. In $B$, woman $A(i)$ is matched to another man $j$. Stability of $B$ forces that woman to prefer $j$ to $i$. Stability of $A$ then forces $j$ to prefer $A(j)$ to $B(j)=A(i)$; otherwise $(j,A(i))$ blocks $A$. Repeat this implication around the alternating cycle. If the initial preference is for $B$, interchange the names of the matchings. ∎
Theorem (Exact first three values): $x_1=1$, $x_2=2$, and $x_3=3$.
Proof: The first equality follows by inspection. For $n=2$, let the men rank $1\succ2$ and $2\succ1$, and the women rank $2\succ1$ and $1\succ2$. Both bijections are stable: in the men's favorite matching no man wants to deviate, and in the other matching no woman wants to deviate. The upper bound $2!$ is exact.

For $n=3$, any two distinct matchings have just one nontrivial alternating cycle. Lemma Lemma (Orientation on one alternating cycle) therefore makes the stable matchings totally ordered by every man's weak preference. Suppose four stable matchings occurred in this order. At least two men change partners between successive matchings. No man can change more than twice along this order, since each change is a strict deterioration among three distinct partners. There are at least six and at most six changes in total. Each step is therefore a transposition, and each man participates exactly twice. The three transpositions must be the three edges of a triangle. Relabeling the men, their order can be written $AB,BC,AC$; the man $A$ returns to his original partner after these swaps. This contradicts strict deterioration. Thus there are at most three stable matchings.

Here is a witness attaining three, with ranks listed from best to worst: \[ \begin{array}{c|c@{\qquad}c|c} \text{man}&\text{women}&\text{woman}&\text{men}\\\hline 1&1,2,3&1&3,1,2\\ 2&1,2,3&2&1,3,2\\ 3&3,2,1&3&1,2,3 \end{array} \] The partner vectors are $(1,2,3)$, $(1,3,2)$, and $(2,3,1)$. In the first, the only woman a man prefers to his partner is woman 1 for man 2, and she prefers her current man 1. In the second, the potential blocking pairs from men's preferences are $(2,1),(2,2),(3,3)$; those women respectively prefer their current men $1,3,2$. In the third they are $(1,1),(2,1),(2,2),(3,2),(3,3)$; the women respectively prefer their current men $3,3,1,1,2$. Hence all three are stable. ∎

Corollary: For $n=3q+r$, $0\le r<3$, $x_n\ge3^q x_r$. In particular $\gamma\ge3^{1/3}$.
Proof: Use $q$ copies of the three-agent witness and the size-$r$ witness in Lemma Lemma (Exact block multiplication), and then take roots. ∎
Proposition (A prefactor does not follow from supermultiplicativity): Positive integer supermultiplicative sequences can share an exponential base and have different polynomial prefactors.
Proof: Take $a_n=4^n$ and $b_n=\lfloor4^n/(n+1)\rfloor$, with both zeroth terms equal to one. Because $(n+1)(m+1)\ge n+m+1$, the integer $b_nb_m$ is at most $4^{n+m}/(n+m+1)$, hence at most $b_{n+m}$. Both root limits are 4, whereas $b_n/a_n\sim1/(n+1)$. This is not a pair of stable-marriage constructions; it proves only that the abstract inequalities obtained above do not force a particular prefactor. ∎

Write $s(I)$ for the number of stable matchings of the preference profile $I$ and \[ x_n=\max_I s(I),\qquad \gamma=\limsup_{n\to\infty}x_n^{1/n}. \] The task is to determine $x_n$, or sharp asymptotics including $\gamma$ and, ideally, subexponential factors. It is an extremal determination problem, not the assertion that some given formula is true. Merely displaying an instance with few or many matchings does not disprove the question.

We set $x_0=1$ for block arguments; this convention does not alter the input. Preferences are complete, ties are forbidden, and labeled matchings are counted without identifying isomorphic objects. Important stress points are cross-block matches, strictness of preferences, and the distinction between a limit and a limsup.

4) WORK

Lemma (Existence and exact block factorization): Every complete strict instance has a stable matching. If two instances are placed in disjoint blocks and every agent ranks all members of its own block ahead of all outsiders, then the stable matchings of the combined instance are exactly the unions of one stable matching from each block. Consequently \[ x_{n+m}\geq x_nx_m\qquad(n,m\geq0). \]
Proof: For existence, let unmatched men propose in preference order and let each woman retain her preferred proposal so far. There are at most $n^2$ proposals. An unmatched man cannot exhaust his list: otherwise every woman has received a proposal and retains a man, which would leave no man unmatched. Thus the procedure ends with a perfect matching. A man who prefers another woman to his final partner proposed to her earlier; she rejected him, or later replaced him, and her retained partner can only improve. Hence that pair cannot block.

For factorization, suppose a perfect matching uses an external partner for some man in a block of size $n$. The number of internally matched men equals the number of internally matched women. Therefore a woman in the same block also has an external partner. These two agents prefer one another to their external partners, so they block. Thus any stable matching is internal to every block. Its restriction to a block must be stable, or an internal blocking pair would block the whole matching. Conversely, a union of stable block matchings has no internal blocking pair, and neither member of a cross-block pair prefers the outsider to its own internal partner. Multiplying the numbers of choices proves the assertion. ∎

This lemma supplies a lower-bound construction and the only supermultiplicativity used below; it does not assert that extremizers themselves decompose into blocks.

Theorem (The growth limit exists): Using the exponential upper bound stated in the literature check above, \[ \lim_{n\to\infty}x_n^{1/n}=\sup_{k\geq1}x_k^{1/k}=\gamma<\infty, \qquad x_n\leq\gamma^n\quad(n\geq1). \]
Proof: Fix $k\geq1$ and write $n=qk+r$, where $0\leq r<k$. The previous lemma and $x_r\geq1$ give $x_n\geq x_k^q$. Since $q/n\to1/k$, $\liminf_n x_n^{1/n}\geq x_k^{1/k}$. Taking the supremum over $k$ proves that the liminf is at least the supremum of the entire sequence. The reverse inequality for the limsup follows from the definition of a supremum. The cited exponential upper bound makes this supremum finite. Each term is bounded by the supremum, which proves the last inequality. ∎
Lemma (Preference orientation on an alternating cycle): Let $M,N$ be two stable matchings. On any single alternating cycle in their symmetric difference, all men prefer $M$ to $N$, or all prefer $N$ to $M$.
Proof: Index the cycle so that $M(u_i)=w_i$ and $N(u_i)=w_{i+1}$, cyclically. Suppose $u_i$ prefers $w_i$ to $w_{i+1}$. Stability of $N$ forces $w_i$ to prefer $u_{i-1}$ to $u_i$. Stability of $M$ then forces $u_{i-1}$ to prefer $w_{i-1}$ to $w_i$. Iterating this implication around the cycle makes every man prefer $M$. If the first man instead prefers $N$, interchange the two matchings. Strict preferences ensure that these are the only cases. ∎
Theorem (Exact small values, with a human-checkable upper bound): \[ x_1=1,\qquad x_2=2,\qquad x_3=3. \]
Proof: The first equality follows from there being only one bijection. For $n=2$, the upper bound is $2!=2$. The instance \[ u_1:w_1\succ w_2,\quad u_2:w_2\succ w_1,\qquad w_1:u_2\succ u_1,\quad w_2:u_1\succ u_2 \] has both bijections stable: in the identity matching the men have their first choices, and in the other matching the women have their first choices.

For $n=3$, any two different permutations differ on a single alternating cycle, of length two or three. The preceding lemma therefore makes any two stable matchings comparable in every man's preference order. These comparisons form a linear order: transitivity follows from the transitivity of each individual preference order.

Suppose four stable matchings existed. Order four of them as $M^0\succ M^1\succ M^2\succ M^3$, from the men's perspective. Every consecutive change moves at least two men, so there are at least six partner changes. Each of the three men can change partners at most twice while strictly worsening among three women. There are consequently exactly six changes: each step moves exactly two men, and every man moves exactly twice. Each step is a transposition of partners, so the permutation taking $M^0$ to $M^3$ is odd. On the other hand, each man has visited three distinct partners, so this permutation has no fixed point. A fixed-point-free permutation of three objects is a three-cycle and is even. This contradiction proves $x_3\leq3$.

For the matching lower bound, use the lists \[ \begin{array}{c|c@{\qquad}c|c} u_1&w_1\succ w_2\succ w_3&w_1&u_2\succ u_3\succ u_1\\ u_2&w_2\succ w_3\succ w_1&w_2&u_3\succ u_1\succ u_2\\ u_3&w_3\succ w_1\succ w_2&w_3&u_1\succ u_2\succ u_3 \end{array} \] and the matchings $(w_1,w_2,w_3)$, $(w_2,w_3,w_1)$, and $(w_3,w_1,w_2)$, where coordinates list partners of $u_1,u_2,u_3$. The first gives all men their first choices and the last gives all women their first choices. In the middle matching, a man's only preferred woman is his first choice. That woman prefers her middle-matching partner to him, as is verified in each of the three displayed lists. Hence all three matchings are stable. ∎

In particular, independent three-agent blocks give $x_{3q+r}\geq3^q x_r$ for $r=0,1,2$. This elementary lower bound is weaker than the literature lower bound; it is included because its proof is complete here.

Exact first gap. There is no matching universal upper and constructive lower exponential bound determining $\gamma$ in this work.

Next three moves.

  1. Derive a restriction on alternating-cycle orientations that survives when several cycles coexist, rather than assuming the three-agent linear order.
  2. Search for interacting blocks whose stable-count-per-agent exceeds that of their components, keeping complete preference-list certificates.
  3. Relate those restrictions to a sharper count of admissible rotation-poset downsets; an arbitrary-poset bound would need its representation hypotheses checked.
Counterexample perspective. There is no specified numerical conjecture to falsify. A construction beating a proposed exponential upper bound would need an infinite amplified family, not just a mislabeled small-instance maximum.

Reproducibility and scope.
This is one of eleven companion reports. The bundle includes verify.py and its executed results. Finite experiments supplement the proofs; they are not proofs of asymptotic claims. Literature coverage is targeted, and no novelty or formal proof-assistant certification is claimed.
