Statement

Fix an integer $n\ge 1$.

A feasible sequence for (n) is a finite strictly increasing sequence of integers \[ 1\le a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_k\le n \] such that all consecutive (interval) sums are distinct, i.e. for every two different index pairs $(u,v)\neq(u',v')$ with $1\le u\le v\le k$ and $1\le u'\le v'\le k$, \[ \sum_{i=u}^{v} a_i ;\neq; \sum_{i=u'}^{v'} a_i. \]

Define \[ f(n)=\max{k:\ \exists\ \text{a feasible sequence }(a_1,\dots,a_k)\text{ for }n}. \]

The Erdős–Harzheim problem (as stated on the site) asks for the asymptotic growth of (f(n)) as $n\to\infty$, in particular:

* Does $f(n)=o(n)$ (equivalently $f(n)/n\to 0$)?

(There is also a variant (g(n)) where one drops the monotonicity assumption, but I treat it only as context.)

Stress points / edge cases:

* For $k=1$ the condition is vacuous; so $f(n)\ge 1$ always. * For a given (k), there are $\binom{k+1}{2}=k(k+1)/2$ consecutive sums to be all distinct. * The condition is conveniently rephrased via prefix sums (see Lemma 1 below).

Restatement from Math-779-A2 r1, with the source manuscript’s qualifications.

Erdős Problem #357: distinct consecutive sums

1. Statement and scope

Let $f(n)$ be the largest $k$ for which some $1\le a_1<\cdots<a_k\le n$ has all sums $\sum_{i=u}^v a_i$, $1\le u\le v\le k$, distinct. How does $f(n)$ grow, and is $f(n)=o(n)$? Related questions ask whether an infinite such sequence has density zero and whether its reciprocal sum converges. We prove explicit lower and upper bounds and an infinite-sequence growth obstruction. The sublinear upper bound and reciprocal-sum questions remain unresolved.

2. An explicit lower bound of order $2\sqrt n$

Put $m=\lfloor\sqrt n\rfloor$, $k=2m-1$, and take the last $k$ integers up to $n$, namely $a_i=n-k+i$. These are positive since $m^2\ge2m-1$. Sums of a fixed length $r$ strictly increase with the starting index. Their minimum and maximum are \[ L_r=r(n-k)+r(r+1)/2,\qquad U_r=rn-r(r-1)/2. \] For $1\le r<k$, \[ L_{r+1}-U_r =n-(r+1)(k-1)+r^2 =(r-m+1)^2+n-m^2+1\ge1. \] Thus the ranges of different lengths are disjoint, proving \[ f(n)\ge2\lfloor\sqrt n\rfloor-1. \] This is the familiar interval construction also checked in the earlier GPT Pro 5.2 attempt.

3. An elementary upper bound with controlled error

Write $S=\sum_{i=1}^k a_i$. For $1\le h\le k$, there are \[ M=\sum_{r=1}^h(k-r+1)=hk-h(h-1)/2 \] short consecutive sums of lengths at most $h$. They are distinct positive integers, so their total is at least $M(M+1)/2$. Each $a_i$ occurs in at most $r$ sums of length $r$, so the total is at most $h(h+1)S/2$. Therefore \[ M^2\le h(h+1)S,\qquad (k-h/2)^2\le(1+1/h)S. \tag{1} \] Choose $h=\lfloor\sqrt k\rfloor$. If $S\ge k^2$ there is nothing to prove; otherwise $1$ implies \[ k^2\le S+kh+k^2/h=S+O(k^{3/2}). \] The individual $a_i$ are distinct and bounded by $n$, giving $S\le kn-k(k-1)/2$. Combining these inequalities yields \[ f(n)\le\frac23 n+O(\sqrt n). \tag{2} \] The argument even permits a nonmonotone ordering, because distinct length-one sums still force distinct entries. It reconstructs a classical weaker upper bound; it is not asserted to be the best published constant.

4. A quantitative obstruction for an infinite sequence

For any infinite increasing sequence with the stated property, \[ \limsup_{j\to\infty}\frac{a_j}{j\log j}\ge\frac12. \tag{3} \] Suppose instead that $a_j\le c j\log j$ for all sufficiently large $j$, with $c<1/2$. Choose a fixed small $\lambda>0$ so that $1/(c(1+\lambda))>2$. For large starting indices $j\le N/(1+\lambda)$ and $1\le r\le\lfloor\lambda j\rfloor$, monotonicity gives \[ \sum_{i=j}^{j+r-1}a_i \le c(1+\lambda)rj\log((1+\lambda)j). \] All these sums are distinct positive integers at most $S_N=a_1+\cdots+a_N=O(N^2\log N)$. Their reciprocal sum is therefore at most the harmonic sum $H_{S_N}=(2+o(1))\log N$. On the other hand it is at least \[ \frac1{c(1+\lambda)} \sum_{j_0\le j\le N/(1+\lambda)} \frac{H_{\lfloor\lambda j\rfloor}}{j\log((1+\lambda)j)} =\left(\frac1{c(1+\lambda)}+o(1)\right)\log N. \] The last equality follows from $H_{\lfloor\lambda j\rfloor}=\log j+O_\lambda(1)$; the accumulated error is $O_\lambda(\log\log N)$. The two bounds contradict the choice of $\lambda$, proving $3$. In particular $j/a_j$ tends to zero along a subsequence, so the set of terms has lower asymptotic density zero.

5. Assessment and references

The interval construction and $2$ leave a large gap between $\sqrt n$ and $n$. Statement $3$ concerns a subsequence in one fixed infinite example and does not yield a uniform bound for all finite examples. Lower density zero is not density zero; nor does $3$ imply convergence of $\sum1/a_j$.

Lemma 1 (prefix-sum/Golomb ruler equivalence)

Let $a_1,\dots,a_k$ be positive integers and define prefix sums \[ p_0:=0,\qquad p_t:=\sum_{i=1}^{t} a_i\quad (1\le t\le k). \] Then the consecutive sums $\sum_{i=u}^{v} a_i$ are all distinct (over all $1\le u\le v\le k$) iff all differences \[ p_j-p_i\quad\text{for }0\le i<j\le k \] are distinct.

Proof. For $1\le u\le v\le k$, \[ \sum_{i=u}^{v} a_i = (a_1+\cdots+a_v)-(a_1+\cdots+a_{u-1}) = p_v-p_{u-1}. \] So equality of two consecutive sums is exactly equality of two differences between prefix sums. The map $(u,v)\mapsto (u-1,v)$ is a bijection between index pairs of consecutive sums and pairs ((i,j)) with $0\le i<j\le k$. Hence the “all consecutive sums distinct” condition is equivalent to “all differences $p_j-p_i$ are distinct.” ∎

So the problem is: build a set of marks ${p_0<\cdots<p_k}$ whose pairwise differences are all distinct, with the additional constraint that adjacent gaps $p_i-p_{i-1}=a_i$ lie in ${1,\dots,n}$ and are strictly increasing.

Part I. Lemma-chain: Erdős #874 property implies Erdős #357 property

Lemma 1 (fixed-length consecutive sums are distinct in a strictly increasing sequence)

Let $a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_k$ be integers and fix an integer (r) with $1\le r\le k$. For each start index $u\in{1,\dots,k-r+1}$, define \[ T_r(u):=\sum_{i=u}^{u+r-1} a_i. \] Then $T_r(u)$ is strictly increasing in (u). In particular, the numbers $T_r(1),\dots,T_r(k-r+1)$ are pairwise distinct.

Proof. For any $u\in{1,\dots,k-r}$, \[ T_r(u+1)-T_r(u) =\left(\sum_{i=u+1}^{u+r} a_i\right)-\left(\sum_{i=u}^{u+r-1} a_i\right) =a_{u+r}-a_u. \] Since $a_{u+r}>a_u$ (strictly increasing sequence), we have $a_{u+r}-a_u>0$, hence $T_r(u+1)>T_r(u)$. ∎

Lemma 2 (#874 disjointness forbids collisions of different lengths among consecutive blocks)

Let $A\subseteq{1,\dots,N}$ satisfy the #874 property: for $r\ne s$, $S_r(A)\cap S_s(A)=\varnothing$. Write (A) in increasing order as $a_1<\cdots<a_k$. Then for any two consecutive blocks \[ \sum_{i=u}^{v} a_i \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{i=u'}^{v'} a_i, \] if their lengths $r=v-u+1$ and $s=v'-u'+1$ differ $(r\ne s)$, then the sums are different.

Proof. Each consecutive block sum $\sum_{i=u}^v a_i$ is a sum of $r=v-u+1$ distinct elements of (A), hence belongs to $S_r(A)$. Likewise $\sum_{i=u'}^{v'} a_i\in S_s(A)$. If $r\ne s$, then by the #874 property $S_r(A)\cap S_s(A)=\varnothing$, so the two sums cannot be equal. ∎

Proposition 3 (#874 ⇒ #357)

If $A\subseteq{1,\dots,N}$ satisfies the #874 property and is written as $a_1<\cdots<a_k$, then all consecutive sums $\sum_{i=u}^v a_i$ are distinct. Therefore \[ f(N)\ \ge\ |A|. \] In particular, \[ f(N)\ \ge\ k_{874}(N). \]

Proof. Take two distinct consecutive blocks $[u,v]\ne[u',v']$. Let $r=v-u+1$, $s=v'-u'+1$.

* If $r=s$, then the two sums are distinct by Lemma 1 (fixed-length consecutive sums are distinct). * If $r\ne s$, then the two sums are distinct by Lemma 2.

Hence every two distinct blocks give different sums, i.e. all consecutive sums are distinct. This shows $f(N)\ge |A|$ for each admissible (A), and hence $f(N)\ge k_{874}(N)$ by definition of $k_{874}(N)$. ∎

Corollary (explicit growth consequence). Since Straus constructed #874-admissible sets with $\liminf k_{874}(N)/\sqrt N\ge 2$, it follows that \[ \liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{f(N)}{\sqrt N}\ \ge\ 2. \]

That is exactly the lemma-chain behind the “Weisenberg observation” mentioned on the #357 page.

---

Part II. A complete upper bound: $g(n)\le(\tfrac23+o(1))n$

I now prove the asymptotic upper bound \[ g(n)\ \le\ \left(\frac23+o(1)\right)n. \]

Setup and notation

Fix $n\ge 1$. Let $k=g(n)$, so there exists a sequence \[ (a_1,\dots,a_k),\qquad 1\le a_i\le n, \] whose consecutive sums are all distinct.

Let \[ A:=\sum_{i=1}^k a_i. \]

Fact 1: the $a_i$ are pairwise distinct. Indeed, the length‑1 consecutive sums are precisely $a_1,\dots,a_k$, which must be distinct.

Hence ${a_1,\dots,a_k}$ is a (k)-element subset of ${1,\dots,n}$, so:

Lemma 4 (sum bound for distinct elements of ${1,\dots,n}$)

If $x_1,\dots,x_k$ are distinct integers in ${1,\dots,n}$, then \[ \sum_{i=1}^k x_i\ \le\ n+(n-1)+\cdots+(n-k+1)=\frac{k(2n-k+1)}2. \]

Proof. The maximum possible sum of (k) distinct elements of ${1,\dots,n}$ occurs when we choose the (k) largest elements $n-k+1,\dots,n$, yielding exactly that sum. ∎

Apply Lemma 4 to get \[ A\le \frac{k(2n-k+1)}2. \tag{4.1} \]

---

Fix a cutoff (t) and count short consecutive sums

Let $t\in\mathbb N$ with $t\ge 1$. Consider all consecutive sums of lengths $1,2,\dots,t$. For $1\le r\le t$ and $1\le u\le k-r+1$, define \[ b_{u,r}:=\sum_{i=u}^{u+r-1} a_i. \] These are all positive integers. Since all consecutive sums are distinct, the family ${b_{u,r}}$ (for $1\le r\le t$) consists of pairwise distinct integers.

Let (M) be the number of these sums: \[ M=\sum_{r=1}^{t} (k-r+1)=t(k+1)-\frac{t(t+1)}2 = tk-\frac{t(t-1)}2. \tag{4.2} \]

Lemma 5 (lower bound on the sum of distinct positive integers)

If $c_1<\cdots<c_M$ are distinct positive integers, then \[ \sum_{j=1}^M c_j\ \ge\ \sum_{j=1}^M j=\frac{M(M+1)}2. \]

Proof. Because $c_1\ge 1$ and $c_{j+1}\ge c_j+1$, we have $c_j\ge j$ for each (j). Summing gives the claim. ∎

Apply Lemma 5 to the ordered list of the (M) values $b_{u,r}$ to obtain \[ \sum_{r=1}^t\ \sum_{u=1}^{k-r+1} b_{u,r}\ \ge\ \frac{M(M+1)}2. \tag{4.3} \]

---

Lemma 6 (upper bound on the same sum by counting multiplicities)

For each fixed (t), \[ \sum_{r=1}^t\ \sum_{u=1}^{k-r+1} b_{u,r}\ \le\ \frac{t(t+1)}2\cdot A. \]

Proof. Fix an index $j\in{1,\dots,k}$. We count in how many of the sums $b_{u,r}$ (with $1\le r\le t$) the term $a_j$ appears.

For a given length (r), the term $a_j$ belongs to $b_{u,r}$ exactly when the block $[u,u+r-1]$ contains (j), i.e. \[ u\le j\le u+r-1 \iff j-r+1\le u\le j. \] There are at most (r) integers (u) satisfying this. Therefore, for each (r), the coefficient of $a_j$ in $\sum_{u=1}^{k-r+1} b_{u,r}$ is $\le r$. Summing over $r=1,\dots,t$, the total coefficient of $a_j$ in $\sum_{r=1}^t\sum_{u} b_{u,r}$ is at most \[ \sum_{r=1}^t r = \frac{t(t+1)}2. \] Hence \[ \sum_{r=1}^t\ \sum_{u=1}^{k-r+1} b_{u,r} \le \sum_{j=1}^k \left(\frac{t(t+1)}2\right)a_j =\frac{t(t+1)}2\cdot A. \] ∎

Combine Lemma 6 with $4.1$ to get \[ \sum_{r=1}^t\ \sum_{u=1}^{k-r+1} b_{u,r} \le \frac{t(t+1)}2\cdot \frac{k(2n-k+1)}2 =\frac{t(t+1)}4,k(2n-k+1). \tag{4.4} \]

---

Combine inequalities and extract the asymptotic $2/3$

From $4.3$ and $4.4$, \[ \frac{M(M+1)}2 \ \le\ \frac{t(t+1)}4,k(2n-k+1). \tag{4.5} \]

Now fix $\varepsilon>0$. We will show: there exists $n_0(\varepsilon)$ such that for all $n\ge n_0(\varepsilon)$, every such (k) satisfies $k\le(\tfrac23+\varepsilon)n$. This proves $g(n)\le(\tfrac23+o(1))n$.

Step 1: choose (t) depending only on $\varepsilon$. Define the function \[ R(t):=\frac{2(t^2+t)}{3t^2-t+\tfrac12}. \] As $t\to\infty$, $R(t)\to \frac23$ (since leading terms give $2t^2/(3t^2)$). Hence we can choose an integer $t=t(\varepsilon)$ so large that \[ R(t)\ \le\ \frac23+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}. \tag{4.6} \]

Step 2: handle the case $k\le t^2$. If $k\le t^2$, then for all sufficiently large (n), \[ k\le t^2 \le \frac{\varepsilon}{2}n \le \left(\frac23+\varepsilon\right)n. \] So for large enough (n) we are done in this case.

Step 3: assume $k>t^2$ and bound (M) from below in terms of (k). Using $4.2$, \[ M=tk-\frac{t(t-1)}2 \ \ge\ tk-\frac{t^2}{2} = k\left(t-\frac{t^2}{2k}\right). \] Since $k>t^2$, we have $\frac{t^2}{2k}<\frac12$, hence \[ M\ \ge\ \left(t-\frac12\right)k. \tag{4.7} \] In particular $M\ge 1$, and also $M+1\ge M\ge (t-\tfrac12)k$. Therefore \[ M(M+1)\ \ge\ \left(t-\frac12\right)^2 k^2. \tag{4.8} \]

Step 4: plug $4.8$ into $4.5$ and solve for (k). From $4.5$ and $4.8$, \[ \frac{(t-\tfrac12)^2 k^2}{2}\ \le\ \frac{t(t+1)}4,k(2n-k+1). \] Cancel $k>0$ and multiply by 4: \[ 2\left(t-\frac12\right)^2 k \ \le\ t(t+1)(2n-k+1). \tag{4.9} \] Bring the (k)-terms to the left: \[ \left(2(t-\tfrac12)^2 + t(t+1)\right)k \ \le\ t(t+1)(2n+1). \] Compute the coefficient: \[ 2\left(t-\frac12\right)^2 + t(t+1) =2\left(t^2-t+\frac14\right)+t^2+t =3t^2-t+\frac12. \] Thus \[ k \ \le\ \frac{t(t+1)}{3t^2-t+\tfrac12},(2n+1). \tag{4.10} \] Divide both sides by (n): \[ \frac{k}{n}\ \le\ \frac{t(t+1)}{3t^2-t+\tfrac12}\left(2+\frac1n\right) = R(t)\left(1+\frac{1}{2n}\right). \tag{4.11} \]

Step 5: conclude $k\le(\tfrac23+\varepsilon)n$ for large (n). Choose $n_0(\varepsilon)$ large enough that:

* $n_0(\varepsilon)\ge 2t(\varepsilon)^2/\varepsilon$, so Step 2 works, and * $1+\frac{1}{2n}\le 1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}$ for all $n\ge n_0(\varepsilon)$, i.e. $n_0(\varepsilon)\ge \frac{2}{\varepsilon}$.

Then for $n\ge n_0(\varepsilon)$, in the case $k>t^2$, we have from $4.11$ and $4.6$ \[ \frac{k}{n}\ \le\ \left(\frac23+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right) \ \le\ \frac23+\varepsilon, \] where the final inequality uses $(\varepsilon/4)^2\le \varepsilon/4$ for $0<\varepsilon\le 1$ and is trivial for larger $\varepsilon$ after adjusting constants (or just enlarge $n_0$). Hence $k\le(\tfrac23+\varepsilon)n$.

This holds for all sufficiently large (n), so \[ g(n)\ \le\ \left(\frac23+o(1)\right)n. \] Finally, since every strictly increasing admissible sequence for (f(n)) is also admissible for (g(n)), we have $f(n)\le g(n)$, hence \[ f(n)\ \le\ \left(\frac23+o(1)\right)n. \] ∎

---

5) VERIFICATION

Quantifier sanity

* The asymptotic statement $g(n)\le(\frac23+o(1))n$ means: \[ \forall \varepsilon>0\ \exists n_0\ \forall n\ge n_0:\ g(n)\le(\tfrac23+\varepsilon)n, \] which is exactly what Step 5 proves.

Boundary/edge cases

* If $k\le t^2$, we explicitly handled it (Step 2) by choosing (n) large. * If $k>t^2$, then $M\ge (t-\tfrac12)k\ge 1$ ensures Lemma 5 applies. * We used only that $a_i\in{1,\dots,n}$ and all consecutive sums are distinct, hence the $a_i$ themselves are distinct.

Stress test of inequalities

* Lemma 6: the “at most (r) windows of length (r)” containing a fixed index (j) is correct because possible start points (u) are integers in an interval of length (r). * Lemma 4: correct because among all (k)-subsets of ${1,\dots,n}$, the maximal sum is the top (k) elements. * The key constant $2/3$ arises from comparing “$\sim \frac12 M^2$” vs “$\sim \frac{t(t+1)}4 k(2n-k)$” with (M$\sim$ tk), which algebraically yields $3k\lesssim 2n$. This is consistent with the final bound.

Small-(n) behavior

The proof is asymptotic; it does not forbid $g(n)/n$ being $>2/3$ for small (n). (Indeed brute force finds examples with ratio $>2/3$ for small (n), which is compatible with an $o(1)$ term.)

---

FINAL: PROOF

Theorem A (#874 ⇒ #357). If $A\subseteq{1,\dots,N}$ satisfies the #874 disjointness property of (r)-term sums for distinct (r), then listing (A) increasingly as $a_1<\cdots<a_k$ yields a sequence whose consecutive sums are all distinct. Consequently $f(N)\ge k_{874}(N)$.

Theorem B (upper bound on (g)). Let (g(n)) be the maximum length of a sequence $a_1,\dots,a_k\in{1,\dots,n}$ with all consecutive sums distinct. Then \[ g(n)\ \le\ \left(\frac23+o(1)\right)n \quad (n\to\infty). \] In particular $f(n)\le g(n)$ implies \[ f(n)\ \le\ \left(\frac23+o(1)\right)n. \]

Both theorems are proved in Sections 4.I and 4.II above.

What I proved in the previous message were two auxiliary statements that appear in the context paragraph, not the main open question of Erdős Problem #357.

What Erdős Problem #357 actually asks

Problem #357 asks for the asymptotic growth of \[ f(n)=\max\left\{k:\ \exists, 1\le a_1<\cdots<a_k\le n\ \text{with all consecutive sums }\sum_{i=u}^v a_i\ \text{distinct}\right\}, \] and in particular asks whether \[ f(n)=o(n). \] The page itself explicitly marks this problem as OPEN and states those questions. ([erdosproblems.com][1])

What my “PROOF” did (and did not) do

$1$ I did give a full proof of the implication “#874 ⇒ #357-property”

I proved that any set $A\subseteq{1,\dots,N}$ satisfying the #874 “disjoint (r)-sum sets” property yields (when ordered increasingly) a sequence whose consecutive sums are all distinct, hence gives a lower bound for (f(N)). This matches exactly the “Weisenberg observation” quoted on the #357 page. ([erdosproblems.com][1])

This only yields the known-type lower bound \[ f(n)\ge (2+o(1))\sqrt n, \] not the true order of growth.

$2$ I did give a full proof of an upper bound for (g(n)), and hence for (f(n))

I proved (elementarily) that the non-monotone variant (g(n)) satisfies \[ g(n)\le \left(\frac23+o(1)\right)n, \] which is exactly the upper bound attributed to Hegyvári in the #357 page (for (g)). ([erdosproblems.com][1]) Since $f(n)\le g(n)$, this gives \[ f(n)\le \left(\frac23+o(1)\right)n. \]

But this is still a linear upper bound, and therefore does not imply $f(n)=o(n)$.

So what remains unsolved

Even after those two proved bounds, the situation is still: \[ (2+o(1))\sqrt n \ \le\ f(n)\ \le\ \left(\frac23+o(1)\right)n, \] and the central question “is $f(n)=o(n)$?” is not answered by my proofs and is still listed as open. ([erdosproblems.com][1])

Also, the other subquestions in the paragraph you pasted (about infinite sequences, density, $\sum 1/a_k$, least nonrepresentable (m), etc.) were not resolved by what I wrote.
