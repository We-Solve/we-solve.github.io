Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.

Definitions and conventions.
A polycrystal orientation field describes local lattice orientation, typically by a map into $SO(3)$ modulo the crystal's point-group symmetry. Unit quaternions give a double cover of $SO(3)$, so opposite quaternions represent the same physical rotation. Topological defects are configurations whose orientation map around a defect cannot be continuously filled within the allowed order-parameter space; line and point defects involve different homotopy tests. Symmetry quotienting matters to their classification.

A quaternion loop check.
Before looking for new defects, consider the orientation space $SO(3)$ without a further crystal quotient. The path of unit quaternions \[ q(\theta)=\cos(\theta/2)+\mathbf k\sin(\theta/2),\qquad0\le\theta\le2\pi, \] projects to rotation by angle $\theta$ about one fixed axis. Its projected path is a closed loop, whereas its lift runs from $1$ to $-1$. This loop cannot contract while its basepoint stays fixed. Indeed the two-to-one quaternion projection is locally invertible; a proposed homotopy can be lifted successively through these local inverse neighborhoods. Its terminal lift lies in the discrete fiber $\{1,-1\}$ and varies continuously with the homotopy parameter, hence stays $-1$. A constant loop instead has lift ending at $1$, a contradiction.
Consequences and limits.
A spatial orientation field around a line can therefore carry a sign-valued obstruction invisible to a single globally chosen quaternion chart. This is the familiar rotation-loop mechanism, not a newly discovered three-dimensional defect. The source explicitly leaves the point-group quotient and already identified list to the report. Passing to $SO(3)/G$ for a finite crystal group $G$ changes the fiber and possible loop endpoints; one cannot simply reuse the two-point argument as a complete classification. Nor does a line-loop obstruction classify point defects, which require filling spheres.
