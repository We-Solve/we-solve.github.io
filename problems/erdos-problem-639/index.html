Statement

In a two-colouring of $K_n$, are there at most $n^2/4$ edges which belong to no monochromatic triangle? Call such edges uncovered, and let $h(n)$ be their maximum possible number. The exact known answer is \[ h(n)= \begin{cases} \binom n2,&n\leq5,\\ 10,&n=6,\\ \lfloor n^2/4\rfloor,&n\geq7. \end{cases} \] Thus the literal assertion for every $n$ has small counterexamples; the intended large-$n$ statement is true. We reconstruct the proof for $n\geq10$ from Keevash and Sudakov, On the number of edges not covered by monochromatic copies of a fixed graph, Theorem 1.1 and Proposition 2.1, https://people.math.ethz.ch/~sudakovb/nim-edges.pdf. Their verified finite cases $n=7,8,9$ are used externally. See https://www.erdosproblems.com/639.

Erdős Problem 639: edges outside monochromatic triangles

Statement and qualification.
In a two-colouring of $K_n$, are there at most $n^2/4$ edges which belong to no monochromatic triangle? Call such edges uncovered, and let $h(n)$ be their maximum possible number. The exact known answer is \[ h(n)= \begin{cases} \binom n2,&n\leq5,\\ 10,&n=6,\\ \lfloor n^2/4\rfloor,&n\geq7. \end{cases} \] Thus the literal assertion for every $n$ has small counterexamples; the intended large-$n$ statement is true. We reconstruct the proof for $n\geq10$ from Keevash and Sudakov, On the number of edges not covered by monochromatic copies of a fixed graph, Theorem 1.1 and Proposition 2.1, https://people.math.ethz.ch/~sudakovb/nim-edges.pdf. Their verified finite cases $n=7,8,9$ are used externally. See https://www.erdosproblems.com/639.
A useful counting observation.
Suppose disjoint vertices $u,v$ and a set $W$ have all edges from $u$ to $W$ blue and all edges from $v$ to $W$ red. No edge inside $W$ is uncovered: its colour forms a triangle with $u$ or $v$. Moreover, at most $|W|$ uncovered edges join $\{u,v\}$ to $W$, provided $|W|\geq2$. Indeed, if $uw$ is uncovered, every edge from $w$ to $W\setminus\{w\}$ is red, making every $vw'$, including $vw$, part of a red triangle. Thus none of the red spokes is uncovered. If no blue spoke is uncovered, the same bound is immediate.
The bound for $n\geq10$.
Suppose there are more than $\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor$ uncovered edges. Mantel's theorem supplies a triangle all of whose edges are uncovered. It cannot be monochromatic, so label it with $xy,xz$ red and $yz$ blue. Every edge from $x$ to $S=V\setminus\{x,y,z\}$ is blue: a red $xt$ would force both $ty,tz$ blue, contradicting that $yz$ is uncovered.

No $xt$, $t\in S$, can itself be uncovered. Otherwise all edges from $t$ to $W=V\setminus\{x,y,z,t\}$ would be red. The preceding observation bounds uncovered edges inside $W$ and from $\{x,t\}$ to $W$ by $|W|$. Allowing all $2|W|$ edges from $\{y,z\}$ to $W$ and all six edges on $\{x,y,z,t\}$, the total would be at most \[ 3(n-4)+6=3n-6<\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor \] for $n\geq10$, a contradiction.

If another vertex $v$ has both $vy,vz$ uncovered, these edges must both be red. They cannot both be blue because of $yz$. If, say, $vy$ were blue and $vz$ red, the uncovered mixed triangle $vyz$ would force all edges from $y$ to $V\setminus\{v,y,z\}$ red. On $W=V\setminus\{x,y,z,v\}$, the opposite spokes from $x,y$ would again give the contradictory bound $3n-6$. Therefore $vy,vz$ are red. Applying the previous paragraph to the triangle $vyz$ shows that every other edge at $v$ is covered.

Only red edges inside $S$ can be uncovered, because all blue ones form triangles with $x$. The uncovered red graph is triangle-free, so it has at most $\lfloor(n-3)^2/4\rfloor$ edges. Hence the number of uncovered edges from $\{x,y,z\}$ to $S$ is at least \[ \lfloor n^2/4\rfloor-\lfloor(n-3)^2/4\rfloor-2\geq n-1. \] None meets $x$. Since $|S|=n-3$, at least two vertices $v,w\in S$ must each have both spokes to $y,z$ uncovered: if at most one did, there would be at most $|S|+1=n-2$. The preceding paragraph says that no other edge at $v,w$ is uncovered. Counting all edges incident with $y,z$, and using Mantel on the remaining $n-5$ vertices, now gives at most \[ 2(n-3)+3+\left\lfloor\frac{(n-5)^2}{4}\right\rfloor \leq\left\lfloor\frac{n^2}{4}\right\rfloor \] uncovered edges. This contradiction proves the upper bound.

Sharpness and the small cases.
Colour the edges across a balanced bipartition red and the edges within parts blue. All $\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor$ red edges are uncovered, proving sharpness for $n\geq7$. For $n\leq5$, use the colouring of $K_5$ whose red and blue graphs are both $5$-cycles, and take induced subgraphs. For $n=6$, take a red $5$-cycle and attach the sixth vertex by red edges to three consecutive cycle vertices; colour all other edges blue. The only covered edges are the five edges in the resulting two red triangles, leaving ten uncovered. The upper bound ten for $n=6$ can be checked by examining all $2^{15}$ colourings; the offline verification accompanying this attempt does so. For $n=7,8,9$, the exact upper bounds are the finite cases of the cited primary theorem, not newly verified exhaustively here.
Scope.
The asymptotic argument is complete and elementary apart from Mantel's theorem; the exact all-$n$ answer additionally uses the explicitly identified published finite cases.
