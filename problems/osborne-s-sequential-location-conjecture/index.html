Osborne's Sequential-Location Conjecture
Problem statement, definitions, and possible variants

Research-problem specification for We--Solve
Based on a conjecture of Martin J. Osborne

27 October 2026

Abstract

This note states Martin J. Osborne's conjecture about subgame-perfect outcomes in sequential electoral entry and separates the original open problem from natural, generally nonequivalent variants. This is a problem description, not a solution. The companion file osborne_ai_attempt.tex records an AI-assisted proof attempt and its verification status.

Original game (exact formulation)

Let $n\ge2$ be a finite integer. Players $1,\ldots,n$ move in that order, each observing all earlier actions, and select an action \[ a_i\in X:=[0,1]\cup\{\mathsf{OUT}\}. \] Choosing $\mathsf{OUT}$ means not running; choosing $x\in[0,1]$ means entering at political location $x$. Every player chooses exactly once, and there are no costs or policy payoffs beyond the terminal payoff specified below.

Voters form a unit mass. Their ideal locations have cumulative distribution function $F:[0,1]\to[0,1]$, assumed nonatomic with support $[0,1]$. Equivalently, $F$ is continuous and strictly increasing, with $F(0)=0$ and $F(1)=1$; its unique median is $m:=F^{-1}(1/2)$. Every voter votes for a nearest occupied position. At voter locations equidistant from distinct occupied positions, the choice of position has measure zero because $F$ is nonatomic. Candidates at the same position divide the votes drawn to that position equally.

For a terminal action profile $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$, let $y_1<\cdots<y_k$ be its distinct occupied positions and $r_j\ge1$ their multiplicities. For $k\ge2$, the constituency masses at these positions are \[ Q_1=F\!\left(\frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right),\qquad Q_k=1-F\!\left(\frac{y_{k-1}+y_k}{2}\right). \] For each interior occupied position, \[ Q_j=F\!\left(\frac{y_j+y_{j+1}}2\right)-F\!\left(\frac{y_{j-1}+y_j}2\right),\qquad 1<j<k. \] Every candidate at $y_j$ has individual vote share $s_i(a)=Q_j/r_j$. For $k=1$, $Q_1=1$ and each of the $r_1$ colocated candidates gets $1/r_1$. For no entry, no candidate has a vote share.

Let $s^*(a)=\max_{i:a_i\ne\mathsf{OUT}}s_i(a)$ when at least one candidate enters, and let \[ W(a)=\{i:a_i\ne\mathsf{OUT},\ s_i(a)=s^*(a)\},\qquad w(a)=|W(a)|. \] Player $i$'s payoff is \[ u_i(a)=\begin{cases} 0,&a_i=\mathsf{OUT},\\ 1/w(a),&i\in W(a),\\ -1,&a_i\ne\mathsf{OUT}\text{ and }i\notin W(a). \end{cases} \] Thus distinct-position ties for the highest vote share are allowed; all tied candidates win with equal probability. This feature is critical. The game has perfect information, and an equilibrium means a pure-strategy subgame-perfect equilibrium (SPE), unless a variant states otherwise. An SPE outcome is the terminal action vector induced by an SPE, not a full contingent strategy profile. Equilibrium strategies may be nonunique even when the outcome is unique.

Osborne's conjecture.
For every $n\ge2$ and every such $F$, the set of pure SPE outcomes consists of the single action vector \[ \boxed{a^*=(m,\underbrace{\mathsf{OUT},\ldots,\mathsf{OUT}}_{n-2\ \text{players}},m).} \] The two entrants are players $1$ and $n$, both at the median. This is an outcome-uniqueness claim, not uniqueness of equilibrium strategies. It contains two logically distinct questions: (E) is $a^*$ supported by an SPE for every $(n,F)$? (U) is every SPE outcome equal to $a^*$?
Known status as described by the originator.
Osborne reports proofs for $n=2,3,4$ and states that the unrestricted conjecture remains open. His 2024 paper proves a result for a different model that prevents ties among candidates at distinct positions. An apparent twelve-player counterexample in a 2015 thesis was reported by Osborne as incomplete and neither confirmed nor refuted. Do not treat any of these as a proof or disproof of the original conjecture [OsborneWeb,Osborne2024].

Suggested subproblems (same underlying model)

The following restrictions retain all payoff and tie rules of the original game, so a positive result would be genuine progress toward the conjecture:
  1. Fixed $n$: prove (E) and (U) separately for $n=5$, $n=6$, or the smallest not-yet-resolved $n$. State whether proofs hold for arbitrary $F$ or only uniform voters.
  2. Uniform distribution: $F(x)=x$, arbitrary finite $n$; identify which parts of the full conjecture use the uniform geometry.
  3. Symmetric distributions: $F(m+t)=1-F(m-t)$ whenever both sides are defined; do not confuse symmetry with uniformity.
  4. Regularity classes: positive continuous density, differentiable density, bounded or log-concave densities. Specify assumptions and check whether the result extends by a change of variable (note that Euclidean distances are not preserved by quantile transforms).
  5. Locally uniform with nonuniform tails: for example $F(t)=t$ on $[1/12,11/12]$, unrestricted continuous strictly increasing tails matching endpoints. Analyze whether the $n=5$ outcome remains unique.
  6. Continuation games: with one fixed median incumbent and $k$ future movers, classify SPEs; with two fixed incumbents at $m,x$, find winning/losing equilibrium continuations for the entrant at $x$. These off-path subgames are essential for a construction of an SPE, not merely a Nash outcome.
  7. Maximum number of entrants: characterize the maximum number of simultaneous terminal winners in an SPE, or characterize all attainable SPE outcomes in subgames with incumbents.
  8. Constructive/procedural proof: give an explicit rule selecting a pure SPE at every history, and show the specified outcome is induced from the root.

Related variants (distinct games; conclusions may change)

These are optional follow-up problems, not equivalent restatements of Osborne's conjecture:
  1. Tie rules. Break ties deterministically by entrant priority, by an independent strict ranking, or by randomization; allow random wins only for colocated candidates. Osborne discusses priority tie-breaking and studies no-distinct-position-ties in the 2024 paper [OsborneWeb,Osborne2024].
  2. Costs and prizes. Replace the $(-1,0,1/w)$ payoffs by expected prize $b/w-c$ for entrants, where $b>0$ and $c>0$, or by common strictly increasing functions of winning probability. Equilibrium incentives need not match the original game.
  3. Policy or vote-seeking objectives. Maximize own vote share, probability of election, or policy utility in addition to office benefit.
  4. Location restrictions. Permit a finite grid, any closed subset $X\subset[0,1]$, or a continuum with forbidden regions; distinguish exogenous candidate sites from voter support.
  5. Order and information. Simultaneous instead of sequential entry; endogenous or random order; imperfect observation of prior locations; private information about $F$.
  6. Voter rules. Probabilistic/spatially noisy voting, multiple issues, non-Euclidean distance, or voter atoms (requiring an explicit rule for voter indifference).
  7. Equilibrium refinements. Mixed or behavioral SPE, trembling-hand perfection, or restrictions on off-path equilibrium selection. Specify the notion of uniqueness (outcome distribution, action profile, or strategy profile).
  8. Entry flexibility. Allow withdrawal, revision of positions, or entry after observing later candidates; this alters the extensive form.

Minimal checklist for proposed resolutions

  1. State $n$, the class of $F$, and whether the claim concerns existence, uniqueness, or both.
  2. At every history, including deviations, specify or prove existence of optimal continuation play; one terminal vote-share configuration is insufficient for SPE.
  3. Keep separate the vote share $s_i$ and payoff $1/w$, and account for tied winners at distinct locations.
  4. Distinguish incumbents fixed before a subgame (who can lose) from fresh entrants during the subgame (who would choose OUT rather than knowingly lose in its equilibrium).
  5. Show the location/action optimization is attained. With finite payoff values, best attainable payoff exists once continuation choices have been fixed, even with a continuum of locations.
  6. Cite checked references and disclose all human/model assistance. An AI proof sketch is not independent mathematical verification.
