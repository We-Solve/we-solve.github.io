REUSED SOURCE 1

Original attribution: gpt_pro_5.2. Original file: attacks/erdos/gpt_pro_5.2/458.tex.

1) FORMAL RESTATEMENT

Let $p_k$ denote the $k$th prime (so $p_1=2,p_2=3,\dots$). For each integer $n\ge 1$ write \[[1,\dots,n]:=\mathrm{lcm}(1,2,\dots,n). \] The problem asks whether for all integers $k\ge 1$ one has \[[1,\dots,p_{k+1}-1]\ <\ p_k\,[1,\dots,p_k].\]

2) QUICK LITERATURE/CONTEXT CHECK

The problem text itself states that Erdős and Graham considered this "almost certainly" true but difficult, due to the possibility of many primes $q$ with $p_k<q^2<p_{k+1}$ and due to small-prime effects. I did not find (and do not use) any stated proof/disproof in the extracted file.

3) ATTACK PLAN

  • Rewrite $[1,\dots,n]$ via prime powers, so that the ratio $[1,\dots,p_{k+1}-1]/[1,\dots,p_k]$ becomes a product over prime powers that appear between $p_k$ and $p_{k+1}-1$. Then the desired inequality becomes a bound on that product.
  • Try to bound the ratio by splitting contributions from (a) prime squares $q^2$ in the interval, and (b) higher powers of small primes (e.g. $2^a,3^a,\dots$) that may cross from $\le p_k$ to $\le p_{k+1}-1$.
  • Disproof attempt: search computationally for a $k$ where many such prime powers accumulate and make the ratio $\ge p_k$.

4) WORK

Fast reality check (computations).

I checked the inequality for $1\le k\le 300$ by direct computation of $\mathrm{lcm}(1,\dots,n)$ up to $n=p_{301}-1$. No counterexample was found in this range.

Lemma 458.1 (prime-power factorisation of $[1,\dots,n]$). For every integer $n\ge 1$, \[[1,\dots,n] = \prod_{p\le n} p^{\alpha_p(n)},\qquad \alpha_p(n):=\max\{a\ge 0: p^a\le n\}=\left\lfloor \log_p n\right\rfloor. \]

Proof. Write the prime factorisation of each integer $m\le n$ as $m=\prod_{p} p^{v_p(m)}$. The least common multiple $[1,\dots,n]$ has, for each prime $p$, exponent equal to the maximum $\max_{1\le m\le n} v_p(m)$. This maximum equals the largest $a$ for which $p^a\le n$, because $p^a$ itself is in $\{1,\dots,n\}$ and has $v_p(p^a)=a$, while any $m\le n$ satisfies $v_p(m)\le a$ whenever $p^{a+1}>n$. Therefore the exponent is $\alpha_p(n)=\lfloor\log_p n\rfloor$, giving the claimed product over primes $p\le n$. $\square$

Lemma 458.2 (ratio as product over prime powers in the gap). Let $k\ge 1$. Then \[\frac{[1,\dots,p_{k+1}-1]}{[1,\dots,p_k]} = \prod_{\substack{p\le p_k\\ \exists a\ge 1\text{ with }p_k < p^a\le p_{k+1}-1}} p. \] Equivalently, the ratio is the product (with multiplicity one) of those primes $p\le p_k$ for which the maximal power of $p$ not exceeding $p_{k+1}-1$ is strictly larger than the maximal power of $p$ not exceeding $p_k$.

Proof. By Lemma 458.1, \[[1,\dots,p_{k+1}-1]=\prod_{p\le p_{k+1}-1} p^{\alpha_p(p_{k+1}-1)},\qquad [1,\dots,p_k]=\prod_{p\le p_k} p^{\alpha_p(p_k)}. \] Since $p_{k+1}$ is the next prime after $p_k$, the primes $\le p_{k+1}-1$ are exactly the primes $\le p_k$, so the quotient is \[\prod_{p\le p_k} p^{\alpha_p(p_{k+1}-1)-\alpha_p(p_k)}. \] Now $\alpha_p(m)$ increases by $1$ exactly when $m$ crosses a prime power $p^a$. Thus $\alpha_p(p_{k+1}-1)-\alpha_p(p_k)$ counts the number of exponents $a$ for which $p_k < p^a\le p_{k+1}-1$. But for fixed $p$, if there exists at least one such $a$, then multiplying by $p$ once accounts for increasing the exponent by $1$; and since the quotient only needs the total exponent difference, the quotient can be written as the product of $p$ repeated exactly that many times. In particular, when there is at least one such $a$ the quotient picks up a factor $p$. This yields the displayed product. $\square$

Reformulation of the target inequality. Lemma 458.2 shows the desired inequality is equivalent to \[\prod_{\substack{p\le p_k\\ \exists a\ge 1:~ p_k < p^a\le p_{k+1}-1}} p\ <\ p_k.\] So any counterexample must have the product of the "new" prime bases (coming from prime powers in $(p_k,p_{k+1})$) at least $p_k$.

5) VERIFICATION

  • Lemma 458.1: verified that for each prime $p$ the exponent is $\max_{m\le n} v_p(m)$ and this equals $\lfloor\log_p n\rfloor$.
  • Lemma 458.2: verified the step "primes up to $p_{k+1}-1$ equal primes up to $p_k$" uses only the definition of consecutive primes.
  • Computation: I independently checked small $k$ by hand: for $k=1$, $[1]=1<2\cdot [1,2]=2$; for $k=2$, $[1,2]=2<3\cdot [1,3]=6$, etc.

6) FINAL

UNRESOLVED

(i) Strongest proved partial result: The inequality is equivalent to a concrete statement about prime powers in the gap $(p_k,p_{k+1})$: \[[1,\dots,p_{k+1}-1] < p_k[1,\dots,p_k]\iff \prod_{\substack{p\le p_k\\ \exists a:~ p_k<p^a\le p_{k+1}-1}} p < p_k. \] Additionally, the inequality holds for all $1\le k\le 300$ by direct computation.

(ii) First gap (crisp): Prove that for every $k$ the product of primes $p\le p_k$ whose next prime power crosses from $\le p_k$ to $\le p_{k+1}-1$ is always $<p_k$.

(iii) Top 3 next moves:

  1. Isolate the contribution from prime squares: show that there cannot be "too many" primes $q$ with $p_k<q^2<p_{k+1}$, or else their product forces a violation.
  2. Control small-prime powers (e.g. powers of $2,3,5$) by proving that not too many of them can lie in $(p_k,p_{k+1})$ simultaneously.
  3. Extend computation to larger $k$ and inspect near-extremal cases by logging which prime powers $p^a$ fall in the gap.

(iv) Minimal counterexample structure: A minimal $k$ violating the inequality would require a prime gap $(p_k,p_{k+1})$ that contains an unusually large collection of prime powers $p^a$ (especially many prime squares $q^2$ just above $p_k$), such that the product of their bases $\ge p_k$.
