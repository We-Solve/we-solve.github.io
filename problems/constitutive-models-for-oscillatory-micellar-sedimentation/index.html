The Reynolds number $\mathrm{Re}=\rho UL/\mu$ compares inertial and viscous effects. A constitutive equation relates those stresses to deformation and internal structural variables. Testing a passive one-relaxation closure.
Let $v$ be settling velocity and $s$ an additional drag stress. The simplest Maxwell-type reduced model is \[ m\dot v=F-\zeta v-s,\qquad \tau\dot s+s=\eta v, \] with constant driving $F$, $\zeta,\tau>0$, $\eta\geq0$, and $m\geq0$. Its equilibrium is $v_*=F/(\zeta+\eta)$, $s_*=\eta v_*$. For $m>0$ the characteristic polynomial of perturbations is \[ m\tau\lambda^2+(m+\zeta\tau)\lambda+(\zeta+\eta). \] Both roots have negative real part: if real, their product is positive and sum negative; if conjugate, each real part is half the negative sum. Oscillations, when the discriminant is negative, therefore decay.
The low-inertia limit is even more restrictive.
At $m=0$, substitute $v=(F-s)/\zeta$ into the stress equation. It becomes a scalar linear relaxation with rate $(1+\eta/\zeta)/\tau>0$. There is no nonconstant periodic solution. These assertions can also be checked directly from the matrix exponential or the single exponential at $m=0$.
Attempt outcome.
This rules out sustained oscillation in this particular passive linear closure; it does not rule out viscoelasticity or wormlike-micelle models generally. The observed nontransient phenomenon needs an ingredient absent here, such as nonlinear structural feedback, more state variables or spatial instability. Selecting such an ingredient from micellar breakage/recombination physics, deriving the sphere-fluid coupling and validating a stable oscillation remain unproved. Merely obtaining complex eigenvalues in a linearization would only explain transient ringing and is insufficient for the original target.
