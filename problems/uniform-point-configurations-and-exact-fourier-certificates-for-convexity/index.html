Math-1411

Uniform point configurations and exact Fourier certificates for convexity

Status Partially solved · unsolvedmath.comLLM status Open unverified

Top Open Problems Not reported cataloguedPartially solvedOpen

Statement

Definitions and mathematical statement

This record concatenates Problems 7 and 8 of the 2007 AIM list, rather than one question.

Problem 7 (S. Robins) asks for an explicit configuration of $N$ "uniformly" arranged points on $S^{n-1}$, and whether these coincide with the vertices of a maximum-volume inscribed $N$-vertex polytope. "Uniformly" is not defined: separation, discrepancy, energy and spherical-design conditions are different targets. A convex polytope cannot literally be contained in the sphere unless degenerate; the meaningful inscribed reading is that its vertices lie on the sphere and the polytope lies in the unit ball. Positive $n$-dimensional volume requires $N\ge n+1$.

An equal-weight spherical 2-design $x_1,\ldots,x_N\in S^{n-1}$ satisfies \[ \frac1N\sum_i x_i=0,\qquad\frac1N\sum_i x_ix_i^{\mathsf T}=\frac1n I_n. \] The aid reports that regular polygons in the planar case and regular simplices for $N=n+1$ simultaneously give maximum inscribed volume, spherical designs and optimal separation in their respective settings. It also gives a mismatch: for odd $n\ge3$ and $N=n+2$, no equal-weight spherical 2-design exists. The maximum-volume configuration attributed to Horvath--Langi is a join of two regular simplices in orthogonal complementary subspaces of dimensions $d=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ and $e=n-d$. The join means the convex hull of their union. It is instead a weighted 2-design: each of the $d+1$ vertices in the first simplex may receive weight $d/[n(d+1)]$, and each of the $e+1$ vertices in the second weight $e/[n(e+1)]$. These weights have sum one, weighted mean zero and weighted second moment $I_n/n$. Thus one particular precise meaning of uniformity need not agree with the volume extremum.

Problem 8 asks whether the Fourier transform of a function $f$ on $S^{n-1}$ can determine whether it is a support function or a radial function of a convex body. The transform convention and regularity are not fixed in the original question. The aid supplies the following great-circle Fourier criterion, for real continuous $f$ and $n\ge2$.

For every orthonormal pair $a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^n$, restrict $f$ to $u(t)=a\cos t+b\sin t$, and define \[ c_k=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(u(t))e^{-ikt}\,{\,\mathrm{d}} t,\qquad k\in{\mathbb{Z}}. \] Then all the matrices \[ T_r=\left[(1-(j-k)^2)c_{j-k}\right]_{j,k=0}^r,\qquad r=0,1,2,\ldots, \] are required to be positive semidefinite for every great-circle restriction. Equivalently, the circle distribution $f+f''$ is nonnegative on each such restriction. The aid's nonsmooth characterization is that this condition is exactly the condition for $f$ to be a support function of a nonempty compact convex set, with homogeneous extension $H(x)=|x|f(x/|x|)$, $H(0)=0$. If "convex body" requires nonempty interior, the additional condition that the resulting set be full dimensional is necessary; it can be expressed by \[ f(u)+f(-u)>0\quad\text{for every }u\in S^{n-1}. \] The Toeplitz condition alone also permits support functions of points and other lower-dimensional sets. This qualification corrects the aid's unqualified use of the word body.

For a positive continuous $f$, set $K_f=\{tu:u\in S^{n-1},\ 0\le t\le f(u)\}$. It is a convex body with origin in its interior exactly when the same Toeplitz test holds for the great-circle Fourier coefficients of $1/f$; in that case $1/f$ is the support function of the polar $K_f^\circ$. Positivity is essential for this reciprocal criterion.

The aid also asserts that no fixed finite spherical-harmonic cutoff decides either property. Specifically, for a cutoff $L$, choose an odd degree $m>L$, sufficiently large that $m(m+n-2)>2(n-1)$, and a real spherical harmonic $Y_m$ of degree $m$ normalized by $\|Y_m\|_\infty=1$. Its oddness gives extrema $1$ and $-1$. The positive perturbation \[ f(u)=1+\frac{2(n-1)}{m(m+n-2)}Y_m(u) \] has exactly the same harmonic modes through degree $L$ as the constant function 1, but the aid reports that it is neither a support function nor a radial function of a convex body. The normalization and sufficiently-large-degree restriction make the claimed positive example precise. The all-circles, all-matrix-sizes criterion is an infinite characterization and is not thereby a finite algorithm for arbitrary input functions.

Short English statement

Robins's point-placement question has several inequivalent meanings of uniformity; the aid gives an explicit case where equal-weight design requirements cannot match a volume maximizer. The appended recognition question receives an infinite Fourier positivity criterion, with reciprocal testing for radial functions. Finite low-frequency data alone cannot decide these properties, and nonempty interior needs a separate qualification for support functions.
