Statement

Definitions and mathematical statement

For a prime $p\nmid a$, let $\operatorname{ord}_p(a)$ be the least $m>0$ with $a^m\equiv1\pmod p$. Then $a$ is a primitive root modulo $p$ exactly when $\operatorname{ord}_p(a)=p-1$. For each integer $a\ne-1$ which is not a square, the target requires a positive prime-relative density \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\#\{p\le x:\operatorname{ord}_p(a)=p-1\}}{\pi(x)}=\delta(a)>0. \] The Artin density is expressed in the usual inclusion-exclusion convention by $\delta(a)=\sum_{m\ge1}\mu(m)/[{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_m,a^{1/m}):{\mathbb{Q}}]$, equivalently its corrected Artin-product form. The algebraic degrees encode dependencies between prime-power obstructions. The density is among primes, not among all positive integers.

Formulation and concept references: [S1].

Short English statement

Does every admissible integer generate the multiplicative group modulo a positive proportion of primes, with precisely Artin's predicted density?

Math-269: What the unconditional three-base theorem proves, and an exact base-two safe-prime subclass

prb_12144618457b494d995b50fd94f9ea89: What the unconditional three-base theorem proves, and an exact base-two safe-prime subclass

Claim type: Literature report

Exact scope: Credit Heath-Brown’s theorem that at most two prime bases fail to be primitive roots at infinitely many primes, hence one of 2,3,5 succeeds infinitely often. Separately prove that if q≡1 mod4 and p=2q+1 are primes, then 2 is primitive mod p. Neither identifies a universal fixed base nor proves the target density.

There is an unconditional disjunctive theorem substantially weaker than the printed density assertion: among any three distinct prime bases, at least one is a primitive root modulo infinitely many primes. Heath-Brown's 1986 result implies this; the checked expert author account is Moree §6.1 [1], with the original publication identified in [2]. In particular at least one of 2,3,5 works infinitely often. The full original sieve proof was not independently reproduced here. This report keeps that theorem distinct from both the prompt's positive-density assertion for every prescribed base and the later unverified manuscript summarized in the earlier attempt.

Let S_a={p prime:p∤a and ord_p(a)=p−1}. The theorem is the statement
\[
\#\{a\text{ prime}:S_a\text{ is finite}\}\le2.
\]
Applied to 2,3,5, it gives ∃a∈{2,3,5} such that S_a is infinite. This is equivalent to saying that their union contains infinitely many primes: a finite union of finite sets would be finite. The argument does not identify a particular a whose set S_a is infinite. Nor does infinitude alone give positive prime-relative density: a set can be infinite and have density zero.

**A fully elementary fixed-base subclass.** Suppose q≡1 mod4 is prime and p=2q+1 is also prime. Then q≥5 and p≡3 mod8. The supplementary quadratic reciprocity formula gives (2/p)=−1, so Euler's criterion yields 2^q≡−1 mod p. The multiplicative order divides p−1=2q and does not divide q. Its only possibilities are therefore 2 or 2q. Order two would give p∣2²−1=3, impossible because p≥11. Hence
\[
\mathrm{ord}_p(2)=2q=p-1.
\]
Every prime pair with those hypotheses contributes a prime in S₂. For example q=5 gives p=11, and q=29 gives p=59. This is a proof for all pairs satisfying the pattern; it does not assert that the pattern occurs infinitely often. That last statement is itself an unresolved prime-pattern task and is not supplied by the argument.

The congruence q≡1 mod4 is substantive. If q≡3 mod4 and p=2q+1 is prime, then p≡7 mod8 and (2/p)=1, so ord_p(2) divides q. Since 2 is not 1 modulo p, its order is exactly q, and 2 fails to be primitive. Thus safe primes split into opposite behaviors according to q mod4. For q=3, p=7 and the order is three. Treating every safe prime as a primitive-root prime for base two would be a specific error, not just an unproved density heuristic.

**An exact finite diagnostic.** The included program sieves q≤10000 and tests primality of every p=2q+1 by trial division/sieve. It checks both order conclusions, using exact factorization of p−1 and modular exponentiation to certify each order. The finite list verifies the implementation and the boundary p=7 but does not supply an infinitude argument. The written order proof covers all admissible pairs, including beyond the computed bound.

**Relation to earlier contributions.** The earlier main report isolates a Kummer-field tail estimate; its second report summarizes an October 2026 existence claim without independently verifying it. Neither is used here as a proved theorem. The unconditional disjunctive result and the elementary safe-prime subclass have precise established or internally proved scopes. They still leave the literal target—positive prime-relative density with the corrected Artin constant for every admissible integer—unresolved.

Artin's Conjecture on Primitive Roots

Definitions and mathematical statement

For a prime $p\nmid a$, let $\operatorname{ord}_p(a)$ be the least $m>0$ with $a^m\equiv1\pmod p$. Then $a$ is a primitive root modulo $p$ exactly when $\operatorname{ord}_p(a)=p-1$. For each integer $a\ne-1$ which is not a square, the target requires a positive prime-relative density \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\#\{p\le x:\operatorname{ord}_p(a)=p-1\}}{\pi(x)}=\delta(a)>0. \] The Artin density is expressed in the usual inclusion-exclusion convention by $\delta(a)=\sum_{m\ge1}\mu(m)/[{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_m,a^{1/m}):{\mathbb{Q}}]$, equivalently its corrected Artin-product form. The algebraic degrees encode dependencies between prime-power obstructions. The density is among primes, not among all positive integers.

Formulation and concept references: [S1].

Short English statement

Does every admissible integer generate the multiplicative group modulo a positive proportion of primes, with precisely Artin's predicted density?

Research attempt

Current frontier.
Hooley proved the predicted density conditional on suitable generalized Riemann hypotheses for the associated Kummer fields. Unconditional results for sets of possible primitive roots or for averaged variants do not establish the prescribed density for every fixed admissible $a$ [E1]. The catalogue's 2024 source concerns a two-variable variant; its introductory formulation is compatible with, but does not solve, the fixed-integer target [S1]. We pursue finite inclusion--exclusion plus a uniform tail estimate, keeping the entanglement correction in the actual field degrees.
Attack route.
Fixed-field Chebotarev gives every finite collection of power-residue tests. The missing interchange is between infinitely many such tests and the prime-counting limit. One route assumes effective Chebotarev with GRH-quality errors; another seeks cancellation after averaging over the Kummer degree. We isolate the needed tail and prove an elementary bound for its large-prime-divisor portion. This leaves a specific middle range, not an unspecified appeal to "independence of residue conditions."
Attempt.
For $p\nmid a$, define the residual index \[ i_p(a)=\frac{p-1}{\operatorname{ord}_p(a)}. \] For a prime $q$, $q\mid i_p(a)$ exactly when $q\mid p-1$ and $a^{(p-1)/q}\equiv1\pmod p$. Away from ramification this is equivalent to complete splitting of $p$ in $L_q={\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_q,a^{1/q})$. For squarefree $m$, simultaneous splitting for its prime factors is splitting in $L_m={\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_m,a^{1/m})$: these fields form the corresponding compositum. The equivalences and degree corrections are standard Kummer ingredients in [E1].

Let $P(y)=\prod_{q\le y}q$, and let $N_y(x)$ count primes $p\le x$, $p\nmid a$, with $\gcd(i_p(a),P(y))=1$. Finite inclusion--exclusion gives \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-31-39-37-3a-66-69-6e-69-74-65} N_y(x)=\sum_{m\mid P(y)}\mu(m)\pi_{\rm split}(L_m,x)+O_{a,y}(1). \end{equation} All sums are finite for fixed $y$. Chebotarev therefore gives \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{N_y(x)}{\pi(x)} =\delta_y(a):=\sum_{m\mid P(y)}\frac{\mu(m)}{[L_m:{\mathbb{Q}}]}. \] The degree computation underlying Artin's corrected density yields $\delta_y(a)\to\delta(a)>0$ for the admissible integers in the question; we retain this established algebraic computation, including its entanglement correction, as recorded in [E1]. A product assuming all fields linearly disjoint would not in general be the required constant.

Let $N_a(x)$ count actual primitive-root primes and set \[ B_a(x,y)=\#\{p\le x:p\nmid a,\ \exists q>y\text{ prime with }q\mid i_p(a)\}. \] Then $0\le N_y(x)-N_a(x)\le B_a(x,y)$. Consequently the sufficient missing estimate is \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-31-39-37-3a-74-61-69-6c} \lim_{y\to\infty}\limsup_{x\to\infty} \frac{B_a(x,y)}{\pi(x)}=0. \end{equation} To verify sufficiency rigorously, fix $\varepsilon>0$, choose $y$ with $|\delta_y-\delta|<\varepsilon$ and limiting tail at most $\varepsilon$, and then take upper and lower limits in the preceding inequalities. Both limits for $N_a(x)/\pi(x)$ lie within $2\varepsilon$ of $\delta$. Sending $\varepsilon\to0$ proves the desired limit. This does not assert that (eq197:tail) follows from the already known fixed-$y$ limits.

Large-$q$ bound. Fix $B>0$. The number of primes $p\le x$ with a prime divisor $q>\sqrt x(\log x)^B$ of $i_p(a)$ is $o(\pi(x))$. The hypotheses on $a$ imply $|a|\ge2$. Such a prime has \[ \operatorname{ord}_p(a)\le\frac{p-1}{q}<M:=\frac{\sqrt x}{(\log x)^B}. \] Discarding the $O(\sqrt x)$ primes $p\le\sqrt x$, all remaining ones divide the nonzero integer \[ R_M=\prod_{1\le j\le\lfloor M\rfloor}|a^j-1|. \] Since $\log|a^j-1|\le j\log|a|+\log2$, $\log R_M\ll_a M^2$. Each distinct prime above $\sqrt x$ contributes at least $\tfrac12\log x$, regardless of repeated divisibility among factors. Their number is at most \[ \frac{2\log R_M}{\log x} \ll_a\frac{x}{(\log x)^{2B+1}}=o\!\left(\frac{x}{\log x}\right). \] Together with the prime number theorem this proves the claim. Negative $a$ causes no problem because absolute values were used and no factor is zero.

Thus only the middle range \[ y<q\le\sqrt x(\log x)^B \] remains in (eq197:tail). Controlling it uniformly would finish the density argument. Ignoring the power-residue condition and merely counting $p\equiv1\pmod q$ loses the extra suppression needed in this range. Applying a fixed-field asymptotic separately to a number of fields growing with $x$ supplies no uniform error bound. Hooley's conditional method addresses precisely such uniform issues; citing it without its analytic hypotheses would not give an unconditional proof [E1].

Stress test.
The finite inclusion--exclusion identity ignores only finitely many ramified primes for fixed $y$, not an uncontrolled set growing in the final limit. The large-$q$ product counts distinct prime divisors using logarithmic size, so it never assumes the factors $a^j-1$ are coprime. The tail bound is a sufficient statement; no unsupported assertion of equivalence is needed. The elementary argument does not establish the middle-range saving, and the exact small-prime order checks in the script do not justify an exchange of $x$ and $y$ limits. A proof of infinitude without the limiting constant would still be weaker than the printed target.
Outcome.
Outcome: Conditional reduction. The exact corrected density follows from a stated uniform tail estimate. Its very large prime-divisor range is bounded unconditionally by an explicit elementary product argument; the middle range remains unresolved. The derivation is a careful reconstruction of a standard strategy, not a new unconditional proof of Artin's conjecture or a claim that the correction factor can be omitted.
