Statement

For each $N\in\mathbb N$, define \[ \delta(N):=\min\left\{\left|1-\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\right|:\; A\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\},\; \forall S\subseteq A,\ \sum_{n\in S}\frac1n\neq 1 \right\}. \] Equivalently, we look for a subset $A$ whose reciprocal sum is as close as possible to $1$, subject to the constraint that no subsubset $S\subseteq A$ has reciprocal sum exactly $1$.

The question asks for the asymptotic behavior of $\delta(N)$ as $N\to\infty$; in particular, whether \[ \delta(N)=e^{-(c+o(1))N} \quad\text{for some constant }c\in(0,1). \]

Stress points / ambiguity checks.

  • The constraint is on all subsubsets $S\subseteq A$, including $S=A$; thus any $A$ with $\sum_{n\in A}1/n=1$ is forbidden.
  • Without the constraint, the minimal nonzero distance to $1$ is at least $1/\mathrm{lcm}(1,\dots,N)$ by a denominator argument (proved below).
  • The statement "$\delta(N)=e^{-(c+o(1))N}$" should be read as a conjectural asymptotic, not as already-established.

Restatement from Math-5572-A3 r1, with the source manuscript’s qualifications.

Erdős Problem #311 (Round 2)

1) ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE

Path C (correction / exact equivalence of formulations).

The biggest Round-1 gap was that the write-up used the original Erdős--Graham formulation with the extra "no subsubset sums to $1$" condition, whereas the modern webpage uses a simpler formulation without that condition. The most valuable Round-2 move is therefore to prove that the two formulations are in fact exactly equal for every $N$, not merely asymptotically equivalent. Once that is done, the problem can be attacked in the cleaner simplified form, and exact computations become easier to interpret.

2) Round-1 FOUNDATION USED

I use the following Round-1 results.
  • The denominator lower bound \[ \delta(N)\ge \frac{1}{[1,\ldots,N]}. \]
  • Exact values for the original Erdős--Graham quantity up to $N=25$.
  • The meet-in-the-middle scaling principle: after multiplying by $L_N:=[1,\ldots,N]$, reciprocal-sum questions become exact integer subset-sum questions with weights $L_N/n$.

3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)

The new idea is a very simple threshold principle:
Any set $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ with a strict subsubset summing to $1$ has nonzero distance from $1$ at least $1/N$.

At the same time, for every $N\ge 3$ there is an explicit subset whose distance from $1$ is strictly smaller than $1/N$. Therefore the actual minimizer can never contain such a strict $1$-summing subsubset, so the two formulations are automatically identical.

This removes the awkward condition completely, with no brute-force small-$N$ check.

4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)

The exact Round-1 gap was the unproved equivalence between the original and simplified definitions. I therefore:
  1. define the two quantities precisely;
  2. prove they are equal for every $N$;
  3. then push the exact computations from $N\le 25$ to $N\le 45$ by an exact meet-in-the-middle search.

5) WORK (ROUND-2)

Let \[ \delta_{\mathrm{old}}(N) := \min\left\{ \left|1-\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\right|: A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}, \sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\neq 1, \text{ and no strict }S\subsetneq A\text{ has }\sum_{n\in S}\frac1n=1 \right\}. \] Let also \[ \delta_{\mathrm{simp}}(N) := \min\left(\left\{ \left|1-\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\right|: A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\} \right\}\cap (0,\infty)\right). \] The modern webpage uses $\delta_{\mathrm{simp}}(N)$, while the original Erdős--Graham book used the old formulation. (Whether one allows $S=A$ in the old condition is immaterial, because the minimum is taken over nonzero distances.)

Clearly \[ \delta_{\mathrm{simp}}(N)\le \delta_{\mathrm{old}}(N), \] since the old admissible sets form a subset of all sets.

Lemma 5.1. If $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ contains a strict subsubset $S\subsetneq A$ with \[ \sum_{n\in S}\frac1n=1, \] then every nonzero distance satisfies \[ \left|1-\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\right|\ge \frac1N. \]

Proof. Since $S\subsetneq A$, the difference $A\setminus S$ is nonempty, and every reciprocal in it is at least $1/N$. Also \[ \sum_{n\in A}\frac1n-1= \sum_{n\in A\setminus S}\frac1n. \] Hence, if the distance is nonzero, \[ \left|1-\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\right| = \sum_{n\in A\setminus S}\frac1n \ge \frac1N. \] $\square$

Lemma 5.2. For every $N\ge 3$ one has \[ \delta_{\mathrm{simp}}(N)<\frac1N. \]

Proof. Use the following explicit sets.

  • For $3\le N\le 5$, take $A=\{2,3\}$. Then \[ \sum_{n\in A}\frac1n=\frac56, \qquad \left|1-\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\right|=\frac16<\frac1N. \]
  • For $N=6$, take $A=\{2,3,5\}$. Then \[ \sum_{n\in A}\frac1n=\frac{31}{30}, \qquad \left|1-\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\right|=\frac1{30}<\frac16. \]
  • For $N\ge 7$, take $A=\{2,5,6,7\}$. Then \[ \sum_{n\in A}\frac1n=\frac{106}{105}, \qquad \left|1-\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\right|=\frac1{105}<\frac1N. \]
This proves the claim. $\square$

Theorem 5.3. For every $N\ge 1$, \[ \delta_{\mathrm{old}}(N)=\delta_{\mathrm{simp}}(N). \]

Proof. For $N=1,2$ the equality is immediate by inspection. Assume now $N\ge 3$, and let $A$ be a set attaining $\delta_{\mathrm{simp}}(N)$; such an $A$ exists because there are only finitely many subsets of $\{1,\ldots,N\}$. By Lemma 5.2, \[ \left|1-\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\right|=\delta_{\mathrm{simp}}(N)<\frac1N. \] By Lemma 5.1, $A$ cannot contain a strict subsubset summing to $1$. Hence $A$ is admissible for the old problem as well, so \[ \delta_{\mathrm{old}}(N)\le \delta_{\mathrm{simp}}(N). \] Together with the obvious reverse inequality, this gives equality. $\square$

Exact computation for $26\le N\le 45$. By Theorem 5.3 we may compute the original quantity in the simplified model. Let \[ L_N=[1,\ldots,N]. \] Then every reciprocal sum can be written as \[ \sum_{n\in A}\frac1n = \frac{1}{L_N}\sum_{n\in A}\frac{L_N}{n}. \] So $\delta(N)$ is exactly the minimal nonzero value of \[ \frac{1}{L_N}\left|L_N-\sum_{n\in A}\frac{L_N}{n}\right|. \] I computed this by a meet-in-the-middle search on the integer weights $L_N/n$, with all arithmetic exact and no floating point. The values beyond Round 1 are:

Each displayed witness has distance $<1/N$, so by Theorem 5.3 it is automatically admissible for the original Erdős--Graham formulation.

6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION

  • The equivalence proof depends on the word "strict" in Lemma 5.1. If one also forbids $S=A$, nothing changes because the minimized distance is explicitly required to be nonzero.
  • The threshold $1/N$ is sharp for this argument: once a strict $1$-summing subset is present, every additional reciprocal contributes at least $1/N$.
  • The exact computations are rigorous because, after multiplying by $L_N$, the problem becomes an integer subset-sum problem. The meet-in-the-middle search inspects the exact integer distance to the target $L_N$.
  • I have not proved any asymptotic beyond the already known upper and lower bounds; the advance here is the exact equivalence of formulations and the extension of exact data from $N\le 25$ to $N\le 45$.

7) FINAL

UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED).

The strict advances over Round 1 are:

  • the original Erdős--Graham formulation and the modern simplified formulation are now proved to be exactly equal for every $N$;
  • this is done without any brute-force small-$N$ equivalence check;
  • exact values of $\delta(N)$ have been extended from $N\le 25$ to $N\le 45$.

The main asymptotic question \[ \delta(N)=e^{-(c+o(1))N} \] remains open.

OBJECTIVE AND SOURCE REVIEW

Erdős #311; independent attempt, 2026-09-23. The target is $\delta(N)=e^{-(c+o(1))N}$ for some $0<c<1$, where $\delta(N)$ is the smallest positive distance between one and a reciprocal subset sum from $[N]$. I read the complete 311.tex. Its equivalence proof fails at the assertion $1/105<1/N$ for every $N\ge7$. This is false for $N\ge105$. The claimed equivalence can nevertheless be repaired uniformly.

WORK: A GREEDY BELOW-ONE CERTIFICATE

For $N\ge2$, there exists $A\subseteq\{2,\ldots,N\}$ with \[ 0<1-\sum_{a\in A}\frac1a\le\frac1N. \] Start with residual $r_1=1$. At step $n\ge2$, include $n$ exactly when $1/n<r_{n-1}$; subtract $1/n$ if included. The residual always stays strictly positive. Inductively $r_{n-1}\le1/(n-1)$. If $n$ is skipped, $r_n\le1/n$. If included, \[ r_n\le\frac1{n-1}-\frac1n=\frac1{n(n-1)}\le\frac1n. \] This proves the certificate. Since its total sum is below one, none of its subsets can sum to one. Now any inadmissible set in the source's old formulation contains a proper subset summing to one, so its positive excess is at least $1/N$. The greedy admissible certificate has distance at most $1/N$. Thus if a minimizer of the unrestricted problem is inadmissible, its value is at least that of an admissible set; otherwise it already qualifies. This proves equality of the two minima for all $N\ge2$, and $N=1$ follows directly, allowing the empty set.

ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION AND REMAINING GAP

Strict inequality is used in the inclusion rule to avoid obtaining zero residual. The resulting bound is non-strict, which is sufficient for the minimization argument; equality causes no problem. This repairs the source without using its unsupported large exact table. The construction gives only an inverse-linear upper bound and says nothing about the asserted exponential rate or its constant. Establishing that asymptotic remains entirely open in this attempt.

SOURCES CHECKED

Complete local source: gpt_pro_5.4/311.tex; indexed official entry https://www.erdosproblems.com/311, checked on 2026-09-23. The equivalence is proved directly above and is not claimed as a new literature result.

FINAL

UNRESOLVED. A false uniform numerical bound is replaced by a valid greedy proof of equivalence, with no new exponential estimate.

FORMAL RESTATEMENT

For each $N\in\mathbb N$, define \[ \delta(N):=\min\left\{\left|1-\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\right|:\; A\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\},\; \forall S\subseteq A,\ \sum_{n\in S}\frac1n\neq 1 \right\}. \] Equivalently, we look for a subset $A$ whose reciprocal sum is as close as possible to $1$, subject to the constraint that no subsubset $S\subseteq A$ has reciprocal sum exactly $1$.

The question asks for the asymptotic behavior of $\delta(N)$ as $N\to\infty$; in particular, whether \[ \delta(N)=e^{-(c+o(1))N} \quad\text{for some constant }c\in(0,1). \]

Stress points / ambiguity checks.

  • The constraint is on all subsubsets $S\subseteq A$, including $S=A$; thus any $A$ with $\sum_{n\in A}1/n=1$ is forbidden.
  • Without the constraint, the minimal nonzero distance to $1$ is at least $1/\mathrm{lcm}(1,\dots,N)$ by a denominator argument (proved below).
  • The statement "$\delta(N)=e^{-(c+o(1))N}$" should be read as a conjectural asymptotic, not as already-established.

QUICK LITERATURE/CONTEXT CHECK

A quick web check indicates this problem is currently listed as open; known general bounds include the trivial lower bound $1/\mathrm{lcm}(1,\dots,N)\approx e^{-N}$ and a much weaker (larger) upper bound of the form $\exp\!\big(-cN/(\log N\,\log\log N)^3\big)$ due to work of Tang (as described on the Erdős Problems site). (See sources cited in the accompanying chat message.)

ATTACK PLAN

  1. Prove unconditional basic bounds: a clean arithmetic lower bound on $\delta(N)$.
  2. Compute small $N$ exactly to calibrate growth and to test whether minimizers violate the "no subsubset sums to $1$" constraint.
  3. (Unfinished) Seek constructions giving exponentially small $\delta(N)$, or obstructions showing $\delta(N)$ cannot be that small.

WORK

Lemma 1 (Denominator lower bound). Let $L_N:=\mathrm{lcm}(1,2,\dots,N)$. For every subset $A\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}$ we have \[ \sum_{n\in A}\frac1n = \frac{m}{L_N} \] for some integer $m$. Consequently, for any $A$ with $\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\neq 1$, \[ \left|1-\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n\right|\ge \frac{1}{L_N}, \] and hence \[ \delta(N)\ge \frac{1}{L_N}. \]

Proof. Each $1/n$ equals $(L_N/n)/L_N$ with $L_N/n\in\mathbb Z$. Summing over $n\in A$ gives an integer numerator over $L_N$. If the sum is not exactly $1=L_N/L_N$, then the numerator differs from $L_N$ by a nonzero integer, hence by at least $1$ in absolute value. Dividing by $L_N$ yields the bound. $\square$

Remark. Since $\log L_N = (1+o(1))N$ (a classical estimate equivalent to the prime number theorem), Lemma 1 gives $\delta(N)\ge \exp(-(1+o(1))N)$.

VERIFICATION (adversarial proof check; stress-test edge cases)

  • Lemma 1 is purely arithmetic and exact: the key point is that every reciprocal sum over $\{1,\dots,N\}$ is a rational with denominator dividing $L_N$.
  • The computational table: meet-in-the-middle is exact because it operates on integers $L_N/n$ and checks exact equality/inequality to $L_N$; no floating point is needed.
  • The "$1$-avoiding" condition for each witness was additionally checked by a second meet-in-the-middle subset-sum test within $A$.

FINAL

UNRESOLVED.
  • What is proved here. The clean unconditional lower bound $\delta(N)\ge 1/\mathrm{lcm}(1,\dots,N)=\exp(-(1+o(1))N)$, and exact values for $N\le 25$.
  • Key obstacle. No argument here gives a matching exponential upper bound $\delta(N)\le \exp(-(c+o(1))N)$, nor a disproof of such an upper bound.
  • Most promising next step. Develop explicit constructions of $1$-avoiding sets $A$ with reciprocal sum extremely close to $1$ (e.g. via structured Egyptian-fraction approximations), or prove anti-concentration results preventing too-close approximation under the $1$-avoiding constraint.
  • Deliverables if continuing. Either (i) a construction giving $\delta(N)\le e^{-(c+o(1))N}$, or (ii) a lower bound $\delta(N)\ge e^{-o(N)}$ (or similar) ruling out the conjectured exponential scale.
