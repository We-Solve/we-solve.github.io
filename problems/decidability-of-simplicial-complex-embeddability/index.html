A complete general-position regime and its failed extension

The primary report already records polynomial and undecidable regimes of piecewise-linear embeddability. We prove the elementary always-embeddable regime $d\ge2k+1$, then test whether the same placement argument extends one dimension lower. Let $K$ be a finite simplicial complex of dimension at most $k\ge1$, with vertices numbered $1,\ldots,N$.
Proposition: Mapping vertex $i$ to $(i,i^2,\ldots,i^{2k+1})$ and extending affinely over each simplex gives a piecewise-linear embedding of $K$ in $\mathbb R^{2k+1}$.
Proof: Any $r\le2k+2$ distinct image vertices are affinely independent. Indeed the first $r$ rows of their homogeneous coordinate matrix are the powers $0,1,\ldots,r-1$; its Vandermonde determinant is $\prod_{i<j}(t_j-t_i)\ne0$. This identity follows because the determinant is an alternating polynomial of degree $r(r-1)/2$, divisible by each difference, and comparison of the leading product coefficient gives constant one. Each simplex has at most $k+1$ vertices, so its affine map is injective. The union of the vertex sets of two simplices has at most $2k+2$ elements and is affinely independent. If their image convex hulls intersect, equality of the two convex combinations and uniqueness of affine coordinates on that union force every nonshared vertex coefficient to vanish. The intersection is therefore exactly the convex hull of the shared vertices, the image of their common face. Thus the map is injective on the whole realization, and it is continuous since its affine restrictions agree on faces. The finite realization is compact, and its image lies in a Hausdorff Euclidean space; a continuous bijection from a compact space to its image has continuous inverse because compact sets have closed images. This proves embedding. ∎
For fixed $k$, each coordinate is an integer of $O(k\log(N+1))$ bits and can be produced with $O(k)$ multiplications per vertex. The same map into larger $d$ is obtained by zero-padding. This is already a known easy regime and settles no disputed pair of dimensions. The first attempted extension uses $(i,i^2)$ for graph vertices in the plane. For $K_4$, edges joining vertices 1 to 3 and 2 to 4 lie respectively on $y=4x-3$ and $y=6x-8$. They meet at $(5/2,7)$ in the interior of both segments. Their abstract edges are disjoint, so this placement fails. This is not a nonembeddability certificate: $K_4$ embeds with three vertices at $(0,0),(2,0),(0,2)$ and the fourth at $(1/2,1/2)$, with all six edges drawn as straight segments. The three interior segments meet only at their common interior vertex and do not cross the triangle boundary.

Verification and remaining gaps

Finite Vandermonde determinants and the exact crossing coordinates were checked. At $d=2k$, the union of two disjoint maximal faces can have $2k+2$ vertices, more than affine independence permits in that dimension. Hence the general-position proof has an actual missing premise; changing the placement might still succeed, as the $K_4$ example shows. This does not decide the lower-dimensional unresolved regimes or reproduce the source's sophisticated obstruction and undecidability theorems.

Verbatim supplied catalogue question

For fixed integers $k\leq d$, is there an algorithm that decides whether the polyhedron of a given finite simplicial complex $K$ of dimension at most $k$ admits a piecewise-linear embedding into $\mathbb R^d$?

Attempt and known regimes rederived

The target concerns embeddings after arbitrary finite subdivisions. A coordinate search on the given vertices alone tests a stronger property. We first give two elementary decidable regimes, then formulate exactly where this tempting finite-coordinate route stops.
Explicit linear embedding into $\mathbb R^{2k+1}$.
For a complex of dimension at most $k$ with $v$ vertices, assign distinct integers $t=1,\ldots,v$ and place its vertices at $p(t)=(t,t^2,\ldots,t^{2k+1})$. Every set of at most $2k+2$ such points is affinely independent. Indeed the first $r$ columns of their augmented coordinate matrix $(1,t,\ldots,t^{2k+1})$ have Vandermonde determinant $\prod_{i<j}(t_j-t_i)\ne0$ for $r$ rows. This determinant formula follows because the determinant, an alternating polynomial of total degree $r(r-1)/2$, is divisible by every $t_j-t_i$; comparison of the coefficient of $t_2t_3^2\cdots t_r^{r-1}$ gives multiplicative constant one. For any two simplices $\sigma,\tau$, their union has at most $2k+2$ vertices. Uniqueness of affine coordinates on this independent set shows that a point in both convex hulls has zero weights outside the intersection, so the hulls intersect exactly in the common face. Each simplex is nondegenerate. Linear extension on simplices is therefore continuous and injective. A continuous injective map from compact $|K|$ to a Hausdorff space is an embedding: images of closed subsets are compact and hence closed, so its inverse on the image is continuous. This proves the claim. Append zero coordinates for larger $d$. For fixed $k$, the integer coordinates have $O(k\log(v+1))$ bits, giving an explicit polynomial-size construction. This is the known general-position regime stated in the report, not a new extension of it.
Complete graph-to-line criterion.
A finite one-dimensional simplicial complex embeds in $\mathbb R$ exactly when every connected component is a path or an isolated vertex. Necessity: a vertex cannot have three incident edges, since three small incident arcs on a line must place two on the same side, where continuity makes their images overlap in an interval beyond the vertex. More explicitly, the image of each incident interval contains every point between the vertex image and a nearby distinct image point, so two such intervals cannot be disjoint away from the vertex. A cycle also cannot embed: its compact connected image would be a closed interval; remove the preimage of an interior point. The cycle minus that point is connected, whereas the interval minus it is not, contradicting the embedding homeomorphism. A finite graph of maximum degree two without cycles has only path/isolated components: following successive neighbors cannot branch or revisit a vertex and therefore exhausts a path component. Conversely place each path in a separate interval with vertices in path order and edges straight; place isolated vertices separately. Degrees, cycle detection and component traversal give a finite decision method. For zero-dimensional complexes in $\mathbb R^0$, at most one vertex is allowed; in positive-dimensional Euclidean space all finite vertex sets embed.
The finite-coordinate proposal and its exact obstruction.
For a specified finite complex $L$, use variables $p_v\in\mathbb R^d$. For each pair of faces $\sigma,\tau$, quantify nonnegative barycentric weights summing to one on each. Require that \[ \sum_{v\in\sigma}\alpha_vp_v=\sum_{v\in\tau}\beta_vp_v \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha_v=\beta_v\ \text{for all }v\in\sigma\cup\tau, \] where a weight outside its face is zero. Include pairs with $\sigma=\tau$ to enforce simplex injectivity. A coordinate assignment satisfies these conditions exactly when its simplexwise linear map is injective, by the uniqueness condition for the two represented domain points. Thus this is a finite first-order formula over the reals for linear embeddability of $L$. A decision procedure for real closed fields would decide that finite formula; that general decision theorem is an external standard dependency, not proved by the displayed encoding. Putting $L=K$ cannot decide the stated PL problem: a PL map is allowed new vertices in a subdivision, and the number of these variables is not bounded by this formula. Even testing successively larger admissible subdivisions would need a proven terminating bound or a separate certificate for NO instances before becoming a decision algorithm. None is supplied. The source itself distinguishes these notions and reports PL-versus-linear examples, so treating the original-vertex test as equivalent would erase an essential hypothesis rather than solve the question.
