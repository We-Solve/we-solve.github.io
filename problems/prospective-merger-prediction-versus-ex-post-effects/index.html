Econ-610

Prospective merger prediction versus ex-post effects

LLM status Open unverified

L41 — Monopolization - Horizontal Anticompetitive Practices Economics catalogue openaigpt 6 astra ultra 65% cataloguednot recordedOpen

Statement

Original identifier: I1 (atlas item 71). Classification: Prospective causal prediction research program. Original tractability label: Medium (editorial, not a complexity result).

Definitions and assumptions

For proposed merger $j$, define a preregistration date $t_j$, baseline information $\mathcal I_j$, and a policy $M_j\in\{0,1\}$ indicating consummation under a specified remedy regime. Let $Y_{jh}(m)$ be the market outcome $h$ periods later under policy $m$, with components for quality-adjusted prices, quantities, entry, and consumer surplus. The counterfactual specifies common macroeconomic shocks and the conduct, entry, and efficiency responses allowed under each policy. A prospective model delivers a predictive distribution $Q_j$ for the causal effect $\Delta_{jh}=Y_{jh}(1)-Y_{jh}(0)$ using only information available at $t_j$.

Each protocol must declare the time origin, period unit and horizons, including how these apply to blocked or abandoned proposals; both intervention paths and their implementation dates; remedy versions; market boundaries; and the treatment of anticipation, overlapping mergers and subsequent transactions. Scalar policy notation does not impose absence of interference. Specify a common measurable outcome representation in which subtraction across policies is defined, including treatment of changing product sets, quality and price normalization, quantity aggregation, the entry measure, and consumer-surplus units and population. Declare the domain of $L$ and any component weights or normalization used in scoring; no particular measurement index or welfare model is selected here.

The stochastic interpretation of the merger-specific contrast and its joint cross-policy law remain to be specified. Common macroeconomic shocks alone do not determine that law, and identification of separate policy-outcome distributions need not identify the distribution of their difference. A conditional average effect is a different scoring target and must not silently replace the displayed contrast. Equilibrium multiplicity must be connected explicitly to the evaluation target: a selector or probability law over equilibria requires a cross-policy convention, while a set-valued effect requires its own evaluation definition. Neither uniqueness nor a particular selection convention is imposed here.

Formal research question

Construct and validate a protocol evaluating $Q_j$ against retrospectively identified $\Delta_{jh}$, including uncertainty in that retrospective identification. For a declared loss function $L$, target \[ R_h=\mathbb E_{j\sim F_*}[L(Q_j,\Delta_{jh})], \] where $F_*$ is the specified population of proposed mergers. The protocol must declare its eligibility criteria and weights, whether it is a finite population or a superpopulation, and the joint probability law and conditioning underlying this expectation. In particular, it must distinguish averaging over proposals and future shocks from conditioning on or averaging over forecasting-model training randomness; retrospective sampling uncertainty must be distinguished from the target risk. These conditioning choices remain unspecified here. Each $Q_j$ must be measurable with respect to the information permitted at $t_j$, with the permitted training data and training cutoff declared. Characterize sufficient sampling, causal-identification, and equilibrium restrictions for identification of $R_h$. Test calibration of prespecified prediction intervals and compare models on held-out merger cohorts. Prespecify coverage levels and events, including whether coverage is marginal or conditional and componentwise or simultaneous, and how retrospective uncertainty enters the test; no one of these calibration targets is selected here. Declare the sampling unit, cohort construction, training separation and dependence structure. If only selected consummated cases are observed, derive bounds or transport corrections for the target proposal population. Distinguish consummation, case availability and selection into retrospective studies in the observation model. Any transport correction must state its supporting overlap and invariance restrictions; where these do not suffice, retain bounds or nonidentification.

Interpretation and scope

A before--after price change is not the merger's causal effect: costs, demand, and rival behavior change contemporaneously. Even a well-designed retrospective may identify effects only for consummated mergers, whereas a prospective benchmark targets cases including blocked or abandoned transactions. This selection issue requires a defined target and assumptions, not a mechanical comparison with realized prices. Forecasts should preserve their original information sets and confidence statements, avoiding revisions after the event. Static merger simulation and dynamic entry models may answer different horizon questions. Quality and product removal require welfare-consistent measurement rather than comparison of prices for a changing set of products. A benchmark can score partial predictions when the retrospective delivers an identified set, using a prespecified rule that does not reward uninformative intervals. Such a rule must be defined explicitly and distinguished from evaluation of $R_h$. Specify the retrospective observation model and distinguish a population identified set from an estimate and its sampling confidence region. Substituting an estimate into a nonlinear loss does not by itself identify the stated risk. Bounds for $R_h$ must range over jointly compatible models under the declared observation law and restrictions, rather than unrelated casewise choices of effects. A separately defined set-based score does not replace the task of identifying or bounding $R_h$. Predictive success for prices alone does not establish correct entry or innovation predictions. Model performance is an empirical quantity, not a universal yes-or-no property of economics.

Common conventions: Source mathematical conventions

Unless an entry states otherwise, agent and firm sets are finite. State and action spaces are measurable, strategies and outcome maps are measurable, probability laws are specified, and expectations exist for the targets under discussion. Infinite-horizon discount factors lie in $(0,1)$ and discounted objectives are finite. Norms, tolerances and complexity input sizes must be specified for computational comparisons. Constants or primitives that appear locally are inputs, not empirical facts asserted by this catalogue.

Identification. A statistical model $m\in\mathcal M$ implies an observable law $P_m$ and target $\theta(m)$. At law $P$, its identified set is \[ \Theta(P;\mathcal M)=\{\theta(m):m\in\mathcal M,\ P_m=P\}. \] Point identification holds when this set is a singleton, or equivalently when $P_{m_1}=P_{m_2}$ implies $\theta(m_1)=\theta(m_2)$ for all compatible models. Sharp bounds mean bounds attained, or approached when the boundary is not attained, by compatible models. Writing this set is a definition, not a solution: the substantive task is to establish informative restrictions and derive or estimate the set. An unrestricted-confounding model may give only trivial bounds.

Inference. Identification, estimability and valid uncertainty quantification are separate questions. Fix $\alpha\in(0,1)$. A confidence procedure $C_n$ over a declared law class $\mathcal P$ has asymptotic uniform coverage at level $1-\alpha$ when \[ \liminf_{n\to\infty}\inf_{P\in\mathcal P}\Pr_P\{\theta(P)\in C_n\}\geq1-\alpha. \] For set-identified targets the coverage event must be defined separately, for example coverage of the full identified set. If an entry uses identification-indexed models instead of uniquely indexed laws, the same coverage convention is applied over those models. Pointwise limits do not establish uniform validity, and asymptotic validity does not guarantee finite-sample precision. Sample sizes, dependence structures and weak-identification sequences are part of each application.

Counterfactuals. $Y_i(a)$ is a potential outcome under a specified intervention, including its timing, implementation and versions. It is not $Y_i$ conditional on voluntarily choosing $a$. A full intervention vector permits interference; shorthand scalar treatment notation does not silently impose no interference. Assignment, selection, attrition, anticipation and measurement must be specified. A claim about an instrument's local effect is not automatically a population policy effect.

Economic equilibrium. A counterfactual model includes best responses, constraints, feasibility, market clearing, state transitions and expectations consistent with the stated information. When several equilibria exist, either a declared selector is an input or the target is set-valued. Comparisons across policy regimes must use the stated selection convention. Reduced-form relationships are not presumed invariant after an equilibrium-changing intervention.

Optimization and welfare. Optimization is over the stated feasible set. If attainment is not established, $\sup$ or $\inf$ and $\varepsilon$-optimal choices replace an asserted maximizer or minimizer. For example, with value $V=\sup_{a\in\mathcal A}W(a)<\infty$, an $\varepsilon$-optimal set is $\{a\in\mathcal A:W(a)\geq V-\varepsilon\}$ for $\varepsilon>0$. Benefits, costs, risk and health or environmental outcomes can be aggregated only using declared units and conversion weights. Interpersonal utility weights, the treatment of fiscal transfers and foreign profits, discounting, and the behavioral welfare criterion are explicit inputs. A comparative-static derivative additionally requires existence and appropriate differentiability of the selected counterfactual.
