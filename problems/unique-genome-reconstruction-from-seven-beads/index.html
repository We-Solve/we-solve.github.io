Research attempt: Finite candidate elimination by a seventh power-law anchor

A conditional separation theorem for the actual summed observation.
Fix any real $\alpha<0$ and define the power-law potential of an unordered pair $P=\{x,y\}$, with multiplicities allowed, by \[ F_P(t)=\|t-x\|^\alpha+\|t-y\|^\alpha \quad(t\notin\{x,y\}). \] Suppose six specified anchors $a_1,\ldots,a_6$ and a true pair $P_*$ have only finitely many real unordered candidate pairs $P$ satisfying $F_P(a_i)=F_{P_*}(a_i)$ for all $i$, with every contact finite. Then for almost every seventh anchor $g\in\mathbb R^3$, the true unordered pair is the unique candidate satisfying the additional equality $F_P(g)=F_{P_*}(g)$. For fixed six-anchor data, the good anchors are also an open dense subset after the finitely many singular locations are removed. We now prove this, including the nonidentity and exceptional-set claims.
Distinct pairs give different functions of the anchor.
If two unordered pairs differ, some point $b$ occurs with different multiplicities, say difference $c\in\{-2,-1,1,2\}$. As $t\to b$ while avoiding $b$, their potential difference is \[ F_P(t)-F_Q(t)=c\|t-b\|^\alpha+O(1). \] The remaining terms stay bounded because their centers are different from $b$. Since $\alpha<0$, the displayed difference diverges in magnitude. Thus it is not identically zero. On $\mathbb R^3$ minus the finitely many centers it is real analytic: each squared distance is positive and the real power of a positive variable has a convergent local power series. That punctured domain is connected. Indeed two points can be joined by a broken line through an intermediate point chosen off the finitely many lines through a removed point and either endpoint; neither segment then meets a removed point.
A nonzero analytic function has a null zero set here.
For clarity, the needed elementary analytic fact is justified. If an analytic function on a connected open set vanishes on a nonempty open subset, it vanishes everywhere: the set where all its derivatives vanish is closed by continuity and open by its local power series, hence is the whole connected set. For a nonidentically zero analytic function, cover the domain by countably many small boxes on which a convergent power series is valid. On each box expand in its last coordinate about the center. At least one coefficient, an analytic function of the other coordinates, is not identically zero on the projected box; otherwise the original function would vanish on that box. Induct on dimension. In one dimension, a zero is isolated unless every Taylor coefficient there vanishes; isolated subsets of an interval are countable, by assigning a rational subinterval isolating each point. In higher dimensions, the zero set of a selected nonzero coefficient has measure zero by induction. Outside that exceptional set of the first coordinates, the one-dimensional slice is not identically zero and has a null zero set. Fubini's theorem gives measure zero on the box, and the countable cover proves the claim. The same identity argument shows that each zero set has empty interior; it is closed relative to the nonsingular domain, so is nowhere dense there.

Apply this fact to $F_P-F_{P_*}$ for each of the finitely many false candidates. Their zero sets, together with their finitely many centers, form a null set. A finite union of relatively closed nowhere dense sets is nowhere dense: inside any open ball, avoid the first by a smaller open ball and repeat for the finitely many sets. The complement is relatively open and dense. This proves the conditional separation theorem.

An entirely algebraic special case at $\alpha=-2$.
Write $q_b(t)=\|t-b\|^2$. For two different pairs $P=\{x,y\}$, $Q=\{u,v\}$, clearing denominators gives \[ p_{P,Q}(t)=(q_x+q_y)q_uq_v-(q_u+q_v)q_xq_y. \] This polynomial is not zero: otherwise the two rational functions would agree away from their poles, contrary to the singularity argument. Thus for fixed finite candidate data, the bad seventh anchors lie in the zero set of a finite product of explicit nonzero polynomials, also including the $q_b$ factors for excluded centers. This proves a nonempty Zariski-open set of valid seventh anchors for each such fixed data set. It does not on its own state a uniform polynomial in all the six-anchor data coordinates.
Joint almost-everywhere statement without an unproved projection step.
Now fix a negative rational $\alpha$ and use the external six-anchor theorem: outside an exceptional algebraic set of base tuples $z=(a_1,\ldots,a_6,x_*,y_*)\in\mathbb R^{24}$, the real candidate fiber is finite. Such a proper algebraic exceptional set is null by the analytic fact just proved, applied to a nonzero polynomial containing it. We verify the measurability needed to pass from conditional to joint statements. Consider tuples $(z,g,x,y)\in\mathbb R^{33}$ satisfying all seven contact equalities, with $(x,y)$ different from both $(x_*,y_*)$ and $(y_*,x_*)$, and with all evaluated contacts nonsingular. For each positive integer $N$, additionally require all coordinates to lie in $[-N,N]$, each evaluated anchor-to-center distance to be at least $1/N$, and the distance of the ordered candidate $(x,y)$ from each of the two true orderings to be at least $1/N$. The resulting incidence set is compact: these inequalities are closed and bounded, and the contact functions are continuous with denominators or negative powers bounded away from their singularities. Its projection onto $(z,g)$ is compact, by continuity of coordinate projection. Every false nonsingular candidate appears in one of these compact sets for sufficiently large $N$. Therefore the set $B$ of bad $(z,g)$ is a countable union of compact sets, in particular Borel measurable. No general semialgebraic projection theorem is needed for this step. For every base tuple in the external full-measure good set, the conditional separation theorem says its $g$-section of $B$ has measure zero. Fubini on bounded boxes now gives measure zero for $B$ over those bases. The excluded null set of bases times $\mathbb R^3$ is also null, by the same bounded-box argument. Hence for Lebesgue-almost every full seven-anchor and true-pair tuple, the summed-contact equations uniquely determine the unordered pair. For a finite number of ambiguous pairs, the conclusion holds simultaneously outside the finite union of their exceptional sets, when their corresponding base tuples satisfy the stated generic conditions.
A necessary boundary check.
Genericity is indispensable. If all anchors lie in the plane $z=0$, reflecting both beads of a candidate pair across that plane preserves every anchor distance and hence every summed contact for any negative exponent. Unless the pair is reflection-invariant up to exchange, the two unordered pairs differ. For example $\{(0,0,1),(1,0,2)\}$ and $\{(0,0,-1),(1,0,-2)\}$ give identical data at any number of planar anchors. This example does not refute the generic statement; it rules out interpreting "seven" as a guarantee for arbitrary anchor positions.

Scope and remaining gaps

This establishes a conditional finite-candidate elimination theorem and, using the explicitly cited six-anchor theorem, an almost-everywhere seven-anchor identifiability result for unordered pairs for each negative rational exponent. The abbreviated original record omits the exact genericity convention and the allowed pair-label and global rigid-motion symmetries. The later paper's Conjecture 1 asks for sufficiently general full tuples at exponent $-2$; a uniform Zariski-open full-tuple conclusion is not proved here. No assertion is made that maternal/paternal labels for every pair can be uniquely assigned from symmetric sums, nor that noisy data admit a unique exact solution. These distinctions prevent declaring the entire broad catalogue target solved. The complete original text and its missing-model warnings remain preserved.
