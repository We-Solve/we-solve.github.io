Statement

For $n\ge 2$, list the positive divisors of $n$ that are $>1$ in increasing order: \[ 1<d_1<d_2<\cdots<d_t=n. \] Define \[ D_n:=\{d_1,\ d_1+d_2,\ \dots,\ d_1+\cdots+d_t\}. \] Question 1: determine/estimate \[ \left|D_n\setminus \bigcup_{m<n} D_m\right|. \] Question 2: for an integer $N$, define \[ f(N):=\min\{n\ge 2: N\in D_n\} \] (if such $n$ exists; otherwise interpret $f(N)=\infty$). Is it true that $f(N)=o(N)$ as $N\to\infty$ (perhaps for almost all $N$)?

1) FORMAL RESTATEMENT.

For $n\ge 2$, list the positive divisors of $n$ that are $>1$ in increasing order: \[ 1<d_1<d_2<\cdots<d_t=n. \] Define \[ D_n:=\{d_1,\ d_1+d_2,\ \dots,\ d_1+\cdots+d_t\}. \] Question 1: determine/estimate \[ \left|D_n\setminus \bigcup_{m<n} D_m\right|. \] Question 2: for an integer $N$, define \[ f(N):=\min\{n\ge 2: N\in D_n\} \] (if such $n$ exists; otherwise interpret $f(N)=\infty$). Is it true that $f(N)=o(N)$ as $N\to\infty$ (perhaps for almost all $N$)?

2) QUICK LITERATURE/CONTEXT CHECK.

No external results are cited in the problem statement beyond the definitions. I will rely only on direct arguments and explicit computations.

3) ATTACK PLAN.

  • Establish basic identities: $|D_n|$ and the maximal element of $D_n$.
  • Find explicit obstructions (values $N$ that never lie in any $D_n$) and explicit constructions (families of $N$ that do).
  • Compute $D_n$ and $f(N)$ for small ranges to see patterns and plausible conjectures.

4) WORK.

Lemma 468.1 (size and endpoint). For $n\ge 2$ with $t=\tau(n)-1$ divisors $>1$, the set $D_n$ has size \[ |D_n|=t=\tau(n)-1, \] and its largest element is \[ \max D_n = d_1+\cdots+d_t = \left(\sigma(n)-1\right), \] where $\tau(n)$ is the number of positive divisors of $n$ and $\sigma(n)$ is the sum of positive divisors of $n$.

Proof. Each partial sum $s_j:=d_1+\cdots+d_j$ is strictly increasing in $j$ because the $d_i$ are positive, hence the $t$ partial sums are all distinct and $|D_n|=t$. The final partial sum is the sum of all divisors of $n$ except $1$, i.e. $\sigma(n)-1$. $\square$

Lemma 468.2 (the integer $4$ never occurs). One has $4\notin \bigcup_{n\ge 2} D_n$. Equivalently, $f(4)=\infty$.

Proof. Suppose for contradiction that $4\in D_n$ for some $n\ge 2$. The first divisor $d_1$ satisfies $d_1\ge 2$. If $d_1\ge 3$, then $d_1\ne 4$ (since $d_1$ must divide $n$ and any divisor $\ge 4$ forces $2$ to divide $n$, contradicting minimality of $d_1$), and also $d_1>4$ is impossible. Thus necessarily $d_1=2$. But then the next divisor $d_2$ satisfies $d_2\ge 3$, so the second partial sum is $d_1+d_2\ge 2+3=5$, and all further partial sums are even larger. Therefore no partial sum equals $4$, contradiction. $\square$

Lemma 468.3 (two-prime construction). Let $p<q$ be primes and set $n=pq$. Then \[ D_{pq}=\{p,\ p+q,\ p+q+pq\}. \] In particular, any integer of the form $N=p+q$ with primes $p<q$ lies in some $D_n$ (namely $n=pq$).

Proof. The divisors of $pq$ are $1,p,q,pq$, hence the divisors $>1$ are $p<q<pq$. The partial sums are exactly $p$, $p+q$, and $p+q+pq$. $\square$

5) VERIFICATION.

  • Lemma 468.2 depends only on the ordering of divisors $>1$ and the fact that if $n$ is even then $2$ is the smallest divisor $>1$.
  • Lemma 468.3 is an explicit divisor computation for semiprimes.
  • Computations were done by exact divisor enumeration and exact set difference/union tracking.

6) FINAL. UNRESOLVED

(i) Strongest proved partial result. We have exact structural identities $|D_n|=\tau(n)-1$ and $\max D_n=\sigma(n)-1$ (Lemma 468.1), and a proven obstruction $4\notin \bigcup_n D_n$ (Lemma 468.2). We also have a simple semiprime construction that forces a prescribed partial sum $p+q$ (Lemma 468.3). Computation up to $N\le 1000$ (searching $n\le 20000$) found that every $N\in[2,1000]\setminus\{4\}$ occurs in some $D_n$.

(ii) First gap (crisp). Prove (or refute) that $f(N)=o(N)$ as $N\to\infty$ (perhaps for almost all $N$), even under the caveat that $f(4)=\infty$. A weaker crisp target is to prove an explicit bound $f(N)\le N^{1-\varepsilon}$ for some fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and all sufficiently large admissible $N$.

(iii) Top 3 next moves.

  1. Constructive approach: given $N$, build an $n$ whose initial divisors $d_1<d_2<\cdots$ have a controlled pattern so that some initial partial sum equals $N$; then optimize this construction to make $n$ as small as possible.
  2. Study "coverage" of $\bigcup_{n\le X} D_n$: estimate how large a set of integers can be produced as partial sums up to height $X$, and whether the complement is small.
  3. Computation: push the search range (both in $n$ and in $N$) and look for $N$ with unusually large ratios $f(N)/N$; attempt to guess the true growth of $\sup_{N\le X} f(N)/N$.

(iv) Minimal counterexample structure. A counterexample to $f(N)=o(N)$ would be a sequence $N_j\to\infty$ such that any $n$ with $N_j\in D_n$ must satisfy $n\ge cN_j$ for some fixed $c>0$ (or even $n\ge N_j/\phi(N_j)$ with $\phi(N_j)\to\infty$ failing). Structurally this would mean: whenever the increasing list of divisors of $n$ is summed from the bottom, the partial sums avoid $N_j$ unless $n$ is large.
