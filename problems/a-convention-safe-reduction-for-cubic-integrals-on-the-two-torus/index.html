Math-289

A convention-safe reduction for cubic integrals on the two-torus

Status Partially solved · unsolvedmath.comLLM status Open unverified

Top Open Problems Not reported cataloguedPartially solvedOpen

Statement

Definitions and mathematical statement

Let $g$ be a smooth Riemannian metric on $T^2={\mathbb{R}}^2/(2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})^2$, with geodesic vector field $X_g$ on $TT^2$ and energy $E(x,v)=\tfrac12g_x(v,v)$. A first integral is a smooth function $F$ with $X_gF=0$. A fiberwise polynomial integral of exact degree three has an expansion $F=F_0+F_1+F_2+F_3$, with $F_j(x,tv)=t^jF_j(x,v)$ and $F_3\not\equiv0$. This means exact degree, not degree at most three. Homogeneous components are separately first integrals for the geodesic flow. Thus one may state the question for a nonzero homogeneous cubic \[ F_3(x,v)=K_x(v,v,v),\qquad K\in\Gamma(\operatorname{Sym}^3T^*T^2), \qquad \nabla_{(i}K_{jkl)}=0. \] A Killing vector field here is a vector field $V\not\equiv0$ with $\mathcal L_Vg=0$. It gives the linear first integral $I_V(x,v)=g_x(V_x,v)$ and hence the cubic $I_V^3$.

Conjecture 10.2.1, [Bo-Ko-Fo1995]. Every Riemannian metric on $T^2$ admitting a nonzero homogeneous cubic first integral admits a nonzero Killing vector field. Equivalently, an integral of exact degree three should force a nonzero linear integral. No global analyticity assumption is added to the metric or coefficient tensor.

Supplied reductions and convention warning. The research aid asserts that the degree-at-most-three version is false: for $0<\varepsilon<1/4$, \[ g_\varepsilon=\exp\left(2\varepsilon(\cos x+\cos y)\right)({\,\mathrm{d}} x^2+{\,\mathrm{d}} y^2) \] is an analytic torus metric with no nonzero Killing field, although its energy is a degree-two integral. This does not refute the exact-degree conjecture. It also gives the pure-trace reduction: if a cubic integral has the global form $F_3=E L$, where $L$ is fiberwise linear, then $L$ is a nonzero linear integral. The summary further claims a reduction for "otherwise polynomially reducible" cubics, but does not specify the global factorization hypotheses; that claim is not used to enlarge the precise pure-trace statement. The aid describes the globally irreducible, everywhere-hyperbolic branch as unresolved; its PDE terminology and hypotheses are absent.

Further questions and historical context in the same extract. The source attributes to Darboux and Whittaker the homogeneous-component observation for integrals analytic in velocities; this is understood as an expansion at the zero section. It attributes to [Kolokoltsov1983] nonexistence, on a closed surface of genus at least two, of a polynomial integral functionally independent of energy. For $S^2$ and $T^2$, its historical table concerns the smallest degree of a nontrivial polynomial integral: degrees one and two have effective classifications [Bo-Ma-Fo1998]; degrees three and four have series of examples on the sphere and partial negative results on the torus; for degrees at least five it says "nothing is known". These are dated source descriptions, not present-day conclusions.

A related version concerns a natural Lagrangian \[ L(x,v)=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}(x)v^iv^j+U(x) \] and a first integral with cubic and linear velocity terms. The source says most cited partial negative results assume $g$ flat, citing [Bialy1987, Mironov2010, De-Ko-Tr2012], while [Bialy2010] does not require that assumption. The displayed plus sign before $U$ is retained; it is the source's Lagrangian convention.

The source proposes analogous torus conjectures for every degree $d$: odd exact degree should force a Killing field; even degree should force a quadratic integral not proportional to energy. In the even case a nontriviality hypothesis is essential: powers $E^{d/2}$ are always integrals. A precise intended nontrivial version assumes ${\,\mathrm{d}} F\wedge{\,\mathrm{d}} E\not\equiv0$ and asks for a quadratic integral $Q$ with ${\,\mathrm{d}} Q\wedge{\,\mathrm{d}} E\not\equiv0$. This explicitly repairs an omitted premise rather than asserting the literal even-degree wording.

The adjacent Section 10.3 asks for further two-dimensional metrics for which all geodesics can be written using elementary functions, beyond constant-curvature and Darboux-superintegrable examples [Br-Ma-Ma2008], and for metrics with explicitly given distance functions [Tao2010]. The admissible meaning of "explicit" is not specified. The final Section 11, communicated by Rotman, defines a stationary net in a Riemannian surface as a graph with geodesic edges, valence at least three, and $\sum_e u_e=0$ at each vertex, where $u_e$ are outgoing unit tangent vectors. It defines a $\Theta$-graph as two vertices joined by three edges. No complete stationary-net existence question occurs in this record.

Short English statement

The conjecture asks whether a genuine cubic geodesic integral on a two-torus forces a continuous isometric symmetry. Exact degree three matters: energy alone would satisfy a degree-at-most-three reading. The supplied aid treats a pure-trace special case and leaves the general conjecture unresolved; higher-degree and adjacent explicit-geodesic questions are retained with their missing conventions.
