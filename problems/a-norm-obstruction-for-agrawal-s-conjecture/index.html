Verbatim supplied catalogue question

Question 1. If $X − 1$ n ≡ Xn − 1 (mod n, X r − 1) and gcd( r, n ) = 1 does this imply that

n2 ≡ 1 (mod r)when n is composite? (AKS)

Attempt through a scalar invariant

Retain positive integers $n,r$ with $n$ composite and $\gcd(n,r)=1$, and the full hypothesis $(X-1)^n=X^n-1$ modulo $(n,X^r-1)$. The source records $r^n\equiv r\pmod n$ as a consequence. We prove this including nonsquarefree $n$, then test whether it can yield the requested congruence $n^2\equiv1\pmod r$.
Finite-free norm proof.
For $r=1$ the scalar conclusion is immediate. For $r\ge2$, put $S_r(X)=1+X+\cdots+X^{r-1}$ and $A=(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)[t]/(S_r(t))$. This is free of rank $r-1$ with basis $1,t,\ldots,t^{r-2}$, because division by a monic polynomial works over any coefficient ring. For $a\in A$ let $N(a)$ be the determinant of multiplication by $a$; determinant multiplicativity gives $N(ab)=N(a)N(b)$. Multiplication by $t$ is the companion matrix of $S_r$, so its characteristic polynomial is $S_r$. This follows directly by expanding $\det(XI-M_t)$ along the companion matrix columns, or equivalently by the defining recurrence $t^{r-1}=-(1+\cdots+t^{r-2})$. Thus $N(t-1)=(-1)^{r-1}S_r(1)=(-1)^{r-1}r$.

Since $t^r=1$, substitution $t\mapsto t^n$ preserves $S_r(t)=0$: its $r$ terms permute those of $S_r$ as $\gcd(n,r)=1$. An inverse substitution uses any integer $a$ with $an\equiv1\pmod r$, so this is an algebra automorphism. Conjugating multiplication operators by this automorphism shows $N(t^n-1)=N(t-1)$. The given polynomial identity descends to $A$ because $S_r$ divides $X^r-1$. Taking determinants therefore yields \[ [(-1)^{r-1}r]^n=(-1)^{r-1}r\pmod n. \] The factor $r$ is a unit modulo $n$. After division the possible sign is $(-1)^{(r-1)(n-1)}=1$: if $n$ is odd then $n-1$ is even, while if $n$ is even then coprimality forces $r$ odd. We have proved \[ r^{n-1}\equiv1\pmod n. \] No step replaced $n$ by its radical or assumed that $A$ is a field.

Exact obstruction to finishing with the norm alone.
Take $n=217=7\cdot31$ and $r=5$. Since $5^6\equiv1\pmod7$ and $5^3=125\equiv1\pmod{31}$, and both $6,3$ divide $216$, we get $5^{216}\equiv1\pmod{217}$. For the first congruence, $5^3\equiv-1\pmod7$, so its square is one. Yet $217^2\equiv4\pmod5$, not one. Thus the scalar norm condition with coprimality and compositeness does not imply the desired conclusion. The example is not a counterexample to the original conjecture: an exact cyclic multiplication computation gives residual coefficients \[ (X-1)^{217}-(X^{217}-1) \equiv207+10X^2+159X^3+58X^4\pmod{217,X^5-1}, \] which is nonzero. The finite computation can be checked by repeated squaring with cyclic convolution of five integer residues.
Automatic periods do not test the difficult implication.
For $r$ dividing $24$, every integer coprime to $r$ has square one modulo $r$. Indeed an odd integer $2k+1$ has square $1+4k(k+1)\equiv1\pmod8$, and an integer not divisible by three has square one modulo three by its two possible nonzero residues. The Chinese remainder theorem gives the assertion for $24$, and reduction gives it for its divisors. Thus calculations at these periods cannot supply evidence for the nontrivial part of the conjecture. No classification of all other periods is needed for this observation.
