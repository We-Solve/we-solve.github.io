Math-1675

A product criterion and a bowtie obstruction for Cayley interval lattices

Status Partially solved · unsolvedmath.comLLM status Open unverified

Statement

Definitions and mathematical statement

J. McCammond's source question calls an edge-labeled structure quasi-Garside if it has the intended Garside properties except the lattice property. For a group $G$ with a finite conjugacy-closed generating set $T$, it asks for natural conditions explaining which Cayley intervals are lattices, and whether this can be understood group-theoretically rather than through a reflection representation. The full quasi-Garside axioms and inverse/positive-word convention are not given in the extract.

For the aid's concrete cases take $T=T^{-1}$ and its word metric. The geodesic interval from $1$ to $g$ has \[ [1,g]_T=\{u:\ell_T(u)+\ell_T(u^{-1}g)=\ell_T(g)\},\qquad u\le_Tv\iff\ell_T(u)+\ell_T(u^{-1}v)=\ell_T(v). \] A lattice has a unique least upper bound and greatest lower bound for every pair. Left translation reduces general Cayley intervals to these based intervals.

The aid's positive criterion fixes a marked direct product \[ G=F\times C_1\times\cdots\times C_r, \] where $F$ is any finite group and each $C_i$ is finite or infinite cyclic. Use every nonidentity element of $F$ as a generator in its coordinate, and the two standard cyclic directions in each cyclic coordinate, omitting identities/duplicates. This finite symmetric marking is conjugacy-closed. Word length is the sum of coordinate lengths, with the finite coordinate contributing $0$ or $1$. Every interval is a direct product of its coordinate intervals: a point or two-chain for $F$; a chain for an infinite cyclic coordinate; and a shortest-arc chain in a finite cycle, except that an even cycle's antipodal endpoint gives two arms with common bottom and top. The two-element cyclic case reduces to a two-chain. Each factor is a lattice, so every such marked-product interval is a lattice. This is not a claim for arbitrary semidirect products or arbitrary markings of the same group.

The counterexample retains the same hypotheses on finiteness, symmetry, and conjugacy closure. In additive ${\mathbb{Z}}^2$ use all eight king moves \[ T=\{-1,0,1\}^2\setminus\{(0,0)\},\qquad\ell_T(a,b)=\max\{|a|,|b|\}. \] In $[0,(3,0)]_T$, the atoms $(1,0),(1,1)$ have two distinct rank-two common upper bounds $(2,0),(2,1)$. Both are minimal common upper bounds, so the atoms have no join; dually those rank-two elements have no meet. Thus the interval is not a lattice. The standard axial marking on ${\mathbb{Z}}^2$, covered by the positive criterion, instead gives lattice intervals, showing that the marked pair $(G,T)$ matters. This does not rule out group-structural criteria within more restricted natural marking classes.

The original remark credits Brady--Watt with a uniform lattice proof for $NC_W$ using the real-reflection realization and asks how far methods independent of that realization extend. The aid's direct-product family and bowtie obstruction do not supply a general classification of quasi-Garside interval lattices.

Short English statement

The supplied result classifies intervals for one explicit direct-product marking and gives a nonlattice king-move interval in ${\mathbb{Z}}^2$. It shows that finite conjugacy-closed generators alone do not force the lattice property, and that the chosen marking matters even for a fixed abelian group.
