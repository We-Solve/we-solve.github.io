Recommended replacement problem statement

1. Definitions and domain

Let $\mathfrak G$ be the domain of all finite normal-form games (with any finite number of players, finite nonempty action sets, and real payoffs). For $G=(I,(A_i)_{i\in I},(u_i)_{i\in I})\in\mathfrak G$, write \[ \Sigma_G=\prod_{i\in I}\Delta(A_i),\qquad \operatorname{NE}(G)\subseteq\Sigma_G, \] and \[ \mathcal C(G)=\{K\subseteq\operatorname{NE}(G):K\ne\varnothing,\ K\text{ is closed and connected}\}. \] Because $\operatorname{NE}(G)$ is compact, every $K\in\mathcal C(G)$ is compact. A solution concept is a correspondence $\Phi$ satisfying \[ \varnothing\ne\Phi(G)\subseteq\mathcal C(G) \qquad\text{for every }G\in\mathfrak G. \tag{1}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-63-6f-72-72-65-73-70-6f-6e-64-65-6e-63-65} \] No hypothesis says that the family $\Phi(G)$ is itself closed in the Hausdorff hyperspace.

Use exactly the following fixed reference: Govindan--Wilson [gw, Definition 2.4; Axioms 3.1, 4.7, 5.4], arXiv:2504.16908v2. The stability solution family $\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)$ is Mertens's characteristic-zero stability, i.e. rational Čech-cohomological essentiality together with the stated connexity requirement. The superscript $0$ concerns the characteristic of the coefficient field and is unrelated to an equilibrium's fixed-point index.

For clarity, the source's constrained-strategy perturbations use \[ P_\varepsilon(G)=\{\delta\tau:0\le\delta\le\varepsilon, \ \tau\in\Sigma_G\}, \qquad \Sigma_i(\eta)=\{x_i\in\Delta(A_i):x_i(a)\ge\eta_i(a) \ \forall a\}. \tag{2}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-70-65-72-74-75-72-62} \] Writing $G(\eta)$ for the constrained game, put \[ E_G=\{(\eta,x):\eta\in P_1(G),\ x\in\operatorname{NE}(G(\eta))\}, \qquad \pi_G(\eta,x)=\eta. \] If $X\subseteq E_G$, let \[ X_\varepsilon=X\cap\pi_G^{-1}(P_\varepsilon(G)),\quad \partial X_\varepsilon=X\cap\pi_G^{-1}(\partial P_\varepsilon(G)), \quad X_0=X\cap\pi_G^{-1}(0). \] The boundary $\partial P_\varepsilon(G)$ is relative to the affine (or, equivalently, linear) span of the perturbation polytope. Its dimension is $d=1+\sum_i(|A_i|-1)$. Conditions involving $X_0$, connexity and the induced rational cohomology maps must be read literally as in the cited Definition 2.4; the summary in $2$ is not a replacement definition of stability.

2. Exact axioms

Require $\Phi$ to satisfy all three axioms globally, not just at the game used to test the conclusion:
  1. Backward induction $B$. For every perfect-recall extensive-form representation $\Gamma$ whose normal form is $G$, every $K\in\Phi(G)$ contains a mixed profile equivalent to a quasi-perfect equilibrium of $\Gamma$, in the precise sense of [gw, Definition 2.1 and Axiom 3.1].
  2. Strongly irrelevant dominated strategies $D^*$. For every strategy extension allowed by [gw, Definition 4.4], the two solution families coincide under the specified face identification, as demanded in [gw, Axiom 4.7]. Weak dominance by itself is not sufficient for a comparison to be admissible.
  3. Strong-embedding invariance $I^*$. If a game $H$ strongly embeds $G$ through $f$ satisfying [gw, Definition 5.2], then \[ \Phi(G)=\{f(L):L\in\Phi(H)\}, \tag{3}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-65-6d-62-65-64-64-69-6e-67} \] using the source's mapping of equilibrium profiles and their connected sets. This is equality of families, containing both an image condition and an existence-of-lifts condition.
No additional axioms (admissibility, genericity, strict equilibrium, closedness of $\Phi(G)$, weak embedding, arbitrary payoff limits) are silently imposed.

3. Principal question S1: nongeneric containment

Prove or disprove the following implication: \[ \boxed{\begin{gathered} \forall\Phi:\mathfrak G\rightrightarrows\mathcal C\quad \left[\Phi\text{ satisfies }(1),B,D^*,I^*\right]\\[-2pt] \Longrightarrow\quad \forall G\in\mathfrak G\ \forall K\in\Phi(G)\quad \exists S\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G):\ S\subseteq K. \end{gathered}} \tag{S1}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-53-31} \] There is no genericity, regularity, isolation, or finite-equilibrium-set restriction on the target game. A positive solution must treat non-isolated equilibrium sets and the actual rational-cohomological stability witness; an argument using only topological robustness or existence of equilibria of perturbed games does not suffice. A negative solution must give a globally defined solution concept satisfying $1$, $B,D^*,I^*$ in all relevant comparison games, and a particular $G,K$ with $K\in\Phi(G)$ but without any $S\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)$ contained in $K$. A counterexample set in one isolated game, without constructing a compatible global rule, is insufficient.

4. Known theorem and the exact missing step

Govindan--Wilson's Theorem 6.1 proves the containment conclusion for generic perfect-recall extensive-form games, for any finite number of players. Their Section 10 observes that nongeneric perturbation graphs can have higher dimension and that the fixed-point (coincidence) property used in the generic proof does not automatically imply the required rational-cohomological essentiality.

The original target is containment, not either stronger demand \[ \Phi(G)\subseteq\operatorname{Stab}_0(G),\qquad \Phi(G)=\operatorname{Stab}_0(G). \] Nor does the assertion refer only to components with nonzero fixed-point index. Replacing Čech essentiality by homotopy essentiality would define a different problem.

Close variations and reductions

The following variants are proposed research questions and reductions, not asserted to be unresolved by a comprehensive literature review. They all keep the global axioms unchanged unless they explicitly say otherwise.

V1. Two-player nongeneric containment

Restrict the tested game in $S1$, but not the domain or axioms of $\Phi$, to games with two players: \[ \forall\Phi\ [B,D^*,I^*]\Longrightarrow \forall G\in\mathfrak G\ (|I(G)|=2)\ \forall K\in\Phi(G) \ \exists S\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G):S\subseteq K. \tag{V1} \] The known generic two-player case is not the whole of $V1$. This is a natural first setting in which to search for a nongeneric counterexample or a stronger 2-player argument.

V2. Bounded players and actions

For integers $n\ge2$, $m\ge2$, ask the same conclusion for every tested game with at most $n$ players and at most $m$ actions per player: \[ \forall\Phi\ [B,D^*,I^*]\Longrightarrow \forall G:\ \left(|I(G)|\le n,\,\max_i|A_i|\le m\right) \quad\forall K\in\Phi(G)\quad \exists S\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G): S\subseteq K. \tag{V2$_{n,m}$} \] Crucially, $\Phi$ is still defined on all finite games because an allowed strong embedding may introduce additional players or actions. Establish the smallest $(n,m)$ for which a counterexample exists, if $S1$ fails, or prove a nontrivial bounded-size positive theorem.

V3. A boundary-degenerate target subclass

Define $\mathfrak G_{\partial}$ to be games $G$ satisfying \[ \operatorname{NE}(G)\cap\operatorname{relint}\Sigma_G=\varnothing, \qquad \operatorname{StrictNE}(G)=\varnothing. \] Ask whether $S1$ holds whenever the target $G$ belongs to $\mathfrak G_{\partial}$, with the axioms still imposed on all games. The restrictions deliberately remove two straightforward sufficient cases: a completely mixed equilibrium in $K$, and a strict equilibrium in $K$. They do not assume that no other stability witness exists. This is a focused candidate hard class, not a claim that all genuinely hard cases can be reduced to it.

V4. Global pruning: an equivalent finite-stage elimination target

One can define the largest globally admissible rule without assuming $S1$. Write $Q_\Gamma$ for the mixed profiles equivalent to quasi-perfect equilibria of a representation $\Gamma$, and let \[ \mathcal A_0(G)=\{K\in\mathcal C(G):K\cap Q_\Gamma\ne\varnothing \text{ for every representation }\Gamma\}. \] For each exact allowed comparison $e:H\to G$, denote the induced continuous map on compact connected equilibrium sets by $e_*$. Define the downward compact recursion \[ \mathcal A_{r+1}(G)=\mathcal A_r(G) \cap\bigcap_{e:G\to F}e_*^{-1}(\mathcal A_r(F)) \cap\bigcap_{e:H\to G}e_*(\mathcal A_r(H)), \qquad \mathcal A_\infty(G)=\bigcap_{r\ge0}\mathcal A_r(G). \tag{4}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-70-72-75-6e-65} \] The accompanying partial-contribution manuscript claims a proof, using the source's established all-game stability axioms, that $\mathcal A_\infty$ is a nonempty globally axiom-compatible rule and every other such rule is included in it. These manuscript claims have not been independently verified. Under those claimed results, $S1$ would be equivalent to \[ \boxed{\forall G\quad \forall K\in\mathcal C(G)\quad \left[K\text{ contains no }0\text{-stable subset}\right] \Longrightarrow\exists r<\infty: K\notin\mathcal A_r(G).} \tag{V4}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-56-34} \] This formulation does not assert a computable depth bound. Each stage still ranges over generally infinitely many permitted representations and comparisons. Prove the displayed elimination or find a survivor that violates it.

V5. Is a continuous equilibrium section necessary for stable singletons?

For a fixed finite game $G$ and $x\in\operatorname{NE}(G)$, define \[ \operatorname{Sec}(G,x) \iff \exists\rho\in(0,1)\ \exists\text{ continuous }s:P_\rho(G)\to\Sigma_G :s(0)=x,\ s(\eta)\in\operatorname{NE}(G(\eta))\ \forall\eta. \] The accompanying contribution claims a proof of $\operatorname{Sec}(G,x)\Rightarrow\{x\}\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)$; that proof has not been independently verified. Determine whether the converse holds in any specified class of games, or supply a finite-game counterexample: \[ \{x\}\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)\ \stackrel{?}{\Longrightarrow}\ \operatorname{Sec}(G,x). \tag{V5} \] This is an independent strengthening of the witness form for singleton stability, not equivalent to $S1$. The existence of a cohomologically essential subset need not yield a section for arbitrary topological maps, so a direct argument from essentiality alone would be invalid without further game structure.

V6. Metastability and a minimal strengthening of invariance

Govindan--Wilson's Section 10 discusses metastability and suggests examining stronger combined invariance principles, potentially adding both players and dominated actions in one comparison. A precise version must first explicitly define the new class $\mathscr E_{\rm comp}$ of allowed composite embeddings, including its off-path preference requirements. Then ask: \[ B+D^*+I^*+I_{\mathscr E_{\rm comp}} \quad\stackrel{?}{\Longrightarrow}\quad\text{(S1)}. \tag{V6} \] The condition that composite embeddings have the required properties must not be replaced by arbitrary embeddings. Until $\mathscr E_{\rm comp}$ is specified, $V6$ is a research programme, not a single theorem-or-counterexample problem. An alternative route is to fix the original literature's metastability rule and determine the exact extent of its strong-embedding invariance.

V7. Exact stable-set selection and minimality axioms

A strictly stronger conclusion than $S1$ would require every selected set to be itself $0$-stable: \[ \forall G\in\mathfrak G\ \forall K\in\Phi(G):\quad K\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G). \tag{V7a} \] One may alternatively seek an explicit, strategically motivated minimality axiom $M$ such that $B+D^*+I^*+M$ implies $V7a$, or even $\Phi(G)=\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)$ for every finite $G$. Merely stating "choose the minimal stable sets" is not an axiomatic characterization: it includes the desired answer in the assumption. Thus a well-posed version of the programme must specify $M$ independently and verify that $\operatorname{Stab}_0$ satisfies it. The new axiom could be tested first on nongeneric games whose Nash set has multiple components or positive-dimensional faces.

Special-case boundaries described in the accompanying manuscript

The accompanying manuscript claims proofs of the following sufficient conditions from the definition, without resolving $S1$. These claims remain subject to independent review:
  1. Every strict Nash equilibrium and every completely mixed Nash equilibrium has a $0$-stable singleton, using an explicit continuous constrained-equilibrium section.
  2. If $x,y\in\operatorname{NE}(G)$, $y$ is completely mixed, and $[x,y]\subseteq\operatorname{NE}(G)$, then $\{x\}\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)$.
  3. For binary-action zero-sum polymatrix games with $u_i(a)=a_i\sum_jA_{ij}a_j$, $A^\mathsf T=-A$, $A\mathbf 1=0$, every equilibrium singleton is $0$-stable; equilibrium continua can be non-isolated and contain no strict equilibria.
The accompanying manuscript offers two-player payoff-limit examples intended to show that stability of a singleton need not pass to a limit of changing games, and a Hopf-map example intended to show that a coincidence property alone need not force rational-cohomological essentiality. These arguments remain subject to independent review; neither is presented as a game-theoretic counterexample to $S1$.

Acceptance criteria and possible output

For a full proof: specify a $0$-stable subset of every permitted selected set, with a witness satisfying both parts of Definition 2.4 (or invoke an applicable theorem implying them). All cross-game quantifiers must be respected.
For a full disproof: give an explicit rule on all finite games, verify nonemptiness, $B,D^*,I^*$ for every permitted comparison, then give one violating $G,K$.
For a partial contribution: prove a nontrivial nongeneric subclass, an exact reduction, a barrier or a rigorously specified counterexample to an intermediate assertion. Keep the missing global step visible.

The two-player question is \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-74-61-72-67-65-74} \forall\,G=(A,B)\text{ finite bimatrix game}\quad \forall K\in\Phi(G)\quad \exists S\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)\quad S\subseteq K. \end{equation} The sizes $m\times n$ are arbitrary. The perturbed feasible set is \[ \Sigma_i(\eta)=\{x_i\in\Delta(A_i):x_i(a)\ge\eta_i(a)\text{ for every }a\}. \] Let $E_G$ be the graph of Nash equilibria of these constrained games over $P_1$, with projection $\pi$. Boundaries of $P_\epsilon$ are relative to its linear span, which has dimension \[ d=1+\sum_i(|A_i|-1). \] The pair $(P_\epsilon,\partial P_\epsilon)$ is homeomorphic to $(D^d,S^{d-1})$. For $X\subseteq E_G$, write \[ X_\epsilon=X\cap\pi^{-1}(P_\epsilon),\qquad \partial X_\epsilon=X\cap\pi^{-1}(\partial P_\epsilon). \] The notation $\partial X_\epsilon$ means this inverse image, not an intrinsic boundary chosen for $X$.

We use the standard closed-witness formulation of Mertens $0$-stability: a closed $X\subseteq E_G$ with $X_0=\{0\}\times S$, nonzero pullback \[ \pi^*:\check H^d(P_\epsilon,\partial P_\epsilon;\mathbb Q) \longrightarrow\check H^d(X_\epsilon,\partial X_\epsilon;\mathbb Q) \] for a positive $\epsilon$, and the source's connexity condition. Connexity requires that, for every neighborhood $V$ of $X_0$ in $X$, some component of $V\setminus\partial X_1$ have closure a neighborhood of $X_0$ in $X$. These are the strategy-perturbation and rational-cohomology conventions of [gw, Definition 2.4], with the closed-witness convention made explicit; see also the definition framework of Govindan--Mertens [gm].

Lemma (Continuous equilibrium section): Suppose $z\in\operatorname{NE}(G)$ and, for some $0<\rho<1$, a continuous map $s:P_\rho\to\Sigma$ satisfies \[ s(0)=z,\qquad s(\eta)\in\operatorname{NE}(G(\eta))\quad(\eta\in P_\rho). \] Then $\{z\}\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)$.
Proof: Take $X=\{(\eta,s(\eta)): \eta\in P_\rho\}$. This is compact, is contained in $E_G$, and has zero fiber $\{(0,z)\}$. For every $0<\epsilon\le\rho$, projection is a homeomorphism of pairs \[ (X_\epsilon,\partial X_\epsilon)\longrightarrow (P_\epsilon,\partial P_\epsilon). \] The induced degree-$d$ map is therefore an isomorphism between copies of $\mathbb Q$, and in particular is nonzero.

Let $V$ be a neighborhood of $(0,z)$ in $X$. There is $0<\kappa<\rho$ such that the graph over $P_\kappa$ lies in $V$. The graph over \[ U_\kappa=\{\eta:0<\delta(\eta)<\kappa,\ \eta_i(a)>0\ \forall i,a\} \] is connected: its base is homeomorphic to $(0,\kappa)\times\operatorname{relint}\Sigma$. It lies in $V\setminus\partial X_1$. Strictly positive directions are dense in $P_\kappa$, so its closure contains the graph over $P_{\kappa/2}$. Consequently, the component of $V\setminus\partial X_1$ containing that graph has closure containing a neighborhood of $(0,z)$ in $X$. This verifies connexity separately from cohomological essentiality. ∎

Lemma (Necessary local robustness): If $S\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)$, then for every open neighborhood $W$ of $S$ there is $\rho>0$ such that every $\eta\in P_\rho$ with $0<\delta(\eta)$ and all coordinates strictly positive has a constrained equilibrium in $W$.
Proof: Let $X$ be a closed stability witness and choose an $\epsilon>0$ for which the cohomological pullback is nonzero. Its projection contains every interior point $a$ of $P_\epsilon$. Otherwise it factors through \[ (P_\epsilon\setminus\{a\},\partial P_\epsilon), \] whose relative degree-$d$ cohomology is zero: radial projection away from $a$ retracts the punctured polytope onto its boundary. The induced pullback would then be zero, a contradiction.

Next, all witness equilibria over sufficiently small common masses lie in $W$. If not, there are $(\eta^k,z^k)\in X$ with $\eta^k\to0$ and $z^k\notin W$. Compactness supplies a subsequence converging to $(0,z)\in X$ with $z\notin W$. But the zero fiber is $S\subset W$. This is impossible. Combining this observation with surjectivity onto the interior of $P_\epsilon$ proves the assertion. Essentiality at one $\epsilon$ suffices; no unproved passage of essentiality to smaller witnesses is used. ∎

A sufficient stability witness for a connected set

Let $\Sigma=\prod_i\Delta(A_i)$ and \[ P_\rho=\{\delta\tau:0\le\delta\le\rho,\ \tau\in\Sigma\},\qquad \Sigma_i(\eta)=\{x_i\in\Delta(A_i):x_i(a)\ge\eta_i(a)\}. \] The common mass is $\delta(\eta)=\sum_a\eta_i(a)$, independent of $i$. Write $E_G$ for the equilibrium graph of the constrained games and $\pi(\eta,x)=\eta$. The perturbation polytope has dimension $d=1+\sum_i(|A_i|-1)$; its boundary is relative to its linear span. For a witness $X\subseteq E_G$, the symbol $\partial X_\epsilon$ means $X\cap\pi^{-1}(\partial P_\epsilon)$, not an independently chosen boundary.
Lemma (A continuous family of sections): Let $K\subseteq\operatorname{NE}(G)$ be nonempty, compact and connected. Suppose that, for some $0<\rho<1$, a jointly continuous map \[ s:K\times P_\rho\longrightarrow\Sigma \] satisfies $s(z,0)=z$ and $s(z,\eta)\in\operatorname{NE}(G(\eta))$ for every $(z,\eta)$. Then $K\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)$. Moreover, every $z\in K$ has a stable singleton.
Proof: Let $X=\{(\eta,s(z,\eta)):z\in K,\eta\in P_\rho\}$. It is compact, lies in $E_G$, and has zero fiber $\{0\}\times K$. Fix $z_0\in K$. For $0<\epsilon\le\rho$, the map \[ j:(P_\epsilon,\partial P_\epsilon)\longrightarrow (X_\epsilon,\partial X_\epsilon), \qquad j(\eta)=(\eta,s(z_0,\eta)) \] satisfies $\pi\circ j=\operatorname{id}$. Hence $j^*\pi^*$ is the identity on $\check H^d(P_\epsilon,\partial P_\epsilon;\mathbb Q)\cong\mathbb Q$, so $\pi^*$ is nonzero.

To check connexity separately, let $V$ be any neighborhood of $X_0$ in $X$. Compactness implies that $X_\kappa\subseteq V$ for some $0<\kappa<\rho$: otherwise a sequence outside $V$ with masses tending to zero has a limit in $X_0$, contradicting the neighborhood property. The set \[ U_\kappa=\{\eta:0<\delta(\eta)<\kappa,\ \eta_i(a)>0\text{ for all }i,a\} \] is connected, being homeomorphic to $(0,\kappa)\times\operatorname{relint}\Sigma$. The image of $K\times U_\kappa$ is connected and lies in $V\setminus\partial X_1$. Its closure contains $X_{\kappa/2}$, by density of strictly positive perturbations and continuity of $s$. A component of $V\setminus\partial X_1$ containing this image therefore has closure containing a neighborhood of $X_0$. This is the required connexity. Restricting $s$ to any singleton parameter $z$ proves the last assertion by the same argument. ∎

Complete containment theorem for all $2\times2$ games

Represent each player's mixed strategy by the probability of action $1$: $x,y\in[0,1]$. The two payoff differences are affine functions \[ f(y)=u_1(1,y)-u_1(0,y),\qquad g(x)=u_2(x,1)-u_2(x,0). \]
Theorem (Every perfect equilibrium of a $2\times2$ game is singleton-stable): For every finite $2\times2$ game, every normal-form perfect equilibrium $z^*=(x^*,y^*)$ admits a local continuous equilibrium section of its lower-bound perturbations. Hence \[ \{z^*\}\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G). \] No genericity, isolation, strictness, or nondegeneracy assumption is made.
Proof: For the row player define $\ell_1=\eta_{1,1}$ and $u_1=1-\eta_{1,0}$, and define $\ell_2,u_2$ similarly. Thus the perturbed strategy intervals are $[\ell_1,u_1]$ and $[\ell_2,u_2]$. Set \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-63-6c-61-6d-70} x^\eta=\min\{u_1,\max\{\ell_1,x^*\}\},\qquad y^\eta=\min\{u_2,\max\{\ell_2,y^*\}\}. \end{equation} This map is continuous, feasible, and equals $z^*$ at zero. For sufficiently small common mass, any interior target coordinate is unchanged, while a target $0$ is sent to its lower bound and a target $1$ is sent to its upper bound.

We prove that $x^\eta$ is a constrained best reply to $y^\eta$. The column-player argument uses $g$ and interchanges the roles of $x,y$.

Case 1: $0<y^*<1$. Choose the mass small enough that $y^\eta=y^*$. The Nash condition gives $f(y^*)\le0$ if $x^*=0$, $f(y^*)\ge0$ if $x^*=1$, and $f(y^*)=0$ if $0<x^*<1$. In the first two cases the clamped $x^\eta$ is the appropriate feasible endpoint. In the last case every feasible $x$ is optimal.

Case 2: $y^*\in\{0,1\}$ and $0<x^*<1$. Perfection supplies $y^k\in(0,1)$ tending to $y^*$, with $x^*$ a best reply for every $k$. Since $x^*$ uses both actions, $f(y^k)=0$ for every $k$. A nonzero affine function cannot vanish at such a sequence approaching a boundary point. Therefore $f$ is identically zero, and every feasible $x^\eta$ is a best reply.

Case 3: $x^*,y^*\in\{0,1\}$. If $x^*=1$, perfection gives $f(y^k)\ge0$ for an interior sequence approaching $y^*$. An affine function with this property is nonnegative on a whole one-sided neighborhood of $y^*$. To check this explicitly, if $f(y^*)>0$ use continuity; if $f(y^*)=0$, the sign just inside the interval is fixed by its slope; and if $f(y^*)<0$, the sequence is impossible. Thus $f(y^\eta)\ge0$ for all sufficiently small bounds, and $x^\eta=u_1$ is optimal. When $x^*=0$ the same reasoning uses the weak inequality $f\le0$, and $x^\eta=\ell_1$ is optimal.

There are only two players and finitely many neighborhood requirements. Taking their minimum yields one positive $\rho$ valid for every perturbation in $P_\rho$, including directions with some zero coordinates. Both players are constrained best replies. Lemma Lemma (Continuous equilibrium section) proves singleton stability. ∎

Corollary (Econ-20 for every $2\times2$ target): Let $\Phi$ satisfy axiom $B$ globally, and let $G$ be any $2\times2$ game. Every selected set $K\in\Phi(G)$ contains a Mertens $0$-stable singleton. In particular, (eq:target) holds for all $2\times2$ targets under the original assumptions $B,D^*,I^*$.
Proof: By (eq:Bsim), $K$ contains a perfect equilibrium $z^*$. Apply Theorem thm:2by2 and take $S=\{z^*\}$. ∎
Remark: The containment argument does not need connectedness of $K$, $D^*$, or $I^*$. It uses only the simultaneous-representation consequence of $B$ and nonemptiness of the selected family. A theorem for $2\times2$ games is not a theorem for arbitrary two-player games. It is also not a theorem for all extensive-form games having two actions per information set: such games can have many pure contingent plans.

An exact singleton criterion at quasi-strict bimatrix equilibria

Let the two payoff matrices be $A,B\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$. The row player uses $p\in\Delta_m$ and the column player $q\in\Delta_n$. Let $(p^*,q^*)$ be a Nash equilibrium with supports $I=\operatorname{supp}(p^*)$ and $J=\operatorname{supp}(q^*)$.
Definition (Quasi-strictness): The equilibrium is quasi-strict if the row player's pure best replies to $q^*$ are exactly $I$, and the column player's pure best replies to $p^*$ are exactly $J$. Thus every unused action is strictly inferior against the target opponent. This is not the definition of quasi-perfect equilibrium.

Choose $i_0\in I$ and $j_0\in J$. Define matrices of supported payoff differences by \[ C_{i,:}=A_{i,:}-A_{i_0,:}\quad(i\in I\setminus\{i_0\}),\qquad D_{j,:}=(B_{:,j}-B_{:,j_0})^{\mathsf T}\quad(j\in J\setminus\{j_0\}). \] An empty collection of difference rows is allowed. Put \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-72-61-6e-6b-6d-61-74-72-69-63-65-73} \begin{aligned} M_A&=\begin{pmatrix}C_{:,J}\\\mathbf 1_J^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix},& N_A&=\begin{pmatrix}C_{:,J^c}\\\mathbf 1_{J^c}^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix},\\ M_B&=\begin{pmatrix}D_{:,I}\\\mathbf 1_I^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix},& N_B&=\begin{pmatrix}D_{:,I^c}\\\mathbf 1_{I^c}^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \end{equation} Here $M_A$ is $|I|\times|J|$ and $M_B$ is $|J|\times|I|$. An off-support matrix has zero columns when its complement is empty.

Theorem (Rank characterization at a quasi-strict equilibrium): At a quasi-strict bimatrix equilibrium $z^*=(p^*,q^*)$, the following are equivalent:
  1. $\{z^*\}\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)$.
  2. Every neighborhood $W$ of $z^*$ contains an equilibrium of every sufficiently small strictly positive common-mass lower-bound perturbation.
  3. \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-72-61-6e-6b-63-6f-6e-64-69-74-69-6f-6e} \operatorname{im} N_A\subseteq\operatorname{im} M_A,\qquad \operatorname{im} N_B\subseteq\operatorname{im} M_B. \end{equation} Equivalently, $\operatorname{rank} M_A=\operatorname{rank}[M_A\ N_A]$ and $\operatorname{rank} M_B=\operatorname{rank}[M_B\ N_B]$.
  4. The constrained-equilibrium correspondence has a local affine section through $z^*$, defined for all sufficiently small nonnegative lower bounds.
Proof: The implication (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii) is Lemma Lemma (Necessary local robustness). We first prove (iii)$\Rightarrow$(iv). Choose matrices $R_A,R_B$ with \[ M_A R_A=N_A,\qquad M_B R_B=N_B. \] For row lower bounds $\alpha$ and column lower bounds $\beta$, define \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-61-66-66-69-6e-65-73-65-63-74-69-6f-6e} \begin{aligned} q_{J^c}&=\beta_{J^c},&q_J&=q_J^*-R_A\beta_{J^c},\\ p_{I^c}&=\alpha_{I^c},&p_I&=p_I^*-R_B\alpha_{I^c}. \end{aligned} \end{equation} The last row of each matrix identity imposes total mass one. The other rows give $Cq=0$ and $Dp=0$, so all originally supported actions remain mutually indifferent for their respective players.

Let $\mu$ be the smallest positive coordinate of $p_I^*$ and $q_J^*$. It is positive because the supports are finite and nonempty. The changes in supported probabilities in (eq:affinesection) are bounded by a constant times $\delta(\alpha,\beta)$. Choosing that mass sufficiently small makes every supported coordinate exceed $\mu/2$ and all lower bounds less than $\mu/2$. Thus feasibility holds. Quasi-strictness and continuity preserve every strict payoff gap for unused actions. A player's constrained best reply must assign the lower bound to each strictly inferior action and may distribute the remaining probability among the tied best actions. That is exactly what (eq:affinesection) does. This proves (iv). Its continuity and Lemma Lemma (Continuous equilibrium section) give (iv)$\Rightarrow$(i).

It remains to prove (ii)$\Rightarrow$(iii). Choose a fixed neighborhood $W$ of $z^*$ in which all coordinates on $I,J$ exceed $\mu/2$, and each unused action remains strictly inferior to each originally supported action. Such a neighborhood exists by quasi-strictness and continuity. For bounds with common mass below $\mu/2$, any constrained equilibrium in $W$ must have \[ p_{I^c}=\alpha_{I^c},\quad q_{J^c}=\beta_{J^c},\quad Cq=0,\quad Dp=0. \] The supported coordinates are strictly above their lower bounds, so they must tie at the maximal payoff; inferior coordinates must be at their bounds. Subtracting the target equations gives the necessary systems \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6e-65-63-65-73-73-61-72-79-73-79-73-74-65-6d-73} M_A(q_J-q_J^*)=-N_A\beta_{J^c},\qquad M_B(p_I-p_I^*)=-N_B\alpha_{I^c}. \end{equation}

Suppose, for instance, $\operatorname{im} N_A\not\subseteq\operatorname{im} M_A$. Finite-dimensional linear algebra gives a row vector $w$ with $wM_A=0$ but $wN_A\ne0$. There is a vector $b$ with all coordinates strictly positive and $wN_A b\ne0$: a nonzero linear form cannot vanish on the open positive orthant. Scale $b$ so $\sum b<1$, extend it on $J$ by strictly positive coordinates of total mass $1-\sum b$, and call the resulting full probability vector $\bar\beta$. Choose any completely mixed $\bar\alpha\in\Delta_m$. For every $\delta>0$ set $\alpha=\delta\bar\alpha$ and $\beta=\delta\bar\beta$. These are strictly positive perturbations with common mass $\delta$, but multiplying the first equation of (eq:necessarysystems) by $w$ gives \[ 0=-\delta wN_A b\ne0. \] No constrained equilibrium lies in $W$, however small $\delta$ is. This contradicts (ii). The second inclusion is proved by the same argument. ∎

Corollary (Isolated quasi-strict equilibria): Every isolated quasi-strict equilibrium of a finite bimatrix game has a Mertens $0$-stable singleton.
Proof: Suppose $M_A$ has a nonzero kernel vector $v$. The profile with $p=p^*$, $q_{J^c}=0$, and $q_J=q_J^*+tv$ is a Nash equilibrium for every sufficiently small $|t|$: supported probabilities stay positive; the last matrix row preserves their sum; the difference rows preserve row-player indifference; and the unused-action strict inequalities persist. Column best replies are unchanged since $p=p^*$. This contradicts isolation. Thus $M_A$ has full column rank, so $|J|\le|I|$. The same argument for $M_B$ gives $|I|\le|J|$. Consequently the supports have equal size and both $M_A,M_B$ are square nonsingular matrices. Their column spaces are their full target spaces, so (eq:rankcondition) holds. Apply Theorem Theorem (Rank characterization at a quasi-strict equilibrium). ∎
Remark (What the local criterion does not say): Quasi-strictness alone does not imply the rank conditions, as the next example shows. Nor does the criterion imply that every axiomatically selected set contains a quasi-strict point satisfying those conditions. The isolated corollary has a classical robustness analogue in Okada [okada]; the self-contained argument above supplies the particular continuous section needed for the Mertens definition used here, rather than importing an unstated equivalence of refinement terminology.

A $2\times3$ obstruction: perfect points need not be stable singletons

Consider the game \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-32-62-79-33-67-61-6d-65} \begin{array}{c|ccc} &L&M&R\\\hline U&(0,0)&(1,-1)&(-1,-1)\\ D&(0,0)&(0,-1)&(0,-1). \end{array} \end{equation} The column player strictly prefers $L$ to either other action against every row mixture. At $L$ the row player is indifferent. Consequently, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-73-65-67-6d-65-6e-74} E:=\operatorname{NE}(G)=\{z_t=(tU+(1-t)D,L):0\le t\le1\}. \end{equation}
Proposition (All equilibria perfect, none singleton-stable): For game (eq:2by3game), every equilibrium $z_t$ is perfect (indeed proper), but no singleton $\{z_t\}$ is Mertens $0$-stable. Every stable set must equal the entire segment $E$.
Proof: For perfection, let $\epsilon\downarrow0$, put \[ p^\epsilon(U)=(1-2\epsilon)t+\epsilon,\qquad q^\epsilon=(1-2\epsilon,\epsilon,\epsilon). \] These profiles are completely mixed and tend to $z_t$. The row payoff difference is $q^\epsilon(M)-q^\epsilon(R)=0$, and $L$ is always the column player's best reply. Thus the target mixed strategies are best replies against each perturbed opponent, proving perfection.

For the optional stronger claim of properness, replace the column profile by $q^\epsilon=(1-2\epsilon^2,\epsilon^2,\epsilon^2)$, with $0<\epsilon<1/2$, and retain the row profile above. The row actions tie. Each inferior column action has probability $\epsilon^2$, whereas the better action has probability $1-2\epsilon^2$, and \[ \epsilon^2\le\epsilon(1-2\epsilon^2). \] Thus these profiles are $\epsilon$-proper: whenever an action has strictly lower payoff than another, its probability is at most $\epsilon$ times the probability of the better action. Their limits are the claimed proper equilibria.

Now impose row lower bounds $\alpha=(\delta/2,\delta/2)$. Consider the two strictly positive column perturbations, ordered as $(L,M,R)$, \[ \beta^+=(\delta/4,\delta/2,\delta/4),\qquad \beta^-=(\delta/4,\delta/4,\delta/2). \] All players' lower bounds have common mass $\delta$. Since $L$ is strictly best, the column player uniquely puts $M,R$ at their lower bounds and all remaining mass on $L$. Under $\beta^+$ the row payoff difference is $\delta/4>0$, giving the unique constrained equilibrium \[ p^+(U)=1-\delta/2,\qquad q^+=(1-3\delta/4,\delta/2,\delta/4). \] Under $\beta^-$ it is $-\delta/4<0$, giving \[ p^-(U)=\delta/2,\qquad q^-=(1-3\delta/4,\delta/4,\delta/2). \] The two equilibria converge respectively to $z_1$ and $z_0$. Lemma Lemma (Necessary local robustness) and closedness imply that every stable set must contain both $z_0$ and $z_1$: a compact set missing one of them has a neighborhood avoided by its corresponding equilibrium ray for all small $\delta$. Stable sets are connected and are subsets of $E$, so they must contain the whole interval. No singleton can do so. Finally, existence of Mertens stable sets in finite games, as used in [gw], implies that $E$ itself is the unique stable set. Only this last existence conclusion uses that general existence theorem. ∎

At $0<t<1$, the equilibrium is quasi-strict with $I=\{U,D\}$ and $J=\{L\}$. Choosing $D$ as row reference gives \[ M_A=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad N_A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}. \] Thus $\operatorname{rank} M_A=1$ whereas $\operatorname{rank}[M_A\ N_A]=2$. Supported row indifference would require $\beta_M=\beta_R$, and a change in the only supported column probability cannot compensate unequal trembles. This is precisely the obstruction in Theorem Theorem (Rank characterization at a quasi-strict equilibrium).

Remark (Not a counterexample to Econ-20): The proposition disproves "every perfect bimatrix equilibrium is a stable singleton," even with "proper" in place of "perfect." It does not construct a rule $\Phi$ satisfying $B,D^*,I^*$ globally and selecting any proper subinterval or singleton of $E$. Normal-form perfection or properness at one game does not establish global invariance. Whether the exact axioms force a particular selected set to expand to the stable set is a different question. No assertion that this specific matrix defeats those axioms is made.

Two elementary comparisons that really satisfy the axioms

Let a bimatrix game have row actions $\{1,\ldots,m\}$, column actions $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and payoff matrices $A,B$. For a column distribution $\tau\in\Delta_n$ and $c>0$, add a column action $t$ whose payoffs are \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-70-65-6e-61-6c-69-7a-65-64} \bar A_{:,t}=A\tau,\qquad \bar B_{:,t}=B\tau-c\mathbf 1. \end{equation} Multiple such actions may be added simultaneously.
Lemma (Penalized mixtures are an admissible $D^*$ extension): An extension formed by finitely many actions (eq:penalized) is obtained by adding strongly irrelevant dominated strategies in the source's exact sense.
Proof: Each $t$ is strictly dominated, for the column player, by the original mixed strategy $\tau$, with the uniform gap $c$. Consequently no new action can be used in a Nash equilibrium. Every original equilibrium survives, since at it each new action is strictly worse than some original best reply. Thus the two games have the same equilibria on the original face.

Define $f_1(p)=p$ and \[ f_2(\bar q)=\bar q_{\rm old}+\sum_t\bar q_t\tau^t. \] This affine map is the identity on the old simplex. For any pair of original row strategies $p,p'$, payoff comparisons against $\bar q$ are exactly the original comparisons against $f_2(\bar q)$, by the first equality of (eq:penalized). For any pair of original column strategies, payoff comparisons against $p$ are unchanged. These statements hold on the entire enlarged strategy space, so in particular on a neighborhood of the original face. They verify every condition of Definition 4.4, not merely dominance. ∎

Lemma (Duplicate plans and a hidden continuation): Fix a nonempty proper subset $J$ of original column actions and add actions $t_j$, $j\in J$, as in (eq:penalized). There is a finite perfect-recall tree with the following properties. At its root the column player chooses an action of $J$ directly or chooses $\mathrm{Out}$. After $\mathrm{Out}$ the same player chooses from $J^c\cup\{t_j:j\in J\}$. The row player then acts at one information set, observing neither column decision. The full normal form $H$ strongly embeds $\bar G$ by a duplicate-collapse map $f$, and $f_1$ is the identity on row strategies.
Proof: Give the terminal node with actual row action $i$ and actual column action $k$ its payoff $(\bar A_{ik},\bar B_{ik})$. Perfect recall holds because the column player's second information set remembers the root choice, and the row player has no earlier decision.

A pure column plan specifies a root action and an off-path continuation. Every plan choosing a particular $j\in J$ directly is a duplicate of $j$, regardless of its continuation. A plan choosing $\mathrm{Out}$ and then $k$ is identified with $k$. Collapsing these duplicates is an affine surjection. Payoffs in $H$ are exactly those of $\bar G$ after collapse. This is a strong embedding with no outsiders: the outsider face is a point and the preference-equivalence condition holds globally.

All actual action pairs are distinct terminal outcomes. Since the row player's information set is always reached, a mixed profile realization- equivalent to a behavioral profile has the same collapsed row and column marginals. Thus a $B$-witness in a lifted set projects into its specified image in $\bar G$; the unreached continuation is not incorrectly treated as an observed action. ∎

A finite strategic exclusion certificate

For a row distribution $p$, let $\operatorname{BR}_2(p)$ denote the column player's pure best replies. The next theorem turns a linear payoff obstruction into a valid global-axiom contradiction.
Theorem (Uniform indifference exclusion): Let $K\subseteq\operatorname{NE}(A,B)$ be nonempty and compact. Suppose that there are nonempty sets $I$ of row actions and $J$ of column actions, with $J^c$ nonempty, and a vector $w\in\mathbb R^m$ such that \begin{align} &p_i>0\quad\text{for all }(p,q)\in K\text{ and }i\in I,\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-75-73-65-64}\\ &\operatorname{BR}_2(p)\subseteq J\quad\text{for all }(p,q)\in K,\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-62-65-73-74}\\ &\operatorname{supp}(w)\subseteq I,\qquad \mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}w=0,\qquad w^{\mathsf T}A_{:,j}=0\quad(j\in J),\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-61-6e-6e-69-68-69-6c-61-74-65}\\ &w^{\mathsf T}A_{:,\ell}\ne0\quad\text{for some }\ell\in J^c. \label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-64-65-74-65-63-74} \end{align} Then $K$ cannot be a selected set of any globally defined rule satisfying $B,D^*,I^*$. The certificate uses at most $|J|$ new dominated actions, a duplicate-plan embedding, and one application of $B$.
Proof: Put $v(p)=\max_j p^{\mathsf T}B_{:,j}$. By (eq:best), compactness, continuity, and finiteness of the action sets, \[ \gamma=\min_{(p,q)\in K}\min_{k\in J^c} \{v(p)-p^{\mathsf T}B_{:,k}\}>0. \] Let $L=\max_{i,j,k}|B_{ij}-B_{ik}|$. Then $L\ge\gamma>0$. Choose \[ \epsilon=\frac{\gamma}{4L},\qquad c=\frac{\gamma}{4},\qquad \tau^j=(1-\epsilon)e_j+\epsilon e_\ell\quad(j\in J). \] Add a penalized mixture $t_j$ for each $\tau^j$. Lemma lem:dominated applies.

For each $(p,q)\in K$, choose $j\in\operatorname{BR}_2(p)\subseteq J$. The payoff from its associated continuation action satisfies \begin{align*} p^{\mathsf T}\bar B_{:,t_j} &= (1-\epsilon)v(p)+\epsilon p^{\mathsf T}B_{:,\ell}-c\\ &\ge v(p)-\epsilon L-c =v(p)-\gamma/2 \ge p^{\mathsf T}B_{:,k}+\gamma/2\quad(k\in J^c). \end{align*} Thus every original action in $J^c$ is strictly worse than some new action at the continuation information set. The inequality persists against row profiles sufficiently close to the projection of $K$.

Assume for contradiction that $K\in\Phi(G)$. Axiom $D^*$ makes $K$, padded by zero probabilities, a selected set of $\bar G$. Apply Lemma Lemma (Duplicate plans and a hidden continuation) and the existence-of-lifts direction of $I^*$ to obtain $K_H\in\Phi(H)$ with $f(K_H)=K$. Axiom $B$ supplies a profile in $K_H$ realization-equivalent to a quasi-perfect behavioral equilibrium $b$ of the hidden-continuation tree. Its collapsed image lies in $K$. Let $b^r\to b$ be the completely mixed behavioral sequence in the definition of quasi-perfection.

The continuation's limiting strategy puts total probability one on the new actions: any positive limiting probability on an old $k\in J^c$ would contradict its strict suboptimality against the approximating row strategies. Denote its probabilities along $b^r$ by $\zeta_k^r$, and let $\theta_r>0$ be the root probability of $\mathrm{Out}$. We have \[ \sum_{j\in J}\zeta_{t_j}^r\longrightarrow1, \qquad \zeta_k^r\longrightarrow0\quad(k\in J^c). \] Writing $a=w^{\mathsf T}A_{:,\ell}\ne0$, equation (eq:annihilate) gives the same value for every new action: \[ w^{\mathsf T}\bar A_{:,t_j}=\epsilon a. \] All root-direct actions have weighted row payoff zero. The weighted row payoff against the full induced column distribution $\bar q^r$ is therefore \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-63-6f-6e-74-72-61-64-69-63-74-69-6f-6e} w^{\mathsf T}\bar A\bar q^r =\theta_r\left( \epsilon a\sum_{j\in J}\zeta_{t_j}^r +\sum_{k\in J^c}\zeta_k^r w^{\mathsf T}A_{:,k}\right). \end{equation} The bracket converges to $\epsilon a\ne0$; since $\theta_r>0$, the expression is nonzero for all sufficiently large $r$.

On the other hand, the target row strategy uses every action in $I$. Quasi-perfection at its sole information set makes each such action a best reply to the opponent's behavioral strategy $b_2^r$ for every $r$. Their payoffs are equal. Since $w$ is supported on $I$ and its coordinates sum to zero, their weighted payoff must be zero. This contradicts (eq:contradiction). No globally compatible rule can select $K$. ∎

Remark (What is and is not being assumed): The new actions are strictly dominated in the original normal-form sense, yet optimal after an off-path restriction to continuation choices. There is no contradiction: the dominating original mixture uses root-direct actions that are unavailable once $\mathrm{Out}$ has been chosen. The proof retains the distinction between a simultaneous game's perfect equilibrium and quasi-perfection in all its allowed representations. It does not assume that equilibrium selection is continuous in payoffs.

Selected sets consisting entirely of quasi-strict equilibria

An equilibrium $(p,q)$ is quasi-strict when $\operatorname{BR}_1(q)=\operatorname{supp}(p)$ and $\operatorname{BR}_2(p)=\operatorname{supp}(q)$: unused actions are strictly inferior. This is not the definition of quasi-perfect equilibrium.
Lemma (Supports are constant on a connected quasi-strict set): If a connected set $K\subseteq\operatorname{NE}(G)$ consists entirely of quasi-strict equilibria, the two supports $I=\operatorname{supp}(p)$ and $J=\operatorname{supp}(q)$ do not depend on $(p,q)\in K$.
Proof: At any quasi-strict equilibrium, positive coordinates remain positive in a sufficiently small neighborhood. Every unused action remains strictly worse than an originally supported action in that neighborhood, so it cannot be used at a neighboring Nash equilibrium. Hence the support map is locally constant on $K$. It takes values in a finite discrete set; connectedness forces it to be constant. ∎

Fix reference actions $i_0\in I$ and $j_0\in J$, and set \[ C_{i,:}=A_{i,:}-A_{i_0,:}\quad(i\in I\setminus\{i_0\}),\qquad D_{j,:}=(B_{:,j}-B_{:,j_0})^{\mathsf T}\quad(j\in J\setminus\{j_0\}). \] Allow empty difference matrices. Define \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6d-61-74-72-69-63-65-73} \begin{aligned} M_A&=\begin{pmatrix}C_{:,J}\\\mathbf 1_J^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix},& N_A&=\begin{pmatrix}C_{:,J^c}\\\mathbf 1_{J^c}^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix},\\ M_B&=\begin{pmatrix}D_{:,I}\\\mathbf 1_I^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix},& N_B&=\begin{pmatrix}D_{:,I^c}\\\mathbf 1_{I^c}^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} \end{equation}

Theorem (Quasi-strict selected sets are stable): Let $\Phi$ satisfy the global axioms and let $K\in\Phi(G)$ for a finite bimatrix game. If every point of $K$ is quasi-strict, then \[ \operatorname{im} N_A\subseteq\operatorname{im} M_A,\qquad \operatorname{im} N_B\subseteq\operatorname{im} M_B. \] Moreover, $K$ itself is Mertens $0$-stable, and every $z\in K$ has a stable singleton. In particular the target containment conclusion holds for this selected set, without any isolation or genericity assumption.
Proof: Supports are constant by Lemma Lemma (Supports are constant on a connected quasi-strict set). Suppose first that $\operatorname{im} N_A\not\subseteq\operatorname{im} M_A$. A separating row vector can be written $(h^{\mathsf T},\lambda)$, with \[ (h^{\mathsf T},\lambda)M_A=0,\qquad (h^{\mathsf T},\lambda)N_A\ne0. \] Choose any $(p^*,q^*)\in K$. Since $C_{:,J}q_J^*=0$ and $\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}q_J^*=1$, multiplication by $q_J^*$ shows $\lambda=0$. Consequently \[ h^{\mathsf T}C_{:,J}=0,\qquad h^{\mathsf T}C_{:,\ell}\ne0 \quad\text{for some }\ell\in J^c. \] Define $w_i=h_i$ for $i\in I\setminus\{i_0\}$, $w_{i_0}=-\sum_{i\ne i_0}h_i$, and $w_i=0$ outside $I$. Then $\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}w=0$ and $w^{\mathsf T}A=h^{\mathsf T}C$. All hypotheses of Theorem Theorem (Uniform indifference exclusion) hold, since each point of $K$ uses $I$ and has column best replies exactly $J$. This contradicts selection of $K$. The first inclusion follows. Exchanging the two players proves the second. Empty complements cause no difficulty; their inclusions are automatic.

Choose matrices $R_A,R_B$ solving $M_AR_A=N_A$, $M_BR_B=N_B$. For $z=(p^*,q^*)\in K$ and row/column lower bounds $\alpha,\beta$, put \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-75-6e-69-66-6f-72-6d-73-65-63-74-69-6f-6e} \begin{aligned} q_{J^c}&=\beta_{J^c},&q_J&=q_J^*-R_A\beta_{J^c},\\ p_{I^c}&=\alpha_{I^c},&p_I&=p_I^*-R_B\alpha_{I^c}. \end{aligned} \end{equation} The probability-sum rows of the identities make $p,q$ distributions; the other rows give $Cq=0$, $Dp=0$. Thus supported actions stay tied.

All coordinates in $p_I^*,q_J^*$ have a common positive lower bound as $z$ ranges over compact $K$. Likewise the unused-action payoff gaps have a common positive lower bound whenever such actions exist. The changes in (eq:uniformsection) are bounded uniformly by a constant times the perturbation mass. Hence, for a common sufficiently small $\rho>0$, all supported coordinates exceed their required lower bounds and all unused actions remain strictly inferior, uniformly over $K$. Inferior actions receive exactly their lower bounds; all remaining probability is assigned to tied best replies. Therefore (eq:uniformsection) is a jointly continuous family of constrained equilibria. Lemma Lemma (A continuous family of sections) proves both conclusions about stability. ∎

Corollary (A necessary feature of any counterexample): If a selected set $K$ in a bimatrix game contains no stable subset, then $K$ contains at least one non-quasi-strict equilibrium. Equivalently, at some point of $K$, an unused action is also a best reply.
Proof: Otherwise Theorem Theorem (Quasi-strict selected sets are stable) makes $K$ itself stable. ∎

Completing the earlier $2\times3$ example at the set level

Consider \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-65-78-61-6d-70-6c-65} \begin{array}{c|ccc} &L&M&R\\\hline U&(0,0)&(1,-1)&(-1,-1)\\ D&(0,0)&(0,-1)&(0,-1). \end{array} \end{equation} The Nash set is $E=\{z_t=(tU+(1-t)D,L):0\le t\le1\}$. Column $L$ is strictly dominant, and row actions tie at $L$.
Theorem (The axioms force the entire segment): For every globally compatible rule $\Phi$, game (eq:example) satisfies $\Phi(G)=\{E\}$. Moreover, $\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)=\{E\}$. Thus this game is not a counterexample to the two-player conjecture, even though no individual equilibrium has a stable singleton.
Proof: First add a new column action $T^+$ with payoffs \[ \bar G(U,T^+)=(1/2,-3/4),\qquad \bar G(D,T^+)=(0,-3/4). \] It is the penalized mixture $\tau^+=(L+M)/2$, with penalty $1/4$. Lemma lem:dominated makes this an exact $D^*$ extension. Use the tree with root choices $L,\mathrm{Out}$ and continuation choices $M,R,T^+$, followed by the uninformed row decision. Its full normal form has six column plans, three of them duplicates of $L$. Lemma Lemma (Duplicate plans and a hidden continuation) and $I^*$ justify the collapse.

At the continuation information set, $T^+$ gives the column player $-3/4$, strictly exceeding the payoff $-1$ of either alternative, against every row strategy. A quasi-perfect equilibrium must therefore choose $T^+$ there. At the root it chooses $L$, which gives $0$. In a completely mixed behavioral sequence witnessing quasi-perfection, write $\theta_r>0$ for the probability of $\mathrm{Out}$ and $\zeta^r$ for the continuation distribution. Then \[ u_1(U,b_2^r)-u_1(D,b_2^r) =\theta_r(\zeta_M^r-\zeta_R^r+\tfrac12\zeta_{T^+}^r)>0 \] for all sufficiently large $r$, since $\zeta_{T^+}^r\to1$. Every quasi-perfect outcome of this tree is therefore $(U,L)$. Lifting an arbitrary $K\in\Phi(G)$ through $D^*$ and the duplicate embedding, then applying $B$, forces $z_1\in K$.

Now instead add the penalized mixture $\tau^-=(L+R)/2$ with the same penalty. Its row payoffs are $-1/2,0$ and its column payoff is again $-3/4$. The identical continuation argument makes the row payoff difference strictly negative along every quasi-perfect witness, so it forces $z_0=(D,L)$ into the same original selected set $K$. A nonempty connected subset of the segment $E$ containing its two endpoints is $E$. Thus every selected set is $E$; nonemptiness of the solution family gives $\Phi(G)=\{E\}$.

Apply this proved selection statement to the known all-game solution concept $\Phi=\operatorname{Stab}_0$. Its existence and satisfaction of the axioms are independent literature results cited in Section 1. It follows that $\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)=\{E\}$. This application does not invoke the unproved nongeneric containment converse. ∎

An unbounded-size subclass: a strictly dominant column

Let the row player have two actions $U,D$ and the column player have arbitrary finite actions $0,1,\ldots,n-1$. Suppose \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-64-6f-6d-69-6e-61-6e-74} B_{i0}>B_{ij}\quad(i\in\{U,D\},\ j\ne0). \end{equation} Define $d_j=A_{Uj}-A_{Dj}$.
Theorem (Containment for every dominant-column $2\times n$ game): Every game satisfying (eq:dominant) satisfies the target (eq:target). In the special case \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6f-70-70-6f-73-69-74-65-73-69-67-6e-73} d_0=0,\qquad \min_{j\ne0}d_j<0<\max_{j\ne0}d_j, \end{equation} every compatible rule selects exactly the full Nash segment $E=\{(tU+(1-t)D,0):0\le t\le1\}$, and this segment is the unique Mertens-stable set.
Proof: Column $0$ is the unique column best reply to every row distribution. If $d_0>0$ or $d_0<0$, there is a unique strict Nash equilibrium. For all sufficiently small lower bounds, keep the dominant column at all its feasible residual mass and clamp the strict row action to its feasible endpoint. The strict payoff gap persists, yielding a continuous equilibrium section. Its singleton is stable by Lemma Lemma (A continuous family of sections). This settles $d_0\ne0$.

Now let $d_0=0$, so the Nash set is $E$. If all $d_j=0$, every target $t\in[0,1]$ has the section \[ p_U=\alpha_U+(1-\delta)t,\quad p_D=\alpha_D+(1-\delta)(1-t),\quad q_j=\beta_j\ (j\ne0),\quad q_0=1-\sum_{j\ne0}\beta_j. \] Both row actions always tie, and column $0$ remains strictly best. Every singleton is stable, so every nonempty selected set contains one.

If all $d_j\ge0$ and at least one is positive, $U$ is the unique best reply against every completely mixed column strategy. Axiom $B$, applied to the simultaneous-move representation, therefore places $(U,0)$ in each selected set. The formula $p_U=1-\alpha_D$, together with the column formula above, is a continuous equilibrium section because $\sum_jd_jq_j\ge0$ for every nonnegative lower-bound vector. Its zero fiber is $(U,0)$, proving the requisite stability. The case with all $d_j\le0$ and at least one negative is symmetric.

It remains to treat (eq:oppositesigns). Put \[ \gamma=\min_{i,j\ne0}(B_{i0}-B_{ij})>0,\qquad M=\max_{i,j\ne0}(B_{i0}-B_{ij})\ge\gamma, \qquad \epsilon=\frac{\gamma}{4M},\quad c=\frac\gamma4. \] Choose $h$ with $d_h>0$, and add the penalized column mixture $\tau=(1-\epsilon)e_0+\epsilon e_h$. For every row action $i$ and original nonzero column $j$, \begin{align*} \bar B_{it}-B_{ij} &= B_{i0}-B_{ij}-\epsilon(B_{i0}-B_{ih})-c\\ &\ge\gamma-\epsilon M-c=\gamma/2>0. \end{align*} Column $0$ strictly beats $t$, and $t$ strictly beats every original nonzero column. Its row payoff difference is $\epsilon d_h>0$. The root choice $0/\mathrm{Out}$ followed by all nonzero original columns and $t$ is therefore exactly the forcing construction of Theorem Theorem (The axioms force the entire segment). It forces $(U,0)$ into every selected $K$. Repeating with a column $h$ satisfying $d_h<0$ forces $(D,0)$ into the same $K$. Connectedness gives $K=E$. Finally apply the proved conclusion to the known nonempty stable-set solution concept to get $\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)=\{E\}$.

The cases $d_0\ne0$, all differences zero, one weak sign, and both strict signs exhaust all possibilities. The proof permits any finite $n$; $n=1$ falls within the first or all-zero case. ∎

A dominant column and a balanced family of payoff vectors

Let the row actions be $\{1,\ldots,m\}$ and the column actions be $\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}$, with $n\ge2$. Assume column $0$ is strictly dominant: \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-64-6f-6d-69-6e-61-6e-63-65} B_{i0}>B_{ij}\qquad\text{for all }i\text{ and }j\ne0. \end{equation} Set \[ I=\arg\max_i A_{i0},\qquad s=|I|,\qquad E=\Delta(I)\times\{e_0\}. \] Here $\Delta(I)$ is the face of the row simplex supported on $I$. By strict dominance and the row best-response condition, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6e-65} \operatorname{NE}(G)=E. \end{equation} Choose any finite list of original column mixtures $\tau^1,\ldots,\tau^k\in\Delta_n$ and define \[ C_{ij}=(A\tau^j)_i\quad(1\le i\le m,\ 1\le j\le k), \qquad C_I=(C_{ij})_{i\in I,\,j\le k}. \] The following are explicit sufficient hypotheses, not consequences of the three axioms: \begin{align} &\text{There exist }z\in\Delta_k,\ z_j>0\text{ for every }j, \text{ and }a\in\mathbb R\text{ such that }C_Iz=a\mathbf 1_I; \label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-62-61-6c-61-6e-63-65}\\ &w\in\mathbb R^I,\quad\mathbf 1_I^Tw=0,\quad C_I^Tw=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w=0.\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-73-70-61-6e} \end{align} We call (eq:balance) balance and (eq:span) spanning. Equivalently, spanning says \[ \operatorname{rank}\begin{pmatrix}C_I^T\\\mathbf 1_I^T\end{pmatrix}=s. \] Balance supplies a strictly positive mixture of probe columns that makes all rows in $I$ indifferent. Spanning says that these probe payoffs distinguish row distributions on that face.

A calibrated, admissible extension

Fix an arbitrary target $r\in\Delta(I)$, allowing zero coordinates. Write \[ a_j(r)=\sum_{h\in I}r_hC_{hj},\qquad \gamma=\min_{i,\,\ell\ne0}(B_{i0}-B_{i\ell})>0, \] and choose \[ D_r=\max\{1,\max_{i,j}|a_j(r)-C_{ij}|\},\qquad c=\gamma/2,\qquad\kappa=\frac{\gamma}{8D_r}>0. \] Add column actions $t_1,\ldots,t_k$, retaining every original payoff, and set \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6e-65-77} \bar A_{i,t_j}=C_{ij},\qquad \bar B_{i,t_j}=B_{i0}-c+\kappa\left(a_j(r)-C_{ij}\right). \end{equation}
Lemma (Exact admissibility): The extension (eq:new) satisfies the source's $D^*$ comparison. Moreover, every new action lies strictly below column $0$ and strictly above every original nonzero column in column-player payoff, uniformly in the row player's strategy.
Proof: For every pure row $i$ and probe $t_j$, \[ \frac{3\gamma}{8}\le B_{i0}-\bar B_{i,t_j} \le\frac{5\gamma}{8}. \] For every original $\ell\ne0$, \[ \bar B_{i,t_j}-B_{i\ell} =(B_{i0}-B_{i\ell})-(B_{i0}-\bar B_{i,t_j}) \ge\frac{3\gamma}{8}. \] The same bounds hold against mixed rows by linearity. Thus every new action is strictly dominated by the original pure action $0$; column $0$ remains strictly dominant in the enlarged game. Its Nash set is exactly the original face $E$, with zero probabilities on new actions. This verifies Definitions 4.1--4.2 of the source.

For Definition 4.4 use the affine dictionary \[ f_1(p)=p,\qquad f_2(\bar q)=\bar q_{\rm old}+\sum_{j=1}^k\bar q_{t_j}\tau^j. \] It is the identity on the original face. For original row strategies $p,p'$, the difference in their enlarged-game payoffs against $\bar q$ is exactly $(p-p')^TAf_2(\bar q)$, because the new row-payoff columns are $A\tau^j$. For any two original column strategies, their payoff comparison is unchanged and evaluated against $f_1(p)=p$. The required preference equivalences therefore hold everywhere, not merely in a neighborhood. Together with domination and equality of Nash sets, these facts establish the exact $D^*$ comparison. ∎

Full normal form, lifting, and quasi-perfection

Represent $\bar G$ by a finite perfect-recall tree. The column player first chooses $0$ or Out. After Out, that same player chooses one of \[ \{1,\ldots,n-1,t_1,\ldots,t_k\}. \] The row player then moves at a single information set, observing neither column move, with all $m$ row actions available. Payoffs are those of the resulting row and column in $\bar G$.

Let $H$ be the full normal form. A column pure plan specifies both a root action and a continuation; hence there are $n-1+k$ duplicate plans with root action $0$. Collapsing those plans to $0$, and mapping each Out plan to its continuation action, gives an affine surjection $g$ from the strategy space of $H$ onto that of $\bar G$. Thus the target continuation is a distribution $y$ over $t_1,\ldots,t_k$.

Remaining gap, checks, and limitations

The conclusions above do not prove the full implication (eq:target) for arbitrary bimatrix targets. The rank theorem concerns a singleton at a quasi-strict equilibrium. The finite exclusion certificate handles selected sets that retain a common positive support and a uniform strictly inferior outside column. The calibrated action construction requires a common strictly dominant column and the stated balance--spanning assumptions. In an arbitrary selected equilibrium set, the supports and best-reply faces may change; unused actions can tie at the boundary. The strict gap used to place new probes below a root action yet above old continuations can vanish. Neither connectedness alone nor simple robustness to perturbations replaces the rational Čech essentiality and connexity in Definition 2.4 of [gw]. We have neither glued the available local certificates into an unrestricted witness nor constructed a solution concept satisfying $B,D^*,I^*$ globally that violates containment.

Compact spaces of candidate solutions

Write \[ \mathscr C(G)=\{K\subseteq\operatorname{NE}(G):K\ne\varnothing,\ K\text{ compact and connected}\}, \] with the Hausdorff metric $d_H$. Compactness and closedness of individual sets are equivalent here because $\operatorname{NE}(G)$ is compact.
Lemma: For every finite game, $\mathscr C(G)$ is compact. If $f:\operatorname{NE}(H)\to\operatorname{NE}(G)$ is continuous, then $f_*:\mathscr C(H)\to\mathscr C(G)$, defined by $f_*(L)=f(L)$, is continuous.
Proof: The Nash set is closed in the compact product of simplexes: each best-response inequality is closed. For completeness, the hyperspace of nonempty compact subsets of any compact metric space $M$ is compact. Finite nets in $M$ yield finite nets in its hyperspace by taking all nonempty subsets of a finite net. It is complete: for a Hausdorff-Cauchy sequence $K_j$, the functions $z\mapsto\operatorname{dist}(z,K_j)$ converge uniformly to a continuous function $v$. The zero set $K=\{v=0\}$ is nonempty by compactness of $M$. Uniform convergence implies $\sup_{z\in K}\operatorname{dist}(z,K_j)\to0$. If the other Hausdorff distance did not tend to zero, points in $K_j$ bounded away from $K$ would have a limit in $M$; uniform convergence would put that limit in $K$, a contradiction. Thus $K_j\to K$.

Connected subsets form a closed subspace of this hyperspace. Indeed, if a Hausdorff limit $K$ of connected compact sets were disconnected, a separation of $K$ into two nonempty compact sets would give disjoint open neighborhoods with positive separation. All sufficiently close $K_j$ would lie in their union and meet both, contradicting connectedness. This proves compactness of $\mathscr C(G)$. Finally, $f$ is uniformly continuous on its compact domain. A modulus of uniform continuity bounds $d_H(f(L),f(L'))$ in terms of $d_H(L,L')$. Continuous images preserve nonemptiness, compactness and connectedness. ∎

For a fixed perfect-recall representation $\Gamma$ of $G$, let $Q_\Gamma\subseteq\operatorname{NE}(G)$ denote exactly the mixed profiles equivalent to quasi-perfect equilibria in the source's sense.

Lemma: $Q_\Gamma$ is compact. Consequently $\{K\in\mathscr C(G):K\cap Q_\Gamma\ne\varnothing\}$ is closed in $\mathscr C(G)$.
Proof: First show that quasi-perfect behavioral profiles form a closed set for a fixed finite $\Gamma$. Suppose $b^j\to b$ and every $b^j$ is quasi-perfect. There are finitely many information-set actions. After discarding finitely many terms, every action with positive probability in $b$ also has positive probability in $b^j$. From a defining completely mixed witness sequence for $b^j$, choose $c^j$ with $\|c^j-b^j\|<1/j$. Every action supported by $b^j$ is optimal against $c^j_{-i}$ at its information set, by [gw, Definition 2.1]. Therefore every action supported by $b$ has the same property. As $c^j\to b$, this is a quasi-perfect witness for $b$. The behavioral strategy space is compact, so its quasi-perfect subset is compact.

The mixed/behavioral equivalence relation used here is closed. In the source's realization or enabling representation it is expressed by equality of finitely many continuous realization coordinates: they are linear in mixed strategies and polynomial in behavioral strategies. Thus the set of pairs $(x,b)$ with $b$ quasi-perfect and $x$ equivalent to $b$ is compact. Its projection to mixed-strategy space is $Q_\Gamma$ and is compact. The usual Nash inequalities are preserved by this equivalence.

Finally, if $K_j\to K$ and $x_j\in K_j\cap Q_\Gamma$, take a convergent subsequence in compact $Q_\Gamma$. Its limit lies in $K$ by Hausdorff convergence. Hence $K\cap Q_\Gamma\ne\varnothing$. ∎

A largest axiom-compatible rule by compact pruning

Comparison maps

Introduce one arrow $e:H\to G$ for each of the following exactly permitted comparisons:
  1. A strong embedding of $G$ in $H$, with the map $f_e:\operatorname{NE}(H)\to\operatorname{NE}(G)$ supplied by Definition 5.2 of the source.
  2. An extension $H$ of $G$ satisfying Definition 4.4 of the source, with $f_e$ the natural deletion of the unused added coordinates on the Nash sets.
In (ii), the two Nash sets are naturally homeomorphic. In (i), Nash profiles indeed map to Nash profiles: the outsiders of a Nash profile lie in the specified face; surjectivity of each insider's strategy map implements any original deviation; and the source's preference preservation would turn a profitable original deviation into a profitable insider deviation in $H$. The other insiders' mapped strategies remain unchanged because their maps depend only on their own strategies and the outsiders' strategies.

Both kinds of $f_e$ are continuous. Put $e_*(L)=f_e(L)$. For these arrows, the two invariance axioms become the single family of equalities \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-61-72-72-6f-77-73} \Phi(G)=e_*(\Phi(H)) :=\{e_*(L):L\in\Phi(H)\}\qquad(e:H\to G). \end{equation} For a dominated-strategy arrow this is just the source's equality under the natural face identification, not an added projection axiom.

The recursion

Let $\mathfrak E(G)$ be the set of all permitted perfect-recall representations of $G$. Define \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-41-30} \mathcal A_0(G)=\bigcap_{\Gamma\in\mathfrak E(G)} \{K\in\mathscr C(G):K\cap Q_\Gamma\ne\varnothing\}. \end{equation} Thus $\mathcal A_0$ imposes exactly $B$ on individual candidate sets. For $r\ge0$, define \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-72-65-63-75-72-73-69-6f-6e} \begin{split} \mathcal A_{r+1}(G)={}&\mathcal A_r(G)\ \cap\! \bigcap_{e:G\to F}e_*^{-1}(\mathcal A_r(F))\ \cap\! \bigcap_{e:H\to G}e_*(\mathcal A_r(H)),\\ \mathcal A_\infty(G)={}&\bigcap_{r=0}^{\infty}\mathcal A_r(G). \end{split} \end{equation} The first intersection of comparisons enforces image compatibility; the second enforces the existence of a compatible lift for each incoming comparison. Different arrows may require different lifts, exactly as in (eq:arrows).
Theorem (Largest compatible solution concept): For every $G$, all families $\mathcal A_r(G)$ and $\mathcal A_\infty(G)$ are nonempty compact subsets of $\mathscr C(G)$. The rule $\mathcal A_\infty$ satisfies $B,D^*,I^*$ globally. Moreover, every globally defined solution concept $\Phi$ satisfying these axioms obeys \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6c-61-72-67-65-73-74} \Phi(G)\subseteq\mathcal A_\infty(G)\qquad\text{for every finite game }G. \end{equation} Thus $\mathcal A_\infty$ is the largest such rule, ordered by inclusion of solution families game by game.
Proof: Compactness. Lemma Lemma makes $\mathcal A_0(G)$ closed in compact $\mathscr C(G)$. Suppose all $\mathcal A_r(F)$ are compact. Each inverse image in (eq:recursion) is closed by Lemma Lemma. Each image $e_*(\mathcal A_r(H))$ is compact, hence closed in the metric space $\mathscr C(G)$. Arbitrary intersections of these closed sets are closed. This proves compactness by induction. The families decrease with $r$.

Nonemptiness. We prove the stronger assertion \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-73-74-61-62-6c-65-70-72-6f-74-65-63-74-65-64} \operatorname{Stab}_0(G)\subseteq\mathcal A_r(G)\qquad\text{for every }G,r. \end{equation} At $r=0$, it follows from stability satisfying $B$. Suppose it holds at stage $r$. For a stable set $S$ in $G$, every outgoing arrow has stable image in its target, hence an image in that target's $\mathcal A_r$. Every incoming arrow admits a stable lift of $S$, hence a lift in its source's $\mathcal A_r$. These statements are exactly the established $D^*,I^*$ properties of $\operatorname{Stab}_0$. Thus $S\in\mathcal A_{r+1}(G)$. Existence of stable sets proves nonemptiness, including at the intersection $\mathcal A_\infty(G)$.

The axioms at the limit. Axiom $B$ holds because $\mathcal A_\infty(G)\subseteq\mathcal A_0(G)$. Fix an arrow $e:H\to G$. For $L\in\mathcal A_\infty(H)$, membership in $\mathcal A_{r+1}(H)$ implies $e_*(L)\in\mathcal A_r(G)$ for every $r$. Hence \[ e_*(\mathcal A_\infty(H))\subseteq\mathcal A_\infty(G). \] For the converse fix $K\in\mathcal A_\infty(G)$. For every $r$, the fiber \[ F_r=\{L\in\mathcal A_r(H):e_*(L)=K\} \] is nonempty: use $K\in\mathcal A_{r+1}(G)$ and the incoming-arrow condition. It is compact by continuity of $e_*$, and $F_{r+1}\subseteq F_r$. Compactness gives a point in $\bigcap_rF_r$. Such a point is a set $L\in\mathcal A_\infty(H)$ with $e_*(L)=K$. This proves the reverse inclusion and therefore (eq:arrows). It is the step that justifies stopping at the countable intersection; independently choosing a different lift at each stage would not suffice without compactness.

Maximality. Let $\Phi$ satisfy the three axioms. Axiom $B$ gives $\Phi(G)\subseteq\mathcal A_0(G)$ for every $G$. Suppose inclusion holds at stage $r$. For $K\in\Phi(G)$, its image under every outgoing arrow belongs to the corresponding $\Phi(F)$ and hence $\mathcal A_r(F)$. Under every incoming arrow it has a lift in $\Phi(H)$ and hence $\mathcal A_r(H)$. Thus $K\in\mathcal A_{r+1}(G)$. Induction and intersection prove (eq:largest). No compactness assumption on the original family $\Phi(G)$ has been used. ∎

A constructive criterion for singleton stability

Fix $G$, write $m_i=|A_i|$, and use the common-mass perturbation polytope \[ P_\varepsilon=\{\delta\tau:0\le\delta\le\varepsilon,\ \tau\in\Sigma_G\}. \] For $\eta\in P_1$, its common mass is $\delta(\eta)=\sum_{a\in A_i}\eta_i(a)$, independent of $i$. The perturbed feasible set is $\Sigma_i(\eta)=\{x_i\in\Delta(A_i):x_i(a)\ge\eta_i(a)\}$. Let $E_G$ be the graph of constrained Nash equilibria over $P_1$ and $\pi:E_G\to P_1$ its projection.

The polytope $P_\varepsilon$ has dimension \[ d=1+\sum_i(m_i-1) \] in its linear span and is homeomorphic, with its relative boundary, to $(D^d,S^{d-1})$. For $X\subseteq E_G$ put $X_\varepsilon=X\cap\pi^{-1}(P_\varepsilon)$ and $\partial X_\varepsilon=X\cap\pi^{-1}(\partial P_\varepsilon)$. In particular $\partial X_1$ refers to the boundary of $P_1$, not an arbitrarily chosen boundary of $X$.

Proposition (Continuous-section criterion): Let $x\in\operatorname{NE}(G)$. Suppose that for some $0<\rho<1$ there is a continuous map $s:P_\rho\to\Sigma_G$ with \[ s(0)=x,\qquad s(\eta)\in\operatorname{NE}(G(\eta))\quad(\eta\in P_\rho). \] Then $\{x\}\in\operatorname{Stab}_0(G)$.
Proof: Let $X=\{(\eta,s(\eta)):\eta\in P_\rho\}$. This is a compact subset of $E_G$ with $X_0=\{(0,x)\}$. For every $0<\varepsilon\le\rho$, projection is a homeomorphism of pairs \[ (X_\varepsilon,\partial X_\varepsilon) \longrightarrow(P_\varepsilon,\partial P_\varepsilon). \] Consequently its induced map on degree-$d$ relative Čech cohomology with coefficients in $\mathbb Q$ is an isomorphism between one-dimensional vector spaces, so it is nonzero.

We verify the source's connexity condition separately. Any neighborhood $V$ of $(0,x)$ in $X$ contains the graph over $P_\kappa$ for some $0<\kappa<\rho$: continuity at zero gives this after shrinking $\kappa$. The graph over \[ B_\kappa=\{\eta:0<\delta(\eta)<\kappa,\ \eta_i(a)>0\text{ for all }i,a\} \] is connected and lies in $V\setminus\partial X_1$. Connectedness follows, for example, by parameterizing $B_\kappa$ by $(0,\kappa)\times\operatorname{relint}\Sigma_G$. The closure of this graph contains the graph over $P_{\kappa/2}$, since strictly positive directions are dense. Hence the connected component of $V\setminus\partial X_1$ containing this graph has closure containing a neighborhood of $(0,x)$ in $X$. The same conclusion holds whether closure is taken relative to $V$ or to $X$, since that smaller graph lies in $V$. This is precisely connexity. Together with essentiality it proves the assertion. ∎

Corollary (Equilibrium segments): Suppose $x,y\in\operatorname{NE}(G)$, $y$ is completely mixed, and the whole segment $[x,y]$ lies in $\operatorname{NE}(G)$. Then $\{x\}$ is stable. Consequently, if $\operatorname{NE}(G)$ is star-shaped about a completely mixed equilibrium, every singleton equilibrium is stable and (eq:target) holds for $G$ for any rule selecting nonempty subsets of $\operatorname{NE}(G)$.
Proof: Set $\mu=\min_{i,a}y_i(a)>0$ and choose $0<\rho<\min\{\mu,1\}$. For $\eta\in P_\rho$, define \[ s(\eta)=\left(1-\frac{\delta(\eta)}{\mu}\right)x+ \frac{\delta(\eta)}{\mu}y. \] It is an original Nash equilibrium by the segment assumption. Every coordinate is at least $\delta(\eta)y_i(a)/\mu\ge\delta(\eta)\ge\eta_i(a)$, so it is feasible for the perturbed game. Restricting deviations cannot invalidate an original equilibrium that remains feasible. The continuous-section criterion applies. Every nonempty selected set contains a point $x$, hence its stable singleton. ∎

A whole family with non-isolated quasi-perfect equilibria

Let $n\ge3$ and let $A\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ satisfy \[ A^{\mathsf T}=-A,\qquad A\mathbf 1=0. \] Player $i$ chooses $a_i\in\{0,1\}$ and receives \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-73-6b-65-77-67-61-6d-65} u_i(a)=a_i\sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij}a_j. \end{equation} This is a finite polymatrix game: each payoff is a sum of pairwise terms. Theorem: For every game (eq:skewgame), \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6b-65-72-6e-65-6c-4e-45} \operatorname{NE}(G)=\ker A\cap[0,1]^n. \end{equation} Every equilibrium is quasi-perfect in the canonical simultaneous-move representation and is non-strict. Proof: The payoff advantage of action $1$ over $0$ is $d_i=(Ax)_i$. At a Nash profile, comparison with the feasible mixture $1/2$ gives $d_i(x_i-1/2)\ge0$. Two useful transfer results

Lifting a section through exact duplicates

Proposition: Suppose $H$ is obtained by exact duplication of pure actions of $G$. If $x\in\operatorname{NE}(G)$ has a continuous lower-bound equilibrium section, then every mixed-profile lift $\widetilde x$ of $x$ under the duplication map has such a section in $H$. In particular, every singleton $\{\widetilde x\}$ is stable.
Proof: Let $q_i:B_i\twoheadrightarrow A_i$ identify duplicates, so $u_i^H(b)=u_i^G(q(b))$. Given lower bounds $\widetilde\eta$, aggregate them as $\eta_i(a)=\sum_{q_i(b)=a}\widetilde\eta_i(b)$. Common perturbation mass is preserved. Write $s(\eta)$ for a section through $x$ in $G$. For each group $q_i^{-1}(a)$ choose weights $\lambda_i(b)\ge0$ summing to $1$. When $x_i(a)>0$, choose $\lambda_i(b)=\widetilde x_i(b)/x_i(a)$; when $x_i(a)=0$, all corresponding $\widetilde x_i(b)$ are zero and arbitrary weights suffice. Define \[ \widetilde s_i(b)=\widetilde\eta_i(b)+\lambda_i(b) \left(s_i(a;\eta)-\eta_i(a)\right),\qquad a=q_i(b). \] These coordinates meet their lower bounds, sum to one, and aggregate to $s_i(\eta)$. Since $s(\eta)$ is a constrained equilibrium, no such deviation is profitable. The section criterion completes the proof, including zero-probability boundary groups. ∎

We show directly that $K_0$ is not stable in $G_0$, using the stipulated connexity condition. Suppose it had a witness $X\subseteq E_{G_0}$ with $X_0=\{(0,(D,R))\}$. Let $p=\Pr(U)$ and $q=\Pr(L)$, and consider the relative neighborhood \[ V=X\cap\{\delta(\eta)<1/4,\ p<1/4,\ q<1/4\} \] of $X_0$. If all coordinates of $\eta$ are strictly positive, then $q\ge\eta_2(L)>0$ makes $U$ strictly preferable to $D$, and $p\ge\eta_1(U)>0$ makes $L$ strictly preferable to $R$. Every constrained equilibrium must therefore satisfy \[ p=1-\eta_1(D)>3/4,\qquad q=1-\eta_2(R)>3/4 \] when $\delta(\eta)<1/4$. No such profile belongs to $V$. Consequently every parameter occurring in $V$ lies on the relative boundary of $P_1$: it has a zero coordinate, including the zero-mass case. Thus $V\setminus\partial X_1$ is empty, contradicting connexity. Hence $K_0$ is not stable. Its only nonempty subset is itself. ∎

The positive-$\theta$ games are already nondegenerate bimatrix games. If a particular stronger genericity convention is desired, take generic payoff arrays arbitrarily close to each $G_{1/k}$ within the open region where $(D,R)$ remains strict. They still converge to $G_0$ and give the same counterexample. This invalidates the unqualified step "approximate by generic games and pass stability to the limit." It does not refute (eq:target): no globally compatible rule selecting $K_0$ is constructed. Indeed the simultaneous-move backward-induction requirement already excludes this particular $K_0$.

A polynomial topological obstruction to a second shortcut

The source identifies a gap between coincidence properties and rational cohomological essentiality in the nongeneric case [gw, Section 10]. The following classical Hopf-map construction makes the distinction explicit. It is not asserted to be a Nash perturbation graph.

Let $D^m$ be the closed Euclidean unit ball. Regard $D^4$ as the unit ball in $\mathbb C^2$ and define the real polynomial map \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-68-6f-70-66} F(z_1,z_2)=\left(2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline z_2),\, 2\operatorname{Im}(z_1\overline z_2),\,|z_1|^2-|z_2|^2\right). \end{equation} Its boundary restriction is a Hopf map, up to the harmless choice of orientation. The non-null-homotopy of this map is a standard input; see [hatcher, Example 4.51, p. 380; Section 4.B, p. 427].

Proposition: The map $F:D^4\to D^3$ has a coincidence with every continuous map $g:D^4\to D^3$. Nevertheless, \[ F^*:\check H^3(D^3,S^2;\mathbb Q)\longrightarrow \check H^3(D^4,S^3;\mathbb Q) \] is zero. The restriction of $F$ to any compact subpair $(Y,Y\cap S^3)$ also induces the zero map in degree three.
Proof: Direct calculation gives $\|F(z)\|^2=(|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2)^2$; hence $F^{-1}(S^2)=S^3$. Suppose a continuous $g:D^4\to D^3$ has no coincidence with $F$. Then \[ R(z)=\frac{F(z)-g(z)}{\|F(z)-g(z)\|} \] is a continuous map $D^4\to S^2$. On the boundary $S^3$, consider \[ h_t(z)=\frac{F(z)-t\,g(z)}{\|F(z)-t\,g(z)\|},\qquad 0\le t\le1. \] For $t<1$, the denominator is nonzero because $\|F(z)\|=1$ and $\|t g(z)\|\le t<1$; for $t=1$ nonvanishing follows from the no-coincidence hypothesis. The map $h_t$ is a homotopy from $F|_{S^3}$ to $R|_{S^3}$. But $R|_{S^3}$ extends over $D^4$ and is therefore null-homotopic, which contradicts the non-null-homotopy of the Hopf map. Thus a coincidence exists for every $g$.

For the ball pairs, relative Čech cohomology agrees with ordinary relative cohomology, giving \[ \check H^3(D^3,S^2;\mathbb Q)\cong\mathbb Q,\qquad \check H^3(D^4,S^3;\mathbb Q)=0. \] Therefore $F^*=0$. For a compact $Y\subseteq D^4$, restriction of the map of pairs to $(Y,Y\cap S^3)$ factors through $(D^4,S^3)$; functoriality shows that the induced degree-three map remains zero. ∎

The graph $\{(F(z),z):z\in D^4\}\subseteq D^3\times D^4$ is compact and semialgebraic, and projection to its first coordinate is $F$ under a homeomorphism. Thus merely insisting that a map be a polynomially described coordinate projection does not cure the obstruction. The example rules out a general topological inference from coincidence to rational essentiality. To turn it into a counterexample to prb_34ecf5fa262f4a85a0fbe4af9837c46b, one would still need to realize suitable structure in an actual Nash perturbation graph and prove compatibility with all comparisons in $B,D^*,I^*$. Neither requirement is established here.
