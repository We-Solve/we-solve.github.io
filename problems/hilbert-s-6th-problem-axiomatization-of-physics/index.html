Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.

Definitions and notation.
An axiomatization specifies mathematical objects, axioms governing their relations and evolution, and the deductions to be established within that framework. In mechanics, a microscopic model describes individual particles; a continuum model describes fields such as mass density and velocity. A limit from one to the other must specify a scaling, topology of convergence and admissible initial data. Hilbert’s original sixth problem is a programme concerning axioms of physical theories, especially probability and mechanics; it is not a single formal proposition.
Catalogue wording retained for traceability.
Develop a mathematical framework that axiomatizes physics, particularly mechanics, thermodynamics, and probability theory. Hilbert’s original programme especially calls for an axiomatic treatment of probability, including mean-value methods in mathematical physics, and a rigorous development of limiting processes leading from atomic mechanics to continuum mechanics.
Scope and source qualification.
The catalogue includes thermodynamics as a later framing. Hilbert supplies no fixed final axiom system or universally agreed completion criterion. Existing axiomatizations of particular theories constitute context rather than a settlement of this entire programme.

Short English statement

Give rigorous axioms for physical theories and justify the passage from microscopic particle mechanics to continuum descriptions.

A precise microscopic-to-continuum benchmark.
Hilbert's programme has no fixed terminal axiom system. To test one component, consider noninteracting particles in $\mathbb R^d$, with positions and velocities $(x_i,v_i)$ and empirical probability measure $\mu_N(0)=N^{-1}\sum_i\delta_{(x_i,v_i)}$. Use the distance $|x-x'|+|v-v'|$ on phase space, and let $W_1$ be the infimum, over couplings of two probability measures, of the mean distance. Assume finite first moments and $W_1(\mu_N(0),\mu_0)\to0$. The free flow is $F_t(x,v)=(x+tv,v)$. For $0\le t\le T$, \[d(F_tz,F_tz')\le(1+T)d(z,z'),\qquad W_1((F_t)_\#\mu_N(0),(F_t)_\#\mu_0)\le(1+T)W_1(\mu_N(0),\mu_0).\] To prove the second assertion, push any initial coupling forward by $(F_t,F_t)$, integrate the first inequality, then take the infimum. This gives convergence uniformly on every bounded time interval without a stochastic independence assumption.
Continuum equation and a closure failure.
For every compactly supported smooth test function $\phi$, differentiation under the integral gives $d\int\phi\,d\mu_t/dt=\int v\cdot\nabla_x\phi\,d\mu_t$, the weak free-transport equation. Yet this kinetic limit is not automatically a closed continuum mechanics system. If a density $f$ has mass $\rho=\int f\,dv$ and mean velocity $u=\rho^{-1}\int vf\,dv$, its momentum equation contains $\int v\otimes vf\,dv$. At a point, the velocity laws $\delta_0$ and $(\delta_e+\delta_{-e})/2$, for a unit vector $e$, have equal mass and mean but different second moments. Therefore mass and momentum alone do not determine the stress.
