Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

For unit vectors u,v, their angular distance is $\arccos\langle u,v\rangle$. A finite set C covers $S^{n-1}$ at angle $\alpha$ when every unit vector has angular distance at most $\alpha$ from some member of C. The available points are independent uniform sphere samples, and the selected cover must use a subsequence of them.

Statement recorded in the supplied source.
Parameters: $n \geq 2$ and $\alpha > 0$. Given independent uniformly random $x_1, x_2, \dots\in S^{n-1} = \{v \in \mathbb R^n : \|v\|_2 = 1\}$, how quickly can we find a subsequence that covers $S^{n-1}$? (Here, "covers" means that every point of $S^{n-1}$ is within angle $\alpha$ of a point in the subsequence.) For example, consider the case when $n = 1000$ and $\alpha = 75^\circ$. [S2]

Research attempt: A finite net certificate and explicit random-sampling bounds

If $\alpha\geq\pi$, one point covers the sphere. Hence assume $0<\alpha<\pi$ and choose $0<\delta<\alpha$. Put $\varepsilon=2\sin(\delta/2)$. There is a finite angular $\delta$-net of size \[ M\leq(1+2/\varepsilon)^n. \tag{1} \] To prove this, take points greedily whose Euclidean distances exceed $\varepsilon$. Open Euclidean balls of radius $\varepsilon/2$ around them are disjoint and lie inside the ball of radius $1+\varepsilon/2$. Comparing $n$-dimensional volumes bounds their number by $1$, so the greedy process must stop. Maximality makes every sphere point Euclidean distance at most $\varepsilon$ from a selected point. For unit vectors, $\|u-v\|=2\sin(\angle(u,v)/2)$, giving the angular net property.

For $0<\beta<\pi$, let $q_n(\beta)$ be the fraction of sphere area in an angular cap of radius $\beta$. Polar coordinates about its center give \[ q_n(\beta)={\int_0^\beta\sin^{n-2}\theta\,d\theta \over\int_0^\pi\sin^{n-2}\theta\,d\theta}. \tag{2} \] The area element has the factor $\sin^{n-2}\theta$ because each latitude is a sphere scaled by $\sin\theta$ in $n-2$ tangent directions; the remaining polar direction has unit length. For $n=2$ the same formula is $\beta/\pi$, including the two arcs.

Set $\beta=\alpha-\delta$. A fixed net point is missed by all $m$ independent samples with probability $(1-q_n(\beta))^m\leq e^{-m q_n(\beta)}$. A union bound and the angular triangle inequality show that the samples cover the whole sphere at angle $\alpha$ with failure probability at most $M e^{-m q_n(\beta)}$. Thus \[ m\geq{n\log(1+2/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\eta)\over q_n(\alpha-\delta)} \tag{3} \] is sufficient for failure probability at most $\eta\in(0,1)$. The angular triangle inequality can be checked directly: decompose $u,v$ around a unit $z$; Cauchy--Schwarz gives $u\cdot v\geq\cos(a+b)$ when $a=\angle(u,z)$, $b=\angle(z,v)$ and $a+b\leq\pi$, while larger sums are trivial.

If a specified net is available, scan the samples and retain one witness within angle $\beta$ for each net point. Once all have a witness, their union is a certifiably covering subsequence of at most $M$ samples. Checking all pairs costs $O(mMn)$ scalar arithmetic operations in a real-arithmetic model. Bound $1$ is an existence bound, not a free algorithm for computing an optimal net. A completely explicit larger net is available: take all nonzero vectors on the grid of spacing $h=\varepsilon/(2\sqrt n)$ with coordinate indices $|z_i|\leq\lceil1/h\rceil$, and normalize them. Rounding any sphere point to this grid gives a nonzero vector $y$ with distance at most $\varepsilon/4$; normalizing changes the distance by at most another $\varepsilon/4$, so this is also an angular $\delta$-net. Its size is at most $(4\sqrt n/\varepsilon+3)^n$. Using this larger count in the union bound yields an explicit, potentially enormous certificate algorithm.

There is also a necessary cardinality bound for every cover, independent of randomness: $k$ caps have total area at most $k q_n(\alpha)$, so $k\geq1/q_n(\alpha)$. This need not match $3$. For a fully elementary lower estimate on its denominator when $0<\beta\leq\pi/2$ and $0<h_0<\beta$, \[ q_n(\beta)\geq {h_0\over\pi}\sin^{n-2}(\beta-h_0), \tag{4} \] by restricting the numerator integral to $[\beta-h_0,\beta]$ and bounding the denominator by $\pi$.

Verification and adversarial audit
