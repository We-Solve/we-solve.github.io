A complete small procedure and its limited accessibility claim

The source asks for an actual beginner on-ramp. Here is a self-contained paper-executable procedure for finding a greatest common divisor, rather than only an outline. The prerequisites are recognizing nonnegative whole numbers and dividing with a remainder. No account, installation, timed task, or spoken instruction is required. These design choices are explicit, but their accessibility has not been tested with users. Choose two nonnegative whole numbers $a,b$, not both zero. Keep a two-column written record labeled $a$ and $b$. If $b=0$, stop and report $a$. Otherwise divide $a$ by $b$ to obtain the remainder $r$, the unique integer with $0\le r<b$ and $a=qb+r$ for an integer $q$. On a new line write the old $b$ in the first column and $r$ in the second, then repeat. If the input contains fractions or negative numbers, it is outside this procedure's stated input contract. If the remainder is not smaller than the divisor, redo that division before continuing.
Proposition: This procedure terminates and reports $\gcd(a,b)$ for every permitted input.
Proof: For integers $a=qb+r$, a positive integer divides both $a,b$ if and only if it divides both $b,r$: subtraction gives one direction and addition the other. Thus each new line preserves the set of common positive divisors. At a nonterminal step the second entry strictly decreases and remains nonnegative. A strictly decreasing sequence of nonnegative integers cannot continue indefinitely, so some line has second entry zero. Its first entry is positive, because a pair not both zero never becomes $(0,0)$ under the update. The common positive divisors of $(a,0)$ are precisely the positive divisors of $a$, whose greatest is $a$. The preserved divisor set proves correctness. ∎
For a worked run, $(252,105)$ becomes $(105,42)$, then $(42,21)$, then $(21,0)$, so the answer is 21. A reader can check the divisions $252=2\cdot105+42$, $105=2\cdot42+21$, and $42=2\cdot21$.

Verification and remaining gaps

The procedure was implemented in a short Python check and tested against the standard-library gcd on all 440 nonzero pairs with entries from 0 through 20.
