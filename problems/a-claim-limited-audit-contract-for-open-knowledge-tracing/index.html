Testing policy selection from logs with no action coverage

For one possible use of knowledge tracing, suppose a tutor must choose between two instructional actions $A,B$. Logged data use only $A$. In two possible worlds, the probability of a correct response under $A$ is $4/5$. Under $B$ it is $1/10$ in $W_0$ and $9/10$ in $W_1$. Repeated logged responses to $A$ are independent with the same distribution in both worlds. These are hypothetical action-response laws, not claims about real learners or latent skills.
Proposition: No amount of these $A$-only data identifies which action is better. If a policy chooses $B$ with overall probability $p$ based on such data, its maximum expected regret across the two worlds is at least $7/80$, attained by $p=1/8$.
Proof: The complete distribution of every finite log is identical in both worlds, so any data-dependent randomized policy has the same marginal $p$ in each. In $W_0$, choosing $B$ loses $4/5-1/10=7/10$ relative to $A$, giving regret $7p/10$. In $W_1$, choosing $A$ loses $9/10-4/5=1/10$, giving regret $(1-p)/10$. The maximum of these two affine functions is minimized where they meet: $7p=1-p$, hence $p=1/8$, with value $7/80$. If $p$ is smaller, the decreasing second function exceeds that value; if larger, the increasing first function exceeds it. This also proves attainability by the fixed randomized policy. ∎
The failed inference is that accurate prediction on logged actions certifies an improved instructional policy on unobserved actions. The example concerns missing action support and says nothing about whether a specific knowledge-tracing package has this problem or whether latent-state inference is impossible under appropriate data.

Verification and remaining gaps

The regret pair was evaluated exactly at $p=0,1/8,1$, with minimum maximum $7/80$. No current-tool survey, fitted model, learner dataset or intervention evaluation was performed.
