Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

A simple $d$-regular graph has degree $d$ at every vertex. For even $n$, a bisection is a partition $V=S\sqcup(V\setminus S)$ with $|S|=n/2$; its size is the number of edges joining the two parts. Let $\operatorname{MaxBis}(G)$ and $\operatorname{MinBis}(G)$ be the maximum and minimum such sizes. The author's full lecture notes use $G\sim G^{\mathrm{reg}}(n,d)$, the uniform distribution on simple $d$-regular graphs on $n$ labeled vertices, and state the source-corrected conjecture \[ \frac{\operatorname{MaxBis}(G)+\operatorname{MinBis}(G)}{n} =\frac d2+o(1) \] for each fixed $d$ as admissible even $n$ tends to infinity. Equivalently, the unnormalized sum is $dn/2+o(n)$. The asymptotic random-graph assertion is interpreted as convergence in probability: for every $\eta>0$, the probability that the absolute difference of the normalized sum from $d/2$ exceeds $\eta$ tends to zero.

Verbatim supplied catalogue question

Given a $d$ -regular graph on $n$ nodes $G$ . Let $MaxBis(G)$ and $MinBis(G)$ denote, respectively, the size of its largest and smallest bisection. Is it true that for every $d$ $MaxBis(G)$ $+ MinBis(G)$ $=$ $\frac{d}{2} + o(1)$ , where $o(1)$ is a term that goes to zero as $n$ grows?

Attempt and results

The source-corrected target is the convergence in probability of $(\operatorname{MaxBis}G+\operatorname{MinBis}G)/n$ to $d/2$ for uniform simple $d$-regular graphs and each fixed $d$. The verbatim supplied wording omits normalization and randomness; both are preserved above and explicitly separated. All graphs below have even $n$ and no loops or multiple edges.
Exact matching case.
For $d=0$ every cut is zero. For $d=1$, every graph is a perfect matching. A bisection with one endpoint of each edge in either side has size $n/2$, which is the total number of edges and hence the maximum. If $n\equiv0\pmod4$, choose $n/4$ complete edges for one side; the minimum is zero. If $n\equiv2\pmod4$, each side has odd cardinality, so at least one matching edge must cross. Choosing $(n-2)/4$ entire edges and one endpoint of another attains one. Hence the normalized sum tends to $1/2$ for $d=1$, for every graph and thus for the uniform model. This is an elementary boundary case rather than new progress in fixed degree at least three.
Exact maximum and a minimum bound for degree two.
Every simple 2-regular graph is a disjoint union of cycles. Write $o$ for its number of odd cycles. No cut crosses all edges of an odd cycle, so every cut has at most $n-o$ edges. Alternating sides on even cycles crosses every edge and balances each cycle. On an odd cycle, an alternating assignment crosses all but one edge and gives a side imbalance of one. Because $n$ is even, $o$ is even. Orient half of these imbalances in each direction; this attains a bisection with $n-o$ crossing edges. Thus $\operatorname{MaxBis}=n-o$. To construct a small bisection, order the cycles, place whole cycles on one side until the next one would pass $n/2$, and then take exactly the needed number of consecutive vertices from that next cycle. Only that cycle can cross, in at most two edges. Consequently \[ 1-o/n\le\frac{\operatorname{MaxBis}+\operatorname{MinBis}}n \le1-o/n+2/n. \] This proves the target for any deterministic sequence of 2-regular graphs whose number of odd cycles is $o(n)$. No distributional theorem on the uniform random model is being asserted without proof here.
Failure of a graph-by-graph symmetry argument.
For $n$ divisible by six, take $n/3$ disjoint triangles. The construction above gives $\operatorname{MaxBis}=2n/3$. Choosing $n/6$ whole triangles on each side gives $\operatorname{MinBis}=0$. Their normalized sum is $2/3$, not the conjectured $d/2=1$. Therefore a proof that tries to show exact or asymptotic complementary extremes for every regular graph cannot work. Complementing a cut merely exchanges its two sides and preserves its crossing count; it does not map the maximum to the total-edge complement of the minimum. This is the failed inference, and the triangles give a concrete witness. The example does not refute a high-probability assertion about uniformly random graphs.

Context and adversarial audit

Bandeira's primary notes, Conjecture 8.5, were inspected to verify the factor $1/n$ and the uniform-random-regular hypothesis. The primary abstract of Zdeborova and Boettcher, arXiv:0912.4861, concerns a related maximum-cut and minimum-bisection conjecture supported there by spin-glass reasoning and numerics; maximum cut is not automatically maximum bisection. The audit checked parity, balancing odd cycles, and that the triangle family only refutes a deterministic strengthening. The results above are elementary checks and failed-route analysis, not a new probabilistic theorem. No enumeration, simulation, or theorem prover was used in this record.
