Verbatim supplied catalogue question

For every transitive permutation group that is not $2$-homogeneous, are there infinitely many core points up to integral translations of the fixed space? Equivalently, does the conjecture hold in the remaining case of primitive permutation groups with rational invariant subspaces?

An invariant-lattice route

The report, printed pages 2750--2751, already distinguishes established infinite families from the remaining primitive rational-invariant case. We try to generate core points by lattice automorphisms commuting with the group, and give both an exact working example and a precise limit on extending that method. No inference from this example to every group is made.

Let $G\le S_n$ and let $T\in\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb Z)$ commute with every $g\in G$. If $z$ is a core point, then so is $Tz$. Indeed, $\operatorname{conv}(GTz)=T\operatorname{conv}(Gz)$. Both $T$ and $T^{-1}$ map integer vectors to integer vectors, so its lattice points are exactly $T(Gz)=G(Tz)$. Iteration preserves core points. For a transitive group, $e_0$ is a core point because its orbit is all unit vectors and their convex hull is $\{x\ge0:\sum_i x_i=1\}$, whose only integer points are those unit vectors.

A fully explicit infinite family for C5

Use coordinates $0,\ldots,4$ and let $P$ cyclically permute them. Define \[ T=(I+P)^4-3(I+P+P^2+P^3+P^4) =-2I+P+3P^2+P^3-2P^4. \] This is an integer matrix commuting with $P$, with every column sum one. Let $\zeta=\exp(2\pi i/5)$. The cyclic Fourier vectors diagonalize $P$: substitution in the cyclic shift shows their eigenvalues are the five roots $\zeta^r$, and their independence follows by orthogonality of geometric sums. The eigenvalue of $T$ at $r=0$ is $16-15=1$, while those at $r=1,\ldots,4$ are $(1+\zeta^r)^4$. Since $\prod_{r=1}^4(x-\zeta^r)=1+x+x^2+x^3+x^4$, evaluating at $x=-1$ gives $\prod_{r=1}^4(1+\zeta^r)=1$. Hence $\det T=1$. The adjugate formula now shows that $T^{-1}$ has integer entries. It follows from the preceding invariant-lattice argument that \[ z_k=T^k e_0\qquad(k=0,1,2,\ldots) \] are all core points for $C_5=\langle P\rangle$.

They give infinitely many classes under the translations in the question. Their coordinate sums are all one. The fixed vectors of a cyclic transitive action have all coordinates equal, so an integral fixed-space translation is $a\mathbf1$, $a\in\mathbb Z$, and changes the sum by $5a$. Two sum-one vectors can be equivalent only when they are equal. On a unit Fourier vector for $r=1$, the projection of $e_0$ has magnitude $1/\sqrt5$, and the eigenvalue of $T$ has magnitude $|1+\zeta|^4=(2\cos(\pi/5))^4>1$. Thus $\|T^ke_0\|\ge(2\cos(\pi/5))^{4k}/\sqrt5$ is unbounded, and in fact that projection magnitude strictly increases with $k$. All $z_k$ are distinct.

The group is transitive and not 2-homogeneous: cyclic shifts preserve whether an unordered pair has cyclic distance one or two. It is primitive because a nontrivial invariant block partition of a transitive action has equally sized blocks, whose size divides the prime number five. This example nevertheless belongs to the already studied irrational-invariant mechanism, not the remaining source case. For example, its real two-dimensional Fourier plane has trace $2\cos(2\pi/5)$ for $P$; this number is irrational since it is a root of $x^2+x-1=0$, as follows by grouping the geometric sum of fifth roots. A rational invariant plane would give a rational matrix for the restriction and thus rational trace, so this Fourier plane is not rational.

Why the same unit-power argument can stop

Consider the additional structural hypothesis that every rational matrix commuting with a group acts as a rational scalar on each member of a fixed direct-sum decomposition of $\mathbb Q^n$ into $s$ invariant subspaces. This is an explicit conditional hypothesis, not a claim about all groups with rational invariant subspaces. Any commuting unimodular integer matrix $T$ then has scalar eigenvalues $a_1,\ldots,a_s\in\mathbb Q$. Each is an integer, because it is a rational root of the monic integer characteristic polynomial of $T$: writing a root in lowest terms in the monic equation shows its denominator divides one. Applying the same argument to the integer inverse $T^{-1}$ shows $1/a_i$ is also an integer. Hence $a_i=\pm1$. There are at most $2^s$ choices of such a scalar operator, and every such $T$ satisfies $T^2=I$. Powers of these commuting scalar operators therefore cannot generate an infinite core-point family.

Thus the working construction does not automatically solve the rational case. Even where this conditional barrier applies, it is a barrier only to this lattice-automorphism route; core points may arise by other constructions. It does not contradict the conjecture.
