CS-182 · Math-4001

Compatible Triangulations

Status Open · unsolvedmath.comLLM status Open unverified

Statement

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
$\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, and $\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$ denote the integers, rationals, reals and complex numbers. Any other conventions are specified locally.

A planar point set is in general position if no three points are collinear. A triangulation of it subdivides its convex hull into triangles with that point set as its vertices and with disjoint triangle interiors. Triangulations of $S_1,S_2$ are compatible if a bijection $\phi:S_1\to S_2$ identifies their face lattices; equivalently, a triple spans an empty triangular face in one precisely when its image spans an empty triangular face in the other. The bijection is free to be chosen, and no extra Steiner vertices are added.

Statement recorded in the supplied source.
For every two $n$-point planar sets in general position having the same number of convex-hull vertices, do compatible triangulations exist? [S2]

Literature and context check

The consulted TOPP Problem 38 page states the free-bijection, no-Steiner-point conjecture and reports known cases with few interior points. Consequently the small example below is a test of an algorithmic recipe, not a new counterexample to the conjecture or a claim beyond those known cases.

Research attempt: A common fan-refinement recipe can produce incompatible outputs

If all points are in convex position, matching cyclic boundary orders and choosing corresponding fans gives compatible triangulations. If there is exactly one interior point, join it to each hull vertex; every ray from it meets a unique hull edge except on boundary rays, so these triangles partition the hull. Matching the interior points and cyclic hull orders then gives compatibility. The attempted extension was to keep this interior fan and insert remaining interior points into the triangles containing them.

The following explicit pair defeats that recipe. Use the common hull \[ A=(0,0),\ B=(6,0),\ C=(7,5),\ D=(0,6), \qquad o=(2,2),\ q=(2,1/2). \] In $S_1$ add $r_1=(3,7/10)$; in $S_2$ add $r_2=(3,4)$. All triples in each seven-point set have nonzero oriented area, and the four listed boundary points form a strictly convex quadrilateral. Both $q,r_1$ lie inside $oAB$, while $r_2$ lies inside $oCD$. These inclusions follow by testing the three directed edges of each counterclockwise triangle; no point is on an edge.

Start in each set with the fan from $o$, insert $q$, and then insert $r_i$, joining an inserted point to all three vertices of its containing triangle. In $S_1$ the first insertion divides $oAB$ into three triangles, every one of which contains $q$ as a vertex. Thus inserting $r_1$ creates the edge $qr_1$; it also need not create $or_1$ in every position, so that edge must be checked rather than assumed. For the displayed coordinates, $r_1$ lies in triangle $qBo$: its directed cross products are positive. Consequently both $or_1$ and $qr_1$ occur, and the subgraph induced by the three interior vertices is the triangle $K_3$. In $S_2$, $r_2$ is inserted into the unchanged triangle $oCD$, so its only interior neighbor is $o$. The induced interior subgraph is the path $q,o,r_2$.

Any face-lattice isomorphism of the completed triangulations preserves the boundary and hence the set of interior vertices. It cannot take a triangle induced on those vertices to a path. Therefore these particular outputs are incompatible even if a new, unrestricted vertex bijection is allowed after the construction. Merely renaming hull vertices or the interior roots does not fix the edge-count discrepancy. Other triangulations may work, as the source's known small-interior cases suggest; this is not a counterexample to their existence.

The failed induction tried to use only equal total interior counts. A recursive compatible construction needs compatible distributions among corresponding faces, or a mechanism for changing the partial triangulations when those distributions differ. The example shows that unconstrained sequential insertion does not preserve that invariant.

Scope and remaining gaps
