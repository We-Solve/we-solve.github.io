Let $f$ and $g$ be degree-$d$ multilinear real polynomials on $\{-1,1\}^n$ such that every variable has relative influence at most $\delta$ in both polynomials. Can $\delta$ be chosen inverse-polynomially in $d$ while still guaranteeing a point $x\in\{-1,1\}^n$ for which $f(x)\neq0$ and $g(x)\neq0$? [S2]

Literature and context check

The primary problem session on printed p. 445 defines relative influence as squared coefficient mass containing a variable divided by total squared coefficient mass, including the constant term in the denominator. It reports an exponential sufficient threshold and an inverse-quadratic obstruction, and asks for polynomial dependence on degree. The following degree-one calculation and amplification tests do not improve those general bounds.

Research attempt: The affine threshold and failed product amplification

Use the uniform Boolean cube and nonzero real multilinear polynomials, so relative influences are defined. For affine functions \[ f=a+\sum_i b_i x_i,\qquad g=c+\sum_i d_i x_i, \] we prove that if they never are simultaneously nonzero, then each has a variable of relative influence $1/2$. Thus a common nonzero point is guaranteed when all influences of both functions are strictly below $1/2$, and this degree-one threshold is sharp.

A nonconstant affine function vanishes on at most half of the cube: fix a coordinate with nonzero coefficient; at most one of its two signs can be a zero for each assignment of the other coordinates. A nonzero constant never vanishes, and would force the other function identically zero if the supports were disjoint. Therefore both functions are nonconstant. Disjoint nonzero supports, each of measure at least $1/2$, force both supports to have measure exactly $1/2$.

Choose $b_i\ne0$. Equality in the preceding count means that for every assignment of the other coordinates, \[ a+\sum_{j\ne i}b_jx_j\in\{-b_i,b_i\}. \] An affine function with at least two nonzero variable coefficients has at least three distinct values on its cube: fixing other coordinates, the two varying terms give extreme values differing by $2(|u|+|v|)$ and a value strictly between the extremes. Hence there is at most one nonzero coefficient besides $b_i$. If there is none, the displayed condition gives $a=\pm b_i$, and the relative influence is $b_i^2/(a^2+b_i^2)=1/2$. If there is one, say $b_j$, the two distinct values $a\pm b_j$ must equal the set $\{-b_i,b_i\}$, so $a=0$ and $|b_j|=|b_i|$; each of the two influences is again $1/2$. Apply the same proof to $g$. Sharpness follows from $f=1+x_1$, $g=1-x_1$, which have disjoint supports and relative influence $1/2$.

Why independent multiplication does not lower influence.
For nonzero multilinear functions $F$ and $H$ on disjoint variable blocks, the Fourier coefficients of $FH$ are products. Therefore \[ \sum_S \widehat{FH}(S)^2=\Big(\sum_A\widehat F(A)^2\Big) \Big(\sum_B\widehat H(B)^2\Big). \] For a variable in the $F$ block, restricting the first sum to coefficients containing that variable gives the same second factor, which cancels in the relative influence. Thus multiplying independent copies raises degree but does not reduce any block's maximum relative influence. It cannot turn the sharp affine disjoint-support pair into a low-influence high-degree obstruction.

Averaging independent copies does lower some normalized coordinate weights, but loses disjointness. With $F=\sum_{i=1}^N(1+x_i)$ and $G=\sum_{i=1}^N(1-x_i)$, every summand pair has disjoint supports, yet \[ FG=N^2-\Big(\sum_i x_i\Big)^2>0 \] whenever the signs are mixed, for $N\geq2$. Cross terms invalidate the proposed transfer of the no-common-support property. A useful polynomial-degree route must control these cross terms rather than treating them as absent.

Verification and adversarial audit

The proof uses the total Fourier mass including constants, as specified by the primary report. Equality of support measures is derived before classifying affine zero sets. Strictness is necessary at $1/2$. The product calculation requires disjoint variable blocks; the averaging counterexample is evaluated at mixed signs and includes $N\geq2$.
