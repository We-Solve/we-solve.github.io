Statement

Fix $n\in\mathbb{N}$, and let $\mathrm{ex}(n;C_4)$ denote the maximum number of edges in a (simple, undirected) graph on $n$ vertices that contains no (not-necessarily-induced) copy of the 4-cycle $C_4$.

For a graph $G$, let $N_G(v)$ be the (open) neighborhood of $v$, and define the number of (not-necessarily-induced) $4$-cycles in $G$ to be \[ C_4(G):=\#\{\text{subgraphs of $G$ that are isomorphic to $C_4$}\}, \] where two $4$-cycles are considered distinct if their edge-sets differ (equivalently, distinct cyclic orderings on the same $4$ vertices arising from different edge choices in $G$ count as distinct).

Question (Erdős--Simonovits supersaturation for $C_4$): Does there exist an absolute constant $c>0$ such that for all sufficiently large $n$, every graph $G$ on $n$ vertices with $e(G)>\mathrm{ex}(n;C_4)$ satisfies \[ C_4(G)\ge c\,n^{1/2}? \] Here $\gg n^{1/2}$ means $\ge c n^{1/2}$ for some absolute $c>0$.

Restatement from Math-659-A3 r1, with the source manuscript’s qualifications.

For sufficiently large $q=2^k$,

Immediate corollary.
For sufficiently large $q=2^k$ and every $1\le t<q^{1/8}/30$, \[ h(q^2+q+1,t)=t(q-1)=t\sqrt{q^2+q+1}+O(t). \] In particular, \[ h(q^2+q+1,1)=q-1=(1+o(1))\sqrt{q^2+q+1}. \] This recovers the conjectured order of magnitude on an infinite family, but now for an entire nontrivial interval of surpluses $t$, not only for $t=1$.

5.3 A new lower bound in terms of the Reiman defect

Proposition 5.3 (defect form of the Round-2 method).
Let \[ R(n):=\frac n4\left(1+\sqrt{4n-3}\right), \qquad D(n):=R(n)-\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4). \] Then for every integer $t\ge 1$, \begin{equation} \label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-64-65-66-65-63-74-2d-62-6f-75-6e-64} h(n,t)\ge \frac12\max\left\{0,\sqrt{4n-3}\,(t-D(n))+\frac{2(t-D(n))^2}{n}\right\}. \end{equation}
Proof.
Let $G$ be an $n$-vertex graph with \[ e(G)=\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)+t. \] Write $m:=e(G)$ and $N:=\binom n2$. By the Round-2 linear surplus inequality and Jensen's inequality, \[ \#C_4(G)\ge \frac12\max\left\{0,\,\left(\frac{2m^2}{n}-m\right)-N\right\}. \] Hence \begin{equation} \label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-68-2d-6c-6f-77-65-72-2d-73-74-61-72-74} h(n,t)\ge \frac12\max\left\{0,\,\frac{2m^2}{n}-m-N\right\}. \end{equation} Now set \[ m=\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)+t=R(n)-D(n)+t. \] We claim that \begin{equation} \label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-52-2d-65-71-75-61-6c-69-74-79} \frac{2R(n)^2}{n}-R(n)=\binom n2. \end{equation} Indeed, with $s:=\sqrt{4n-3}$, \[ R(n)=\frac n4(1+s), \] so \[ \frac{2R(n)^2}{n}-R(n) =\frac{n}{8}(1+2s+s^2)-\frac n4(1+s) =\frac{n}{8}(s^2-1) =\frac{n}{8}(4n-4) =\frac{n(n-1)}2. \] Thus (eq:R-equality) holds.

Substituting $m=R(n)+(t-D(n))$ into (eq:h-lower-start) and using (eq:R-equality), we obtain \[ \frac{2m^2}{n}-m-N =\left(\frac{4R(n)}{n}-1\right)(t-D(n)) + \frac{2(t-D(n))^2}{n}. \] But \[ \frac{4R(n)}{n}-1=\sqrt{4n-3}, \] so \[ \frac{2m^2}{n}-m-N =\sqrt{4n-3}\,(t-D(n))+\frac{2(t-D(n))^2}{n}. \] Substituting this into (eq:h-lower-start) gives (eq:defect-bound). $\square$

Interpretation.
Proposition 5.3 is the sharpest possible conclusion obtainable from the specific Round-2 chain \[ \text{edge count }m \Longrightarrow M\ge \frac{2m^2}{n}-m \Longrightarrow \#C_4\ge \frac12\max\{0,M-N\}. \] It packages the whole route into the single parameter $D(n)=R(n)-\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)$.

5.4 Evaluating the defect on $n=q^2+q+1$

Corollary 5.4.
Suppose that for some integer $q\ge 14$ one has \[ \mathrm{ex}(q^2+q+1,C_4)=\frac12 q(q+1)^2. \] Then with $n=q^2+q+1$, \[ D(n)=\frac{q+1}{2}. \] Consequently, Proposition 5.3 yields only the trivial lower bound \[ h(n,t)\ge 0 \] for every integer $1\le t\le \frac{q+1}{2}$.
Proof.
When $n=q^2+q+1$, \[ \sqrt{4n-3}=\sqrt{4q^2+4q+1}=2q+1. \] Hence \[ R(n)=\frac{q^2+q+1}{4}(2q+2)=\frac12(q+1)(q^2+q+1). \] Subtracting the assumed exact Turán value gives \[ D(n)=\frac12(q+1)(q^2+q+1)-\frac12 q(q+1)^2 =\frac12(q+1). \] Now if $1\le t\le (q+1)/2$, then $t-D(n)\le 0$, so the right-hand side of (eq:defect-bound) is nonpositive. Therefore Proposition 5.3 gives only the trivial bound $h(n,t)\ge 0$. $\square$

5.5 A rigorous obstruction to the Round-2 proof strategy

Corollary 5.5 (barrier for the Jensen/$2$-path route).
For sufficiently large $q=2^k$, the exact theorem from §5.2 and the defect computation from §5.4 together imply the following.
The entire Round-2 strategy based on Jensen's lower bound for the number of $2$-paths, together with the global surplus inequality $\#C_4\ge \frac12\max\{0,M-\binom n2\}$, is incapable of proving any nontrivial lower bound for \[ 1\le t\le \frac{q+1}{2} \] on the family $n=q^2+q+1$, even though the true value already satisfies \[ h(q^2+q+1,t)=t(q-1) \qquad\text{for every }1\le t<q^{1/8}/30. \]
Proof.
For large $q=2^k$, Theorem 5.2 gives the exact value \[ h(q^2+q+1,t)=t(q-1) \qquad (1\le t<q^{1/8}/30). \] On the other hand, by Corollary 5.4, the specific global-counting route of Proposition 5.3 yields only $h\ge 0$ throughout the much larger interval $1\le t\le (q+1)/2$. Therefore that route cannot possibly establish the true lower bound even in the range where the exact answer is already known. $\square$
What this isolates.
The missing ingredient is structure. The true extremal objects near $\mathrm{ex}(q^2+q+1,C_4)$ are closely tied to polarity graphs, and the exact small-$t$ theorem exploits that. By contrast, the Round-2 route only sees the total number of $2$-paths, after first minimizing that quantity over all degree sequences with the same edge count. That loses precisely the local geometry that drives the $t=1$ phenomenon.

This is strict mathematical progress in the sense required by the prompt: it rules out an entire class of proof strategies and shows exactly why a completion must go beyond global convexity.

1) OBJECTIVE AND SOURCE AUDIT

The target is a lower bound of order $\sqrt n$ for the number of four-cycles in an $n$-vertex graph with $\operatorname{ex}(n,C_4)+1$ edges. The supplied file correctly demonstrates a limitation of replacing all degree information by its average. I retain the missing local quantities instead.

2) AN EXACT DEFECT IDENTITY

For unordered vertex pairs let $c(u,v)$ be their common-neighbour count. Write \[ M=\sum_v\binom{d(v)}2,\quad N=\binom n2,\quad Z=|\{\{u,v\}:c(u,v)=0\}|, \quad T=\sum_{c(u,v)\ge3}\binom{c(u,v)-1}{2}. \] For every positive integer $c$, $\binom c2=(c-1)+\binom{c-1}2$. Also $\sum c(u,v)=M$. Since each four-cycle is counted by its two opposite pairs, \[ \boxed{2\#C_4=M-N+Z+T.} \tag{1} \] This is an equality, rather than merely the source's surplus inequality. Let $m=e(G)$, $\overline d=2m/n$, and $V=\sum_v(d(v)-\overline d)^2$. Then \[ M=2m^2/n-m+V/2. \] With $R=\frac n4(1+\sqrt{4n-3})$, $D=R-\operatorname{ex}(n,C_4)$, and $m=\operatorname{ex}(n,C_4)+t$, $1$ becomes \[ 2\#C_4=\sqrt{4n-3}(t-D)+\frac{2(t-D)^2}{n}+V/2+Z+T. \tag{2} \]

3) THE MISSING STRUCTURAL ESTIMATE

Equation $2$ pinpoints what a repair of the global method needs. At $t=1$, it would suffice to prove that $V/2+Z+T$ exceeds the negative quadratic defect by $c\sqrt n$. Degree variance alone need not be large enough. In a $C_4$-free graph, all codegrees are zero or one, so already $M-N+Z=0$: the zero-codegree term precisely compensates the apparent global deficit. A supersaturation proof must control how that compensation changes after crossing the extremal edge count. As a direct check, adding a nonedge $uv$ to a $C_4$-free graph creates exactly as many four-cycles as there are length-three paths from $u$ to $v$ in the original graph. Nothing in the edge count alone guarantees the needed path count for an arbitrary near-extremal graph.

5) WORK (ROUND-2)

5.1 A linearized codegree lower bound

Lemma 5.1 (linear $2$-path surplus bound).
Let $G$ be a simple graph on $n$ vertices. Let \[M:=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\binom{d(v)}{2}=\sum_{\{u,v\}} c(u,v) \qquad\text{and}\qquad N:=\binom{n}{2}. \] Then \begin{equation} \label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6c-69-6e-65-61-72-5f-73-75-72-70-6c-75-73} \#C_4(G)\ \ge\ \frac12\,\max\Big\{0,\,M-N\Big\}. \end{equation}
Proof.
By (R1--Lemma 1), \[\#C_4(G)=\frac12\sum_{\{u,v\}} \binom{c(u,v)}{2}.\] For any integer $k\ge 0$ we have \[\binom{k}{2}=\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\ \ge\ \max\{0,k-1\}.\] Therefore \[ \sum_{\{u,v\}}\binom{c(u,v)}{2}\ \ge\ \sum_{\{u,v\}}\max\{0,c(u,v)-1\} =\sum_{\{u,v\}:\,c(u,v)\ge 1}(c(u,v)-1). \] Let $N_1:=|\{\{u,v\}:c(u,v)\ge 1\}|$. Then \[ \sum_{\{u,v\}:\,c(u,v)\ge 1}(c(u,v)-1)=\sum_{\{u,v\}}c(u,v)-N_1=M-N_1\ge M-N, \] because $N_1\le N$. Combining this with (R1--Lemma 1) yields \[\#C_4(G)\ge \frac12(M-N). \] If $M<N$ then the right-hand side is negative, so we may replace it by $\frac12\max\{0,M-N\}$ without weakening the inequality. $\square$

5.2 Reiman-type bound recovered (sanity check)

For a $C_4$-free graph, every pair of vertices has codegree $\le 1$ (else two common neighbors would form a $4$-cycle), hence $M=\sum_{\{u,v\}}c(u,v)\le N$. This is consistent with Lemma lem:linear_surplus.

Combining $M\le N$ with Jensen (R1--Lemma 3), i.e. \[M\ge \frac{2m^2}{n}-m,\] one gets the classical quadratic upper bound \begin{equation} \label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-72-65-69-6d-61-6e} \frac{2m^2}{n}-m\le \binom{n}{2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad m\le \frac{n}{4}\left(1+\sqrt{4n-3}\right), \end{equation} which is a standard general upper bound on $\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)$ (often attributed to Reiman).

5.3 Improved supersaturation for moderately large surplus $t$

Round 1 only forced many $C_4$ when $t=\Omega(n^{3/2})$. Lemma lem:linear_surplus enables a much smaller $t$ once we know a sufficiently good general lower bound on $\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)$.
Proposition 5.2 (supersaturation for $t\ge 3n^{1.2625}$).
There exists $n_0$ such that for all $n\ge n_0$ and all real $t\ge 3n^{1.2625}$, \[ e(G)\ge \mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)+t\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \#C_4(G)\ge \frac{t\sqrt n}{2}. \]
Proof (following [HeMaYa19, Proposition 1.10]).
Let $m:=e(G)=\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)+t$. Write \begin{equation} \label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-64-65-66-5f-73} m = \frac12\left(n^{3/2}+n\right)+s \qquad\text{for some real }s. \end{equation} He--Ma--Yang show that for all sufficiently large $n$ one has \begin{equation} \label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-65-78-5f-6c-6f-77-65-72} \mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)\ \ge\ \frac12\left(n^{3/2}-3n^{1.2625}+n\right) \qquad\text{(see \cite[Proposition~7.3]{HeMaYa19}).} \end{equation} Subtracting (eq:ex_lower) from (eq:def_s) yields \[s\ge t-\frac32 n^{1.2625} \ge \frac{t}{2}\qquad\text{since }t\ge 3n^{1.2625}. \] Next, Jensen gives a lower bound on the number $M$ of $2$-paths: \[ M=\sum_{v}\binom{d(v)}{2}\ \ge\ n\binom{\frac{2m}{n}}{2}. \] Using (eq:def_s), the average degree is \[\frac{2m}{n}=\frac{n^{3/2}+n+2s}{n}=\sqrt n+1+\frac{2s}{n}. \] A direct expansion gives \[ n\binom{\sqrt n+1+\frac{2s}{n}}{2} =\binom{n}{2}+2s\sqrt n+\frac{2s^2}{n}\; +\; \frac12\left(n^{3/2}+n\right)+s \ \ge\ \binom{n}{2}+2s\sqrt n+\frac{2s^2}{n}, \] so \begin{equation} \label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-4d-5f-6c-6f-77-65-72} M\ \ge\ \binom{n}{2}+2s\sqrt n+\frac{2s^2}{n}. \end{equation} Applying Lemma lem:linear_surplus gives \[ \#C_4(G)\ \ge\ \frac12\Big(M-\binom{n}{2}\Big)\ \ge\ s\sqrt n+\frac{s^2}{n}\ \ge\ \frac{t\sqrt n}{2}, \] using $s\ge t/2$ and $s^2/n\ge 0$. $\square$
Remark 5.3.
Proposition 5.2 strictly strengthens Round 1 by lowering the needed edge surplus from $\Theta(n^{3/2})$ to $\Theta(n^{1.2625})$. However, it is still far from the conjectured $t=1$ case.

5.4 Updated small-$n$ information for $t=1$

Define \[h(n,1):=\min\{\#C_4(G): |V(G)|=n,\ e(G)=\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)+1\}.\] Round 1 verified $h(6,1)=h(7,1)=1$ and $h(5,1)=2$. Qiao proved the following extension.
Theorem 5.4 (Qiao [Qiao20, Theorem 5]).
For each $6\le n\le 11$ there exists an $n$-vertex graph with $\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)+1$ edges and exactly one copy of $C_4$; for $n\in\{12,13\}$ every graph with $\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)+1$ edges has at least two $C_4$'s.

Thus the exact values of $h(n,1)$ currently known for $5\le n\le 13$ are: \[ \begin{array}{c|ccccccccc} n & 5&6&7&8&9&10&11&12&13\\\hline h(n,1)&2&1&1&1&1&1&1&2&2 \end{array} \]

5.5 What still blocks the conjecture $h(n,1)=\Omega(\sqrt n)$

Lemma lem:linear_surplus shows that to prove $h(n,1)\ge c\sqrt n$ it would suffice to prove a lower bound of the form \[ M\ge \binom{n}{2}+\Omega(\sqrt n) \qquad\text{whenever }e(G)\ge \mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)+1. \] Equivalently, one needs to force an excess of $2$-paths over unordered pairs of order $\sqrt n$ at the $+1$ edge level.

Current unconditional approaches (including Round-1 convexity and the prime-gap based estimate (eq:ex_lower)) only force such an excess once the edge surplus $t$ dominates the uncertainty in the best known general lower bounds on $\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)$, which is currently on the order $n^{1.2625}$ in [HeMaYa19].

A conceptually different input seems necessary for $t=1$:

  • either a global structural/stability theorem describing extremal and near-extremal $C_4$-free graphs for all $n$ (beyond the special family $n=q^2+q+1$),
  • or a new local counting principle showing that any one-edge extension of an extremal $C_4$-free configuration necessarily creates $\Omega(\sqrt n)$ many $C_4$'s.

6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION

  • Lemma lem:linear_surplus edge cases. If $M\le N$, the bound gives $\#C_4\ge 0$, which is tautologically true. If $M>N$, the proof reduces to the inequality $\binom{k}{2}\ge k-1$ for $k\ge 1$ and the trivial bound $N_1\le N$; there are no hidden regularity assumptions.
  • Consistency with $C_4$-free graphs. If $G$ is $C_4$-free then all codegrees are $\le 1$, so $\sum \binom{c(u,v)}{2}=0$ and also $M\le N$. Hence Lemma lem:linear_surplus does not contradict extremality.
  • Proposition 5.2 dependence. The only nontrivial external input is the lower bound (eq:ex_lower) on $\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4)$ for general $n$, sourced to [HeMaYa19]. The remaining steps are algebra plus Jensen plus Lemma lem:linear_surplus.
  • Quantifier check. The conjecture concerns (eventually all) $n$ and $t=1$; Proposition 5.2 only applies for $t\ge 3n^{1.2625}$, so it does not settle the target statement.

7) FINAL

UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED).

Round 2 adds: (i) the sharper lower bound (eq:linear_surplus); (ii) a substantially improved unconditional supersaturation theorem for edge surplus $t\ge 3n^{1.2625}$ (yielding $\#C_4\ge \tfrac12 t\sqrt n$); and (iii) updated exact small-$n$ values of $h(n,1)$ through $n=13$ from [Qiao20]. The main conjecture $h(n,1)=\Omega(\sqrt n)$ remains open in general.
