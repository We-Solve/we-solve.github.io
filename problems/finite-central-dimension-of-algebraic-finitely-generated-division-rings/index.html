A finite central-dimension bound for algebraic quantum-commuting generators

A finite central-dimension bound for algebraic quantum-commuting generators

Claim type: Partial result

Exact scope: If the finitely many algebra generators x_i are algebraic over the center and satisfy x_j x_i=q_ij x_i x_j with nonzero central q_ij, then [D:F]≤product deg_F(x_i); this proves the prompt for this noncommutative class.

Let F=Z(D) and suppose D=F[x_1,…,x_r] as a unital algebra, with x_jx_i=q_{ij}x_ix_j for i<j and q_{ij}∈F*. Zero generators can be deleted. For each remaining i choose its monic minimal polynomial
\[
x_i^{d_i}+\sum_{a<d_i}c_{ia}x_i^a=0,\qquad c_{ia}\in F.
\]
We claim that the at most product_i d_i ordered monomials
\[
x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_r^{a_r},\quad0\le a_i<d_i,
\]
span the whole algebra over F.

First reorder any word by repeatedly swapping an adjacent inverted pair x_jx_i with j>i. Every swap multiplies by a nonzero central scalar and decreases the number of inversions in the index word. The process terminates at a scalar multiple of an ordered monomial. Next reduce any exponent a_i≥d_i by the monic relation, lowering that exponent and introducing central coefficients. This yields a finite linear combination of the displayed monomials. Repetition terminates by decreasing total exponent sum. Every element of the generated algebra is a linear combination of finite words, proving the claim and the finite-dimensional bound.

There is no assertion that these monomials are independent: extra relations can make the actual dimension smaller. If one initially knows only that the division subring generated by the x_i is D, the same conclusion still holds. The spanned algebra B is a finite-dimensional subalgebra of the division ring and has no zero divisors. For b≠0, left multiplication on B is injective, hence surjective by finite dimension; thus b has a right inverse in B, which is its unique inverse in D. Therefore B is already a division subring and equals D. In the literal prompt algebra generation was already given, so this strengthening is optional.

The class is genuinely noncommutative. For a quaternion division algebra (a,b)_F in characteristic not two, generators i,j obey i²=a, j²=b and ji=−ij. Their degrees are at most two, giving central dimension at most four; when the quaternion algebra is division the basis 1,i,j,ij has dimension four and attains the bound. Over the real numbers the usual Hamilton algebra supplies an explicit example.

Algebraicity of every element of D is stronger than what was needed: algebraicity of the finitely many quantum-commuting generators suffices. Without the scalar reordering relations, powers of individual generators do not bound mixed words such as x_1x_2x_1x_2…, so the spanning argument fails. In particular the present proof does not silently replace the general division-ring problem by a commutative field-extension tower. The general case remains outside the advertised conclusion.

Relation to earlier contributions. The earlier record announces an unverified two-generated algebraic infinite-dimensional division ring. This report neither adopts nor refutes that announcement; it gives a finite-dimensional obstruction in the explicitly quantum-commuting subclass, with a quantitative spanning bound.

Finite central dimension of algebraic finitely generated division rings

OpenAI's claim

OpenAI presents a counterexample in the selected manuscript. An algebraic division ring is claimed to be generated as an algebra by two elements while having infinite dimension over its actual center.

Mathematical statement of the claimed result

Result 1.
There is a division ring $D$, countable as a set, with $\operatorname{char}D=0$ and $Z(D)=F$, generated by two elements over its center, such that \[ D=F[x,y]=F(x,y),\qquad[D:F]=\infty,\qquad \forall z\in D\ \exists0\ne f_z\in F[t]:\ f_z(z)=0. \] Algebraicity of each element therefore coexists with infinite central dimension in this claimed finitely generated example. The notation $F[x,y]$ here means the unital algebra generated by $x,y$ over the centre $F$, permitting noncommutative words.
