Statement

A tournament on vertex set $V$ is an orientation of the complete graph: for every unordered pair $\{u,v\}$ exactly one of $u\to v$ or $v\to u$ holds.

For a vertex $x$ and a set $S\subseteq V\setminus\{x\}$, say that $x$ dominates $S$ if $x\to s$ for all $s\in S$.

For $n\ge 1$, let $f(n)$ be the minimum integer $N$ such that there exists a tournament on $N$ vertices with the property:

\[ \forall S\subseteq V\ \text{with }|S|=n,\ \exists x\in V\setminus S\ \text{such that }x\text{ dominates }S. \]

Estimate $f(n)$.

Original attribution: gpt_pro_5.2. Original file: attacks/erdos/gpt_pro_5.2/902.tex.

1) FORMAL RESTATEMENT

A tournament on vertex set $V$ is an orientation of the complete graph: for every unordered pair $\{u,v\}$ exactly one of $u\to v$ or $v\to u$ holds.

For a vertex $x$ and a set $S\subseteq V\setminus\{x\}$, say that $x$ dominates $S$ if $x\to s$ for all $s\in S$.

For $n\ge 1$, let $f(n)$ be the minimum integer $N$ such that there exists a tournament on $N$ vertices with the property:

\[ \forall S\subseteq V\ \text{with }|S|=n,\ \exists x\in V\setminus S\ \text{such that }x\text{ dominates }S. \]

Estimate $f(n)$.

2) QUICK LITERATURE/CONTEXT CHECK

The problem statement records the bounds \[2^{n+1}-1\le f(n)\ll n^2 2^n,\qquad\text{and }\qquad n2^n\ll f(n), \] and exact values $f(1)=3$, $f(2)=7$, $f(3)=19$.

3) ATTACK PLAN

  • Prove the lower bound $f(n)\ge 2^{n+1}-1$ by an induction on $n$ using inneighborhoods.
  • Prove an explicit probabilistic upper bound of the form $f(n)\le C n^2 2^n$ by union bounding over all $n$-sets in a random tournament.
  • Brute-force sanity check the exact value $f(2)=7$ by enumerating all tournaments up to 6 vertices, and verify an explicit 7-vertex example.

4) WORK

PHASE 1 — FAST REALITY CHECK (computation for $n=2$).

I brute-forced all tournaments on $N\le 6$ vertices (there are $2^{\binom{N}{2}}$) and checked the $n=2$ domination property. Result: no tournament on $N\le 6$ vertices has the property for $n=2$. I then checked the Paley tournament on 7 vertices (vertices $0,\dots,6$ with $i\to j$ iff $j-i\pmod 7$ is a quadratic residue); it does satisfy the property for $n=2$. This matches the stated value $f(2)=7$.

Problem-specific lemmas (recursive lower bound and probabilistic upper bound).

Lemma 902.1 (Indegree recursion and $2^{n+1}-1$ lower bound). Let $T$ be a tournament on $N$ vertices with the property that every $n$-set is dominated by some outside vertex. Then there exists a vertex $v$ whose inneighborhood $I(v):=\{u\in V: u\to v\}$ induces a tournament with the analogous property for $(n-1)$-sets. Consequently \[ f(n)\ \ge\ 2 f(n-1)+1, \] and since $f(1)=3$ this implies \[ f(n)\ge 2^{n+1}-1. \]

Proof. First, in any tournament on $N$ vertices, the average indegree is $(N-1)/2$. Hence there exists a vertex $v$ with indegree $|I(v)|\ge (N-1)/2$.

We claim the subtournament induced by $I(v)$ has the domination property for $(n-1)$-sets. Let $S\subseteq I(v)$ with $|S|=n-1$. Consider the $n$-set $S\cup\{v\}$ in $T$. By hypothesis, there exists a vertex $x\notin S\cup\{v\}$ that dominates $S\cup\{v\}$. In particular, $x\to v$, so $x\in I(v)$. Also, $x$ dominates all vertices in $S$. Thus within the induced tournament on $I(v)$, the vertex $x$ dominates $S$. This holds for every $(n-1)$-set $S\subseteq I(v)$.

Therefore $|I(v)|\ge f(n-1)$ whenever $N=f(n)$, implying \[(N-1)/2 \ge f(n-1)\quad\Rightarrow\quad N\ge 2f(n-1)+1. \] With base case $f(1)=3$ (witnessed by the directed 3-cycle), the recurrence solves to $f(n)\ge 2^{n+1}-1$ by induction. $\square$

Lemma 902.2 (Random-tournament upper bound). There is an absolute constant $C$ such that for every $n\ge 1$, \[ f(n)\le C\, n^2 2^n. \]

Proof. Let $N$ be a parameter and take a uniformly random tournament on $[N]$ by orienting each edge independently with probability $1/2$. Fix an $n$-set $S\subseteq[N]$. For a vertex $v\in[N]\setminus S$, the events $\{v\to s\}_{s\in S}$ are independent and each has probability $1/2$, so \[\mathbb{P}(v\text{ dominates }S)=2^{-n}. \] Thus \[\mathbb{P}(v\text{ does not dominate }S)=1-2^{-n}. \] Independence over distinct $v\notin S$ gives \[ \mathbb{P}(S\text{ is undominated}) = (1-2^{-n})^{N-n} \le \exp\left(-\frac{N-n}{2^n}\right). \] There are $\binom{N}{n}$ choices of $S$, so by the union bound the probability that some $n$-set is undominated is at most \[ \binom{N}{n}\exp\left(-\frac{N-n}{2^n}\right). \] Using $\binom{N}{n}\le (eN/n)^n$, this is at most \[ \left(\frac{eN}{n}\right)^n \exp\left(-\frac{N-n}{2^n}\right). \] Choose \[ N := 10\,n^2 2^n. \] Then $N/2^n=10n^2$ and so \[ \exp\left(-\frac{N-n}{2^n}\right) \le \exp(-9n^2). \] Meanwhile \[ \log\left( (eN/n)^n\right) = n\log(eN/n) \le n\log(10e\,n2^n) \le n(\log(10e)+\log n + n\log 2)\le 2n^2 \] for all $n\ge 2$ (crude bound). Hence the union bound probability is at most $\exp(2n^2)\exp(-9n^2)=\exp(-7n^2)<1$. Therefore with positive probability the random tournament has the desired property, showing $f(n)\le N$ for $n\ge 2$. The $n=1$ case holds with $f(1)=3$. Absorbing constants gives $f(n)\le Cn^2 2^n$. $\square$

5) VERIFICATION

  • Lemma 902.1: key point is that the vertex $x$ dominating $S\cup\{v\}$ must satisfy $x\to v$, hence lies in $I(v)$; no hidden assumption.
  • Lemma 902.2: independence is valid in the random tournament model. Union bound and $\binom{N}{n}\le (eN/n)^n$ are standard. The final numeric inequality $n\log(10e\,n2^n)\le 2n^2$ holds for all $n\ge 2$; for small $n$ one can check separately.
  • Computation: brute force for $N\le 6$ and explicit Paley$7$ check confirm $f(2)=7$.

6) FINAL

UNRESOLVED

(i) Strongest proved partial result. A clean recursive argument gives $f(n)\ge 2^{n+1}-1$ (Lemma 902.1). A random-tournament union bound gives $f(n)\le C n^2 2^n$ (Lemma 902.2). Computationally, $f(2)=7$ is confirmed by exhaustive search up to $N=6$ and a 7-vertex construction.

(ii) First gap (crisp). Determine the true order of magnitude of $f(n)$: is it $\Theta(n2^n)$ or $\Theta(n^2 2^n)$ (or something else)?

(iii) Top 3 next moves.

  1. Improve the probabilistic upper bound by sharper counting and/or dependent random choice to reduce the $n^2$ factor.
  2. Construct explicit tournaments (instead of random) matching the best known upper bounds and analyze their domination coverage.
  3. Computational: for $n=3$, implement heuristic search for tournaments on $N<19$ to confirm nonexistence and explore near-extremal structures.

(iv) Minimal counterexample structure (if conjectured $\Theta(n2^n)$ is false). If $f(n)$ were significantly larger than $n2^n$, near-extremal tournaments would likely exhibit a “covering design” obstruction: many $n$-sets would each require specialized dominators, forcing large overlap constraints on outneighborhoods. Such a structure might resemble a sparse family of outneighborhoods failing to cover $\binom{V}{n}$ efficiently.
