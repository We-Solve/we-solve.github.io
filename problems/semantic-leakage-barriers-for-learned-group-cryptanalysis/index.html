Verbatim supplied catalogue question

Machine learning-based cryptanalysis of group-theoretic protocols

There have been some results in the application of machine learning to the cryptanalysis of lattice-based schemes; whether these methods translate to the group-theoretic realm remains open.

Attempt and results

No protocol, data distribution, or attack goal is fixed in the source. We test whether high prediction accuracy on encoded group words could itself establish a group-theoretic attack. The counterexample and information bound below show what that inference lacks; they do not rule out useful learning attacks on specified protocols.
Perfect syntactic prediction with zero semantic signal.
Let $G$ be any group represented by words with a formal letter $a$ and its inverse. Let a fair bit $B$ determine the word $W$: use the empty word if $B=0$ and the two-letter word $aa^{-1}$ if $B=1$. Both evaluate to the identity in $G$. The rule that predicts one exactly for length two recovers $B$ with probability one. However, any classifier whose input is only the represented group element always receives the identity; even with independent randomness its success probability is exactly $1/2$. Thus observed word-label prediction does not imply extraction of any semantic group secret. This is a constructed diagnostic distribution, not an assertion that an existing experiment uses it. Adding arbitrary independent identity padding at training time can change the syntactic distribution while preserving the semantic problem, so a claimed transfer needs an explicit representation and train/test generation rule.
The optimal information-only binary benchmark.
For a fair bit $B$ and a countable observation $Z$ with distributions $P_0,P_1$, let $a(z)\in[0,1]$ be the probability that a possibly randomized classifier outputs zero. Its success is \[ \tfrac12\sum_z\left(P_0(z)a(z)+P_1(z)(1-a(z))\right) =\tfrac12+\tfrac12\sum_z a(z)(P_0(z)-P_1(z)). \] The maximum is attained by $a(z)=1$ wherever $P_0(z)>P_1(z)$, yielding $\tfrac12(1+\operatorname{TV}(P_0,P_1))$, where $\operatorname{TV}=\tfrac12\sum_z|P_0(z)-P_1(z)|$. For a deterministic semantic parser $\pi$, grouping terms by fibers and using $|\sum u_i|\le\sum|u_i|$ proves $\operatorname{TV}(\pi_*P_0,\pi_*P_1)\le\operatorname{TV}(P_0,P_1)$. In the preceding example these quantities are zero and one respectively. This bound applies to any predictor, learned or otherwise, and says nothing about the computational cost of attaining it.
Multiple conjugacy reveals a coset, not the planted secret.
Let $H$ be a finite subgroup of a group $G$, let $b_1,\ldots,b_t$ be fixed, and publish $\Phi(s)=(s^{-1}b_i s)_{i=1}^t$ for uniform $s\in H$. For $s,u\in H$, equality $\Phi(s)=\Phi(u)$ is equivalent to $us^{-1}$ commuting with every $b_i$: multiply the conjugacy equalities on the left by $u$ and on the right by $s^{-1}$. Thus every fiber is the left coset $Cs$, where $C=H\cap\bigcap_i C_G(b_i)$, and has size $|C|$. Conditioned on the transcript, $s$ is uniform on that fiber. An estimator using only this transcript and independent randomness recovers the exact planted $s$ with probability at most $1/|C|$. If the cryptanalytic task instead accepts any conjugator with the same transcript, this bound does not restrict success: every element of $Cs$ is a valid solution. Additional public leakage or correlated side information would also change the conditioning and must be modeled explicitly.
