Statement

We seek a polynomial $f(x)\in\mathbb Z[x]$ such that for all pairs of nonnegative integers $a<b$ and $c<d$, \[ \left(\{a,b\}\neq\{c,d\}\right)\ \Longrightarrow\ f(a)+f(b)\neq f(c)+f(d). \] Equivalently, the map \[ \{a,b\}\mapsto f(a)+f(b) \] from 2-element subsets of $\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}$ to $\mathbb Z$ is injective.

Question: does such an integer-coefficient polynomial exist?

Edge cases. If one allowed $a=b$ then $f(a)+f(a)$ would collide trivially under permutations; the problem explicitly requires $a<b$.

Restatement from Math-763-A2 r1, with the source manuscript’s qualifications.

\title{Erdős Problem 324: a checked attempt} \author{GPT_6_Astra_Ultra} \date{25 September 2026} \maketitle

Statement and scope

Does an integer polynomial $f$ exist such that $f(a)+f(b)$ are distinct for all distinct unordered pairs of nonnegative indices $a<b$? Source: https://www.erdosproblems.com/324. We prove that no polynomial of degree at most two works, even after deleting any finite initial range of indices, and verify failures of the cubic and quartic monomials. Existence in higher degree remains unresolved here.

The condition is stated on two-element index sets. It does not include the sums with $a=b$; changing that convention would give a stronger requirement. The older GPT-5.2 draft's claim about repeated-index sums colliding trivially under permutations was not a valid reason to exclude them. We use the precise $a<b$ convention throughout. No novelty is claimed for these obstructions.

All linear and quadratic polynomials fail

For a linear polynomial $f(t)=At+B$, the identity \[ f(x)+f(x+3)=f(x+1)+f(x+2) \] holds for every nonnegative integer $x$. It includes constant polynomials and supplies distinct pairs arbitrarily far out.

Now let $f(t)=At^2+Bt+D$ with $A\ne0$. Put \[ h=1+2|A|,\qquad y=hx+\frac{h^2-1}{2}+\frac{B(h-1)}{2A}. \tag{1} \] Here $h$ is odd and $(h-1)/(2A)=|A|/A$ is an integer, so $y$ is an integer for every integer $x$. Since $h>1$, for all sufficiently large positive $x$ we have $y>x+h$. Direct subtraction gives \[ f(x+h)-f(x)=2Ahx+Ah^2+Bh, \qquad f(y+1)-f(y)=2Ay+A+B. \] They are equal by $1$. Rearranging yields \[ f(x)+f(y+1)=f(x+h)+f(y). \tag{2} \] All four indices are nonnegative and strictly ordered $x<x+h<y<y+1$. Thus $2$ is a genuine collision between different permitted pairs. Varying $x$ proves infinitely many collisions above every fixed threshold, for every nonzero leading coefficient and every linear or constant term. This extends the older draft's obstruction for $t^2$ alone to all quadratics.

Two higher-degree monomials also fail

The identities \[ 1^3+12^3=9^3+10^3=1729, \] \[ 59^4+158^4=133^4+134^4=635318657 \] have distinct positive entries and distinct unordered pairs. Multiplying all four entries by any positive integer gives infinitely many collisions for the corresponding monomial, so finite initial deletions cannot repair either example. These identities also rule out $At^d+D$ with $A\ne0$ for $d=3,4$, since multiplication by $A$ and addition of $2D$ preserve the equalities. They do not rule out all mixed-term quartics.

Why finitely many modular obstructions do not decide the question

For an integer polynomial and a modulus $q$, the value $f(n)\pmod q$ depends only on $n\pmod q$. Thus any fixed finite set of modulus tests supplies only finitely many residue patterns for an infinite set of index pairs; many different pairs necessarily pass the same tests. Congruence equality is a necessary condition for an integer equality, but passing those tests does not produce a collision, while a bounded search finding none does not prove injectivity on all indices.

More concretely, if $f(a)=f(c)$ for different indices, choosing a third index $b$ gives an immediate permitted pair collision. So a candidate must itself be injective on nonnegative integers. Eventual monotonicity of any nonconstant real polynomial provides only eventual injectivity of its values; $2$ shows why that fact does not control equality of two-term sums.

Remaining gap

The complete low-degree obstruction and the displayed monomial identities are proved. The known theorem excluding all cubics is recorded on the source page but is not reconstructed here. We neither construct a qualifying higher-degree integer polynomial nor rule out the suggested candidate $t^5$. The finite no-collision searches in the old draft are not used to support either conclusion.

Edge cases. If one allowed $a=b$ then $f(a)+f(a)$ would collide trivially under permutations; the problem explicitly requires $a<b$.

2) QUICK LITERATURE/CONTEXT CHECK

I do not use external literature. The statement itself suggests:

* "Probably $f(x)=x^5$ should work." * The Lander--Parkin--Selfridge conjecture would imply $f(x)=x^n$ works for all $n\ge 5$.

3) ATTACK PLAN

I cannot resolve existence. I will instead:

$1$ Prove that degree-1 polynomials cannot work.

$2$ Give explicit counterexamples showing that the monomials $x^2$, $x^3$, and $x^4$ do not work.

$3$ Do a computation-based sanity check: search for collisions for $x^d$ for $d=5,6$ up to a moderate range.

4) WORK

PHASE 1: FAST REALITY CHECK (collision search for $f(x)=x^d$)

For $f(x)=x^d$ and bounds $0\le a<b\le M$, I searched for distinct pairs with equal sums $a^d+b^d=c^d+d^d$. The first collisions found were:

* $d=2$, already within $M=20$: $0^2+5^2=3^2+4^2=25$. * $d=3$, already within $M=20$: $1^3+12^3=9^3+10^3=1729$. * $d=4$, no collision up to $M=100$, but a collision appears by $M=200$: $59^4+158^4=133^4+134^4=635318657$.

For $d=5$ and $d=6$, no collision was found for $M\le 200$. (This does not prove injectivity for any $d\ge 5$; it is only a sanity check.)

Lemma 324.1 (no linear polynomial works).

No polynomial of degree $\le 1$ has the required property.

Proof. Let $f(x)=ux+v$ with $u,v\in\mathbb Z$. If $u=0$, then $f$ is constant and $f(a)+f(b)$ is constant, so injectivity fails. Assume $u\neq 0$. Then \[ f(a)+f(b)=u(a+b)+2v. \] Thus $f(a)+f(b)$ depends only on $a+b$. Choose distinct pairs with the same sum, e.g. \[ (a,b)=(0,3),\qquad (c,d)=(1,2), \] which satisfy $0<3$, $1<2$, and $a+b=c+d=3$. Then $f(0)+f(3)=f(1)+f(2)$, so the required distinctness fails. $\square$

Lemma 324.2 ($f(x)=x^2$ fails).

For $f(x)=x^2$ there exist distinct pairs $a<b$ and $c<d$ with $f(a)+f(b)=f(c)+f(d)$.

Proof. Take $(a,b)=(0,5)$ and $(c,d)=(3,4)$. Then \[ 0^2+5^2=25=9+16=3^2+4^2. \] The pairs are distinct and satisfy $0<5$, $3<4$. So $x^2$ fails. $\square$

Lemma 324.3 ($f(x)=x^3$ fails).

For $f(x)=x^3$ there exist distinct pairs $a<b$ and $c<d$ with $f(a)+f(b)=f(c)+f(d)$.

Proof. Take $(a,b)=(1,12)$ and $(c,d)=(9,10)$. Compute \[ 1^3+12^3 = 1+1728 = 1729, \] and \[ 9^3+10^3 = 729+1000 = 1729. \] The pairs are distinct and satisfy $1<12$, $9<10$. So $x^3$ fails. $\square$

Lemma 324.4 ($f(x)=x^4$ fails).

For $f(x)=x^4$ there exist distinct pairs $a<b$ and $c<d$ with $f(a)+f(b)=f(c)+f(d)$.

Proof. Take $(a,b)=(59,158)$ and $(c,d)=(133,134)$. Direct computation gives \[ 59^4+158^4 = 635318657 = 133^4+134^4. \] The pairs are distinct and satisfy $59<158$, $133<134$. So $x^4$ fails. $\square$

5) VERIFICATION

-- Lemma 324.1: the collision uses only additivity of a linear function.

-- Lemmas 324.2--324.4: each is verified by explicit arithmetic equality; order constraints $a<b$ are satisfied.

-- Computation: the collision search was an exhaustive check over pairs $0\le a<b\le M$ for each $M$ reported.

6) FINAL

UNRESOLVED

(i) Strongest fully proved partial result obtained here.

* No linear polynomial works (Lemma 324.1). * The monomials $x^2$, $x^3$, and $x^4$ fail by explicit equal-sum identities (Lemmas 324.2--324.4). * Computation finds no collisions for $x^5$ or $x^6$ on the range $0\le a<b\le 200$.

(ii) Exact first gap.

Construct (or rule out) an integer-coefficient polynomial $f$ such that $f(a)+f(b)$ is injective on pairs $a<b$. Even proving this for the specific candidate $f(x)=x^5$ is beyond what I achieved.

(iii) Top 3 next moves (concrete targets).

1. Attempt a proof for $f(x)=x^5$ by showing the Diophantine equation $a^5+b^5=c^5+d^5$ has no nontrivial solutions in nonnegative integers with $a<b$, $c<d$. 2. Search computationally for the smallest collision for $x^5$ (if any) by extending the brute-force range significantly and/or using modular sieving. 3. Explore mixed-degree polynomials such as $f(x)=x^5+Cx^2$ to break any potential modular obstructions.

(iv) What a minimal counterexample would likely look like.

If no such polynomial exists, then for every $f\in\mathbb Z[x]$ there must be distinct pairs $a<b$, $c<d$ with $f(a)+f(b)=f(c)+f(d)$. A minimal counterexample to existence would likely arise from a structured parametric family of solutions to the Diophantine equation \[f(a)+f(b)=f(c)+f(d),\] analogous to known parametric families for equal sums of like powers.
