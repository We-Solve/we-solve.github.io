Statement

Let $1=s_1<s_2<s_3<\cdots$ be the increasing sequence of squarefree positive integers. The question is whether \[ s_{n+1}-s_n = o\left(s_n^{1/5}\right)\qquad (n\to\infty). \] Equivalently: for every $\varepsilon>0$ does there exist $N(\varepsilon)$ such that $s_{n+1}-s_n \le \varepsilon\, s_n^{1/5}$ for all $n\ge N(\varepsilon)$?

Erdős Problem #208

1) ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE

Path pursued: (A) full proof of the Round 1 target statement.

Round 1 treated the statement \[ s_{n+1}-s_n=o(s_n^{1/5}), \] where $s_1<s_2<\cdots$ is the increasing sequence of squarefree integers, as unresolved and only obtained the much weaker bound $s_{n+1}-s_n\ll \sqrt{s_n}$.

The decisive new fact is that the Round 1 target statement is now a theorem: Pandey proved a fixed exponent improvement beyond $1/5$. Therefore the correct Round 2 objective is to convert that theorem into a short, gap-free proof of the original statement.

2) Round-1 FOUNDATION USED

I use only the following Round 1 items.

  • The definition of the sequence $s_1<s_2<\cdots$ of squarefree integers.
  • The precise target statement from Round 1: \[ s_{n+1}-s_n=o(s_n^{1/5}). \]

I do not reuse the Round 1 proof of the weaker bound $s_{n+1}-s_n\ll \sqrt{s_n}$.

3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)

The new tool is Pandey's 2024 theorem:

There exists $\eta>0$ such that, for all sufficiently large $X$, the interval \[ [X,\ X+X^{1/5-\eta}] \] contains a squarefree integer.

This is strictly stronger than the original Round 1 target. Indeed, it immediately implies \[ s_{n+1}-s_n\ll s_n^{1/5-\eta}, \] and therefore \[ s_{n+1}-s_n=o(s_n^{1/5}). \]

4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)

The Round 1 gap was simple: no theorem beyond the exponent $1/5$ was available inside the write-up.

To close that gap, I will:

  1. state Pandey's theorem precisely;
  2. substitute $X=s_n+1$ (not $X=s_n$);
  3. deduce a direct upper bound for $s_{n+1}-s_n$;
  4. divide by $s_n^{1/5}$ and let $n\to \infty$.

The choice $X=s_n+1$ is important: if one took $X=s_n$, the guaranteed squarefree integer in $[X,X+X^{1/5-\eta}]$ could be the left endpoint $s_n$ itself, which would not control the next gap. Shifting by $1$ avoids that issue completely.

5) WORK (ROUND-2)

External theorem used (Pandey).
There exists a constant $\eta>0$ such that for all sufficiently large real numbers $X$, the interval \[ [X,\ X+X^{1/5-\eta}] \] contains at least one squarefree integer.
Theorem 5.1.
For the increasing sequence $s_1<s_2<\cdots$ of squarefree integers, one has \[ s_{n+1}-s_n=o(s_n^{1/5}). \]
Proof.
Let $\eta>0$ be as in Pandey's theorem. Fix $n$ large enough that $X:=s_n+1$ lies in the range where Pandey's theorem applies. Then the interval \[ [s_n+1,\ s_n+1+(s_n+1)^{1/5-\eta}] \] contains a squarefree integer.

Let $t$ be such a squarefree integer. Since $t\ge s_n+1$, we have $t>s_n$. By definition, $s_{n+1}$ is the least squarefree integer strictly larger than $s_n$, so \[ s_{n+1}\le t\le s_n+1+(s_n+1)^{1/5-\eta}. \] Therefore \[ s_{n+1}-s_n\le 1+(s_n+1)^{1/5-\eta}. \] Now divide by $s_n^{1/5}$: \[ \frac{s_{n+1}-s_n}{s_n^{1/5}} \le \frac{1}{s_n^{1/5}} + \frac{(s_n+1)^{1/5-\eta}}{s_n^{1/5}}. \] As $n\to\infty$, we have $s_n\to\infty$, so \[ \frac{1}{s_n^{1/5}}\to 0, \] and also \[ \frac{(s_n+1)^{1/5-\eta}}{s_n^{1/5}} = \left(1+\frac{1}{s_n}\right)^{1/5-\eta}s_n^{-\eta}\to 0. \] Hence \[ \frac{s_{n+1}-s_n}{s_n^{1/5}}\to 0, \] which is exactly \[ s_{n+1}-s_n=o(s_n^{1/5}). \] $\square$

What this does and does not settle.
The Round 1 target statement is therefore solved affirmatively. However, the current stronger formulation recorded on the Erdős problem page asks whether \[ s_{n+1}-s_n\ll_\varepsilon s_n^\varepsilon \qquad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0, \] which is much stronger. Pandey's theorem gives a single fixed exponent $1/5-\eta$, not arbitrary $\varepsilon$. So the original Round 1 target is solved, while the stronger modern formulation remains open.

6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION

(i) Why was $X=s_n+1$ chosen instead of $X=s_n$?
Because if $X=s_n$, Pandey's theorem only guarantees a squarefree number somewhere in \[ [s_n,\ s_n+s_n^{1/5-\eta}], \] and that number could be $s_n$ itself. Then no bound on $s_{n+1}-s_n$ would follow. The shift by $1$ removes this possibility.
(ii) Does Pandey's theorem apply to integer $X$ only?
The statement is formulated for sufficiently large $X$. Taking $X=s_n+1$ is legitimate since $s_n+1\to\infty$.
(iii) Is the deduction from the existence of $t$ to the bound on $s_{n+1}$ valid?
Yes. By definition, $s_{n+1}$ is the smallest squarefree integer greater than $s_n$, and $t$ is one such squarefree integer. Therefore $s_{n+1}\le t$.
(iv) Do the limits really give little-$o$?
Yes. Both terms on the right-hand side after division by $s_n^{1/5}$ tend to $0$, so the whole quotient tends to $0$.
(v) Any hidden issue with endpoint conventions?
No. Since $t\ge s_n+1$, we have $t>s_n$ regardless of whether the interval is interpreted with closed or half-open endpoints at the left.

The proof is therefore gap-free.

7) FINAL (EXACTLY ONE)

FULL SOLUTION --- FULL PROOF.

Pandey's theorem implies \[ s_{n+1}-s_n\le 1+(s_n+1)^{1/5-\eta} \] for some fixed $\eta>0$ and all sufficiently large $n$, and hence \[ s_{n+1}-s_n=o(s_n^{1/5}). \]

COMPLETION: 100%

Current statement and scope

For successive squarefree integers $s_n<s_{n+1}$, is $s_{n+1}-s_n\ll_\epsilon s_n^\epsilon$ for every $\epsilon>0$? More sharply, is the gap at most \[ (1+o(1))\frac{\pi^2}{6}\frac{\log s_n}{\log\log s_n}? \] Source: https://www.erdosproblems.com/208. Both assertions remain unresolved here. The older GPT-5.2 and GPT-Pro-5.4 drafts instead targeted $o(s_n^{1/5})$. That weaker assertion follows from Pandey's theorem and does not settle the present questions.

We retain the elementary counting method, repair the CRT size argument, and prove a logarithmic lower bound with constant $1/2$. This is weaker than the known constant $\pi^2/6$ recorded on the problem page and carries no novelty claim.

An elementary uniform upper bound

The identity $\mu(m)^2=\sum_{d^2\mid m}\mu(d)$ gives \[ Q(x):=\sum_{m\le x}\mu(m)^2 =\sum_{d\le\sqrt x}\mu(d)\left\lfloor\frac{x}{d^2}\right\rfloor =\frac{x}{\zeta(2)}+O(\sqrt x). \] Indeed the floor errors total $O(\sqrt x)$, and the omitted absolutely convergent tail contributes at most $x\sum_{d>\sqrt x}d^{-2}=O(\sqrt x)$. The Euler product gives $\zeta(2)^{-1}=6/\pi^2$. For a sufficiently large fixed $C$, subtraction at $x$ and $x+C\sqrt x$ now gives $Q(x+C\sqrt x)-Q(x)>0$ for all large $x$. Hence \[ s_{n+1}-s_n=O(\sqrt{s_n}). \tag{1} \] The same subtraction shows that every sufficiently long interval immediately to the left of $x$, of length $C\sqrt x$, also contains a squarefree number.

Long gaps with controlled location

Let $p_1,\ldots,p_h$ be the first $h$ primes and put $P_h=\prod_{j=1}^h p_j^2$. The Chinese remainder theorem supplies $r\in[0,P_h)$ satisfying $r\equiv-j\pmod{p_j^2}$. Set $x=P_h+r$, rather than making an unsupported lower bound on the least residue. Then \[ P_h\le x<2P_h,\qquad p_j^2\mid x+j\quad(1\le j\le h). \] Thus $x+1,\ldots,x+h$ contain no squarefree integer.

Use the prime number theorem in the form $p_h\sim h\log h$ and $\sum_{p\le y}\log p\sim y$, an explicit external input. It gives \[ \log x=(2+o(1))h\log h,\qquad h=\left(\frac12+o(1)\right)\frac{\log x}{\log\log x}. \] Let $s$ be the last squarefree integer at most $x$, and $t$ its successor. Then $t-s\ge h+1$. The left-interval consequence of $1$ gives $s=x-O(\sqrt x)$, so $s\to\infty$ and $\log s\sim\log x$. Consequently infinitely many consecutive squarefree pairs satisfy \[ t-s\ge\left(\frac12+o(1)\right)\frac{\log s}{\log\log s}. \tag{2} \] Distinct pairs occur because their left endpoints tend to infinity. Only an upper bound on a CRT representative would not justify the old draft's assertion $\log x\asymp h\log h$; choosing the representative in $[P_h,2P_h)$ supplies both bounds.

The older target and the remaining gap

Pandey's primary theorem, https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13981, states that for some $\eta>0$ every sufficiently large $[X,X+X^{1/5-\eta}]$ contains a squarefree number. With $X=s_n+1$, this implies \[ s_{n+1}-s_n\le1+(s_n+1)^{1/5-\eta}=o(s_n^{1/5}). \] This implication is complete relative to the named external theorem; its proof is not reconstructed. A single fixed exponent improvement does not give bounds for every positive exponent, much less the proposed logarithmic upper bound. Those two current targets are the remaining gaps.

Erdős Problem #208

1. FORMAL RESTATEMENT

Let $1=s_1<s_2<s_3<\cdots$ be the increasing sequence of squarefree positive integers. The question is whether \[ s_{n+1}-s_n = o\left(s_n^{1/5}\right)\qquad (n\to\infty). \] Equivalently: for every $\varepsilon>0$ does there exist $N(\varepsilon)$ such that $s_{n+1}-s_n \le \varepsilon\, s_n^{1/5}$ for all $n\ge N(\varepsilon)$?

2. PHASE 1 --- FAST REALITY CHECK

  • Computed small-scale gap data (squarefree sieve).
    1. Up to $10^6$, the maximum observed gap between consecutive squarefree numbers is $8$ (e.g. from $217069$ to $217077$).
    2. Up to $10^7$, the maximum observed gap is $10$ (e.g. from $8870023$ to $8870033$).
    These are far smaller than $x^{1/5}$ at these scales (for $x=10^7$, $x^{1/5}\approx 25.1$).

3. KEY DEFINITIONS / LEMMAS

  1. Squarefree indicator. Let $\mu$ be the Möbius function. Then \[ \mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{sqfree}}(n)=\mu(n)^2. \] Also, the identity \[ \mu(n)^2 = \sum_{d^2\mid n} \mu(d) \] holds for all $n\ge 1$.

  2. Counting squarefree numbers. Let \[ Q(x):=\#\{n\le x: n\text{ squarefree}\}=\sum_{n\le x}\mu(n)^2. \] Then \[ Q(x)=\frac{6}{\pi^2}x + O(\sqrt{x}). \]

  3. Immediate (weak) consequence for gaps. There exists an absolute constant $C>0$ such that every interval $[x, x+C\sqrt{x}]$ with $x$ sufficiently large contains a squarefree integer. Equivalently, $s_{n+1}-s_n \ll \sqrt{s_n}$.

  4. A simple infinite-family lower bound (gaps exist). There exist infinitely many gaps of length at least $c\,\dfrac{\log x}{\log\log x}$ between consecutive squarefree numbers, for some absolute $c>0$. (A concrete proof is given below using CRT with prime squares.)

4. WORK (Main proof or counterexample)

Lemma 3.2 (squarefree counting with $O(\sqrt{x})$ error).
Claim. $Q(x)=\dfrac{6}{\pi^2}x + O(\sqrt{x})$.

Proof. Using $\mu(n)^2 = \sum_{d^2\mid n}\mu(d)$, \[ Q(x)=\sum_{n\le x}\mu(n)^2 =\sum_{n\le x}\ \sum_{d^2\mid n}\mu(d) =\sum_{d\le \sqrt{x}}\mu(d)\,\#\{n\le x: d^2\mid n\} =\sum_{d\le \sqrt{x}}\mu(d)\,\lfloor\frac{x}{d^2}\rfloor. \] Write $\lfloor x/d^2\rfloor = x/d^2 + O(1)$, giving \[ Q(x)= x\sum_{d\le \sqrt{x}}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^2} + O(\sqrt{x}). \] As $x\to\infty$, the partial sums converge to \[ \sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^2} = \frac{1}{\zeta(2)}=\frac{6}{\pi^2}, \] and truncation at $\sqrt{x}$ introduces an error $\ll \sum_{d>\sqrt{x}}1/d^2\ll 1/\sqrt{x}$, which after multiplying by $x$ is $O(\sqrt{x})$. Hence $Q(x)=\frac{6}{\pi^2}x+O(\sqrt{x})$. $\square$

Corollary 3.3 (gap bound $\ll\sqrt{x}$).
Claim. There is a constant $C>0$ such that for all sufficiently large $x$, \[ Q(x+C\sqrt{x})-Q(x) \ge 1. \] Consequently, $s_{n+1}-s_n \ll \sqrt{s_n}$.

Proof. By Lemma 3.2, \[ Q(x+C\sqrt{x})-Q(x) =\frac{6}{\pi^2}C\sqrt{x} + O(\sqrt{x}). \] Choosing $C$ larger than the implied constant in the $O(\sqrt{x})$ term makes the right-hand side positive for all large $x$, hence at least $1$ for all large $x$. Therefore every such interval contains a squarefree integer. Taking $x=s_n$ gives $s_{n+1}-s_n\le C\sqrt{s_n}$ for all large $n$. $\square$

Lemma 3.4 (explicit construction of infinitely many large squarefree gaps).
Claim. There exists $c>0$ such that for infinitely many $X$ there is an interval of length at least $c\,\dfrac{\log X}{\log\log X}$ containing no squarefree numbers.

Proof. Let $y$ be large and let $\mathcal{P}(y)$ denote the primes $p\le y$. For each prime $p\le y$ choose a distinct integer $i(p)$ in $\{1,2,\dots,\pi(y)\}$ (for example enumerate the primes in increasing order and set $i(p)$ to be its index). Impose the congruences \[ n\equiv -i(p) \pmod{p^2}\qquad (p\le y). \] The moduli $p^2$ are pairwise coprime, so by CRT there exists $n$ satisfying all of them. Then for each $p\le y$ we have $p^2\mid (n+i(p))$, hence each of the integers \[ n+1,\ n+2,\ \dots,\ n+\pi(y) \] contains at least one square prime factor and therefore is not squarefree. Thus there is a squarefree gap of length at least $\pi(y)$. Now set $X:=n$. The CRT solution satisfies $n\equiv -i(p)\pmod{p^2}$ for all $p\le y$, hence $n$ is bounded by the product \[ n \le \prod_{p\le y} p^2 = \exp\left(2\sum_{p\le y}\log p\right). \] A standard Chebyshev bound gives $\sum_{p\le y}\log p \asymp y$ and also $\pi(y)\asymp \dfrac{y}{\log y}$. Therefore $\log X\asymp y$ and \[ \pi(y) \asymp \frac{y}{\log y} \asymp \frac{\log X}{\log\log X}. \] This provides infinitely many $X$ with a squarefree gap of size $\gg \log X/\log\log X$. Taking $c>0$ to be a suitable absolute constant completes the proof. $\square$

5. SANITY CHECK

  • Lemma 3.2 is consistent with the known density of squarefree numbers: $6/\pi^2\approx 0.6079$.
  • The computed maximal gaps up to $10^7$ (at most $10$) are consistent with the easy upper bound $\ll\sqrt{x}$.
  • The CRT construction in Lemma 3.4 is structurally analogous to the classical constructions of long prime gaps (Erdős--Rankin type), but with prime squares.

6. FINAL

UNRESOLVED.
  • (i) Best partial results proved here. We proved:
    1. The classical counting asymptotic $Q(x)=\frac{6}{\pi^2}x+O(\sqrt{x})$.
    2. A consequent unconditional gap upper bound $s_{n+1}-s_n\ll\sqrt{s_n}$.
    3. A constructive lower bound showing gaps of size $\gg \log x/\log\log x$ occur infinitely often.
    4. Small-scale computation: max gap $\le 10$ for squarefrees up to $10^7$.

  • (ii) First gap/obstruction to a full solution. The question asks for a much stronger upper bound: $s_{n+1}-s_n=o(s_n^{1/5})$. The elementary $O(\sqrt{x})$-type methods above are far from the $x^{1/5}$ scale. Reaching exponents near $1/5$ requires deep estimates for squarefree numbers in very short intervals (lattice-point counts near curves / exponential sum technology).

  • (iii) What would be needed next. To resolve the problem one would need either:
    1. a proof that every interval $[X,X+X^{1/5}/g(X)]$ (with $g(X)\to\infty$) contains a squarefree number, or
    2. equivalently, a global upper bound on the maximal squarefree gap up to $X$ of order $o(X^{1/5})$.
    This lies beyond classical inclusion--exclusion and requires the strongest known short-interval methods. (As noted in the problem statement, much sharper results are known in the literature, including improvements beyond the $1/5$ exponent.)

7. OPTIONAL: SHARPENING / DISCUSSION

  • The lower bound construction in Lemma 3.4 can be optimized (with more sophisticated residue-class choices) to increase the constant in front of $\log x/\log\log x$; the conjectured/known-best constants in the literature are substantially larger.
  • Conditional results (e.g. from the ABC conjecture) can force gaps to be extremely small (polylogarithmic), which would imply $o(x^{1/5})$ immediately.
