Statement

Let $A\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ be the vertex set of a convex $n$-gon (i.e. $|A|=n$ and the points are in convex position). Let \[ U_\mathrm{conv}(A):=|\{\{x,y\}\subset A: |x-y|=1\}| \] be the number of unit-distance pairs among the vertices. Conjecture (Erdős--Moser): there exists an absolute constant $C$ such that for all $n$ and all convex $n$-gons, \[ U_\mathrm{conv}(A)\le Cn. \]

Erdős Problem #96 --- Round 2

1) ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE

I pursue (A) proof track. A full resolution of the open problem still looks out of reach, but Round-1 only produced the generic planar bound $u(A)=O(n^{3/2})$, which does not use the convex structure in any essential way. The most promising strict advance is therefore to prove linear upper bounds on natural structured subclasses of convex polygons, and to make precise the local-to-global reduction from the related equidistance problem (Problem #97) that was only mentioned in Round-1.

2) Round-1 FOUNDATION USED

I use the following Round-1 results without reproving them.

  • Round-1 Lemma 96.1: a regular $n$-gon scaled so that its side length is $1$ has exactly $n$ unit-distance pairs.
  • Round-1 Lemma 96.2: every convex $n$-gon satisfies the generic planar bound \[ u(A)\le \frac{\sqrt 2}{2}n^{3/2}+\frac14 n. \]
  • Round-1 context from the problem statement: Problem #96 asks whether $u(A)=O(n)$ for convex $n$-gons, and a positive answer would follow from the related local equidistance problem (Problem #97).

3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)

There are three genuinely new ingredients in this round.

  • Exact cyclic theorem. If the vertices lie on a circle, then for every fixed distance $r$ the corresponding distance-$r$ graph has maximum degree at most $2$, hence at most $n$ edges. In particular, among cyclic convex $n$-gons the maximum possible number of unit distances is exactly $n$.
  • Parabolic linear theorem. If the vertices lie on a parabola, then for every fixed distance $r$ each vertex has at most $4$ neighbors at distance $r$, because the distance equation reduces to a quartic. Hence the number of unit distances is at most $2n$ on this subclass.
  • Explicit local-to-global reduction. If every convex polygon has a vertex from which no distance is repeated more than $k$ times, then every convex $n$-gon has at most $3+k(n-3)\le kn$ unit distances. In particular, Problem #97 would imply the explicit bound $u(A)\le 3n-6$.

4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)

The exact gaps left after Round-1 were:

  • the Round-1 upper bound was the generic $O(n^{3/2})$ planar bound and did not exploit convex structure;
  • the statement "a positive answer would follow from Problem #97" was not proved;
  • no substantial subclass of convex polygons had been solved sharply.

The plan is therefore:

  1. solve the problem exactly on the cyclic subclass;
  2. prove a second linear theorem on a different curved subclass (parabolas), to show that the obstruction is not present there either;
  3. turn the heuristic relation to Problem #97 into a rigorous induction with an explicit constant.

5) WORK (ROUND-2)

For a finite set $A\subset \mathbb R^2$ and a fixed real number $r>0$, write \[ e_r(A):=\left|\{\{x,y\}\subset A:\ \|x-y\|=r\}\right| \] for the number of unordered pairs at distance exactly $r$. Thus $u(A)=e_1(A)$.

Proposition 96.1 (exact fixed-distance bound on a circle). Let $A\subset \mathbb R^2$ be a finite set of $n$ points lying on a circle. Then for every $r>0$, \[ e_r(A)\le n. \] In particular, if $A$ is the vertex set of a cyclic convex $n$-gon, then \[ u(A)\le n. \]

Proof. Fix $r>0$. Form the graph $G_r(A)$ on vertex set $A$ by joining two vertices when they are at distance $r$. It is enough to prove that every vertex of $G_r(A)$ has degree at most $2$.

Fix $x\in A$. All points of $A$ lie on a common circle $\Gamma$. The neighbors of $x$ in $G_r(A)$ are precisely the points of $A\cap C(x,r)$, where $C(x,r)$ denotes the circle of radius $r$ centered at $x$. Now $\Gamma$ and $C(x,r)$ are two circles in the Euclidean plane. Since $r>0$, the center of $C(x,r)$ is $x$, while $x\in \Gamma$, so $\Gamma$ and $C(x,r)$ are not the same circle. Hence they intersect in at most two points. Therefore \[ \deg_{G_r(A)}(x)=|A\cap \Gamma\cap C(x,r)|\le 2. \] This holds for every vertex $x$, so the handshake lemma gives \[ 2e_r(A)=\sum_{x\in A}\deg_{G_r(A)}(x)\le 2n. \] Thus $e_r(A)\le n$, as claimed. The case $r=1$ is exactly the unit-distance bound. $\square$

Corollary 96.2 (cyclic subclass solved sharply). Among cyclic convex $n$-gons, the maximum possible number of unit-distance pairs is exactly $n$.

Proof. The upper bound is Proposition 96.1 with $r=1$. The matching lower bound $n$ is exactly Round-1 Lemma 96.1, which exhibits a regular $n$-gon with exactly $n$ unit distances. $\square$

Proposition 96.3 (linear fixed-distance bound on a parabola). Let $A$ be a finite subset of the standard parabola \[ \mathcal P:=\{(t,t^2): t\in \mathbb R\}. \] Then for every $r>0$, \[ e_r(A)\le 2|A|. \] In particular, if the vertices of a convex $n$-gon lie on a parabola, then the number of unit-distance pairs is at most $2n$.

Proof. Fix $r>0$ and form the distance-$r$ graph $G_r(A)$ as above. It is enough to prove that every vertex has degree at most $4$.

Fix a vertex $p=(a,a^2)\in A$. A point $q=(x,x^2)\in \mathcal P$ satisfies $\|p-q\|=r$ exactly when \[ (x-a)^2+(x^2-a^2)^2=r^2. \] Since $x^2-a^2=(x-a)(x+a)$, this becomes \[ (x-a)^2\left(1+(x+a)^2\right)=r^2. \] Expanding gives the quartic equation \[ x^4-2ax^3+(1+2a^2)x^2-2a(1+a^2)x+a^4+a^2-r^2=0. \] A real quartic has at most four real roots. Therefore there are at most four real numbers $x$ such that $(x,x^2)\in \mathcal P$ lies at distance $r$ from $p$. Hence \[ \deg_{G_r(A)}(p)\le 4. \] Since this holds for every $p\in A$, the handshake lemma gives \[ 2e_r(A)=\sum_{p\in A}\deg_{G_r(A)}(p)\le 4|A|, \] so $e_r(A)\le 2|A|$.

For the final sentence: every parabola is the image of $\mathcal P$ under a Euclidean similarity. Similarities preserve incidences of "being at a fixed distance", up to replacing the distance $1$ by another positive number $r$. Since the proposition was proved for every $r>0$, the same $2n$ bound holds for any finite set lying on any parabola, and in particular for convex polygons inscribed in a parabola. $\square$

Proposition 96.4 (rigorous reduction from Problem #97 to Problem #96). Assume the following local multiplicity hypothesis for some integer $k\ge 1$:

For every convex polygon $P$ there exists a vertex $v$ of $P$ such that no distance from $v$ to the other vertices of $P$ occurs more than $k$ times.

Then every convex $n$-gon $A$ satisfies \[ u(A)\le 3+k(n-3)\le kn \qquad (n\ge 3). \] In particular, an affirmative answer to Problem #97 (the case $k=3$) would imply \[ u(A)\le 3n-6 \] for every convex $n$-gon $A$.

Proof. Let $U(n)$ be the maximum possible number of unit-distance pairs among convex $n$-gons. We prove by induction that \[ U(n)\le 3+k(n-3) \qquad (n\ge 3). \]

Base case $n=3$: every triangle has at most $3$ unit distances, so $U(3)\le 3$, which matches the formula.

Induction step: assume $n\ge 4$ and let $A=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ be a convex $n$-gon with $u(A)=U(n)$. By the hypothesis, there exists a vertex $v=v_i$ of $A$ such that among the distances from $v$ to the other $n-1$ vertices, no value occurs more than $k$ times. In particular, the distance value $1$ occurs at most $k$ times from $v$, so $v$ is incident to at most $k$ unit-distance pairs.

Remove $v$ from the vertex list. The remaining points, in the induced cyclic order, form a convex $(n-1)$-gon $A'$. Every unit-distance pair of $A$ either survives inside $A'$ or is incident to $v$. Therefore \[ U(n)=u(A)\le u(A')+k\le U(n-1)+k. \] By the induction hypothesis, \[ U(n)\le \left(3+k(n-4)\right)+k = 3+k(n-3). \] This proves the claim. The inequality $3+k(n-3)\le kn$ is immediate for $n\ge 3$ and $k\ge 1$.

For Problem #97 we have $k=3$, because "no other $4$ vertices equidistant from it" means exactly that each distance value from the chosen vertex is repeated at most $3$ times. Hence $U(n)\le 3n-6$. $\square$

Proposition 96.5 (Erdős's stronger local-sum conjecture would imply the $2n$ upper bound). For a convex polygon $A$, define \[ g_A(x):=\max_{r>0}\left|\{y\in A\setminus\{x\}:\ \|x-y\|=r\}\right|. \] Then \[ 2u(A)\le \sum_{x\in A} g_A(x). \] Consequently, the stronger conjecture \[ \sum_{x\in A}g_A(x)<4|A| \] would imply \[ u(A)<2|A|. \]

Proof. For each vertex $x\in A$, the number of unit-distance neighbors of $x$ is \[ d_1(x):=\left|\{y\in A\setminus\{x\}:\ \|x-y\|=1\}\right|. \] Since $g_A(x)$ is the maximum multiplicity of any distance from $x$, we have $d_1(x)\le g_A(x)$ for every $x$. Summing over all vertices and using the handshake lemma for the unit-distance graph, \[ 2u(A)=\sum_{x\in A} d_1(x)\le \sum_{x\in A} g_A(x). \] The final implication is immediate. $\square$

6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION

  • Cyclic theorem: the only geometric input is that two distinct circles intersect in at most two points. They are indeed distinct, because the circle centered at a vertex $x$ cannot coincide with the common circumcircle: its center is $x$, while $x$ lies on the circumcircle and a circle never contains its own center.
  • Handshake counts: in Propositions 96.1 and 96.3, the graph is simple and undirected, so $2e_r(A)=\sum \deg(x)$ is valid.
  • Parabola theorem: the quartic equation was expanded explicitly. The argument only uses "at most four real roots", so repeated roots and tangent cases do not cause trouble. Also $r>0$ excludes the trivial coincidence $q=p$.
  • Similarity step for parabolas: a Euclidean similarity preserves the number of pairs at any prescribed distance value up to rescaling the value. Because Proposition 96.3 was proved for every $r>0$, the transfer to an arbitrary parabola is legitimate.
  • Reduction from Problem #97: deleting one vertex from a convex polygon leaves a convex polygon on the remaining vertices in cyclic order. The local multiplicity condition implies the chosen vertex has at most $k$ unit-distance neighbors because "distance $1$" is one of the allowed distance values. No additional assumption is hidden.
  • Use of Round-1: the only Round-1 theorem used in a nontrivial way is the regular-$n$-gon lower bound to prove sharpness of the cyclic result. No Round-1 proof was re-run.

7) FINAL

UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED)

This round strictly advances beyond Round-1 in three ways.

  1. The problem is solved exactly on the cyclic subclass: a cyclic convex $n$-gon has at most $n$ unit distances, and this bound is sharp by the regular $n$-gon.
  2. A second genuine linear theorem is proved on a different curved subclass: any convex $n$-gon inscribed in a parabola has at most $2n$ unit distances.
  3. The relation to Problem #97 is now rigorous and explicit: a positive answer to Problem #97 would imply the concrete bound $u(A)\le 3n-6$ for every convex $n$-gon. Moreover, Erdős's stronger local-sum conjecture would imply the stronger global bound $u(A)<2n$.

The main unresolved gap is still the passage from these structured situations (cyclic, parabolic, or local multiplicity control) to arbitrary convex polygons.

Problem 96: a two-degeneracy argument on a parabola

1) OBJECTIVE AND SOURCE REVIEW

Attempt dated 2026-09-23. The target is a linear bound for unit distances among vertices in convex position. I read gpt_pro_5.4/96.tex fully. Its circle argument is sound and its quartic-root argument gives $2n$ for a parabola, though its expanded quartic is incorrect. I strengthen the latter bound to $2n-3$ for $n\ge2$ using a vertex that can always be removed.

2) WORK

Let $P(t)=(t,t^2)$ and fix a distance $r>0$. In a finite set of parameters choose $a$ with $|a|$ minimal. Reflection permits $a\ge0$. Every remaining parameter lies either in $[a,\infty)$ or in $(-\infty,-a]$. The squared distance from $P(a)$ is \[ D_a(x)=(x-a)^2+(x^2-a^2)^2, \qquad D_a'(x)=2(x-a)\left(1+2x(x+a)\right). \] For $x>a$ both factors are positive. For $x<-a$, the first factor is negative and $x(x+a)\ge0$, so the second is positive. Thus $D_a$ is strictly increasing on the right ray and strictly decreasing as $x$ increases on the left ray. Each ray contributes at most one point with $D_a(x)=r^2$. The chosen vertex has at most two distance-$r$ neighbors.

Every subset has the same property, by choosing its own smallest absolute parameter. Removing such vertices until two remain counts each edge once and gives \[ e_r(A)\le2(n-2)+1=2n-3\qquad(n\ge2). \] For $n=1$ the bound is zero. Euclidean similarities transport this argument to every parabola, because the prescribed distance merely rescales. No assumption of uniform spacing is used.

3) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION

The actual expansion is \[ D_a(x)-r^2=x^4+(1-2a^2)x^2-2ax+a^4+a^2-r^2; \] the cubic term and several coefficients in the supplied expansion should be removed or changed. This does not invalidate its root-count bound, but it does invalidate the printed polynomial identity. When $a=0$, the two rays meet only at the center point, which cannot be a distance-$r$ neighbor. When both $a$ and $-a$ occur, the latter is included in the left ray without duplicating the chosen point.

4) FINAL

UNRESOLVED. The proof certifies a stronger linear bound on the parabolic subclass. General convex polygons do not admit a common parabolic parameter or this monotonicity argument. A uniformly bounded-degree vertex in every convex subset remains unproved here.

5) SOURCES CHECKED

The complete supplied version, read 2026-09-23. The algebra, differentiation, and deletion proof are self-contained and do not use an unverified external geometric theorem. No literature-priority claim is made.

6) COMPLETION ESTIMATE

Subjective progress toward arbitrary convex polygons. COMPLETION: 12%

FORMAL RESTATEMENT. Let $A\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ be the vertex set of a convex $n$-gon (i.e. $|A|=n$ and the points are in convex position). Let \[ U_\mathrm{conv}(A):=|\{\{x,y\}\subset A: |x-y|=1\}| \] be the number of unit-distance pairs among the vertices. Conjecture (Erdős--Moser): there exists an absolute constant $C$ such that for all $n$ and all convex $n$-gons, \[ U_\mathrm{conv}(A)\le Cn. \]

QUICK LITERATURE/CONTEXT CHECK. The problem statement reports known bounds: $O(n\log n)$ and more precise variants, and a lower bound construction with $2n-7$ unit distances. I do not re-prove those results here. Instead I record an elementary linear lower bound and an elementary $O(n^{3/2})$ upper bound (which holds without convexity).

ATTACK PLAN.

  • Lower bound sanity: show that $\Omega(n)$ unit distances are attainable even with convexity (regular polygon).
  • Upper bound (weak): apply the general unit-distance $O(n^{3/2})$ bound (Lemma 90.2) to convex point sets.
  • (Unresolved) improvement: exploit convex position to beat the generic $n^{3/2}$ bound, aiming toward $O(n\log n)$ and then $O(n)$.

WORK.

Lemma 96.1 (convex linear lower bound). For every $n\ge 3$ there exists a convex $n$-gon with at least $n$ unit-distance pairs. Proof. Take a regular $n$-gon and scale it so that its side length is $1$. Then each of the $n$ sides is a unit distance. We claim no diagonal has length $1$. Indeed, in a regular $n$-gon with circumradius $R$, the chord length between vertices at step $k$ is $2R\sin(k\pi/n)$. After scaling to make the $k=1$ chord equal to $1$, we have $2R\sin(\pi/n)=1$. If $2R\sin(k\pi/n)=1$ also held for some $2\le k\le n-2$, then $\sin(k\pi/n)=\sin(\pi/n)$, which for $0<k\pi/n<\pi$ forces $k=1$ or $k=n-1$. Thus only adjacent pairs are at distance $1$, giving exactly $n$ unit distances. $\square$

Lemma 96.2 (generic $O(n^{3/2})$ upper bound). For every convex $n$-gon vertex set $A\subset\mathbb{R}^2$, \[ U_\mathrm{conv}(A)\le \frac{\sqrt 2}{2}\,n^{3/2}+\frac14 n. \] Proof. This is immediate from Lemma 90.2, which bounds the number of unit-distance pairs in any $n$-point set in the plane; convexity is an additional constraint and cannot increase the maximum. $\square$

FAST REALITY CHECK (regular polygons). For regular $n$-gons scaled to side length $1$, a direct computation confirms the unit-distance count equals $n$ for $3\le n\le 10$: 

n=3 -> 3 unit pairs
n=4 -> 4 unit pairs
n=5 -> 5 unit pairs
...
n=10 -> 10 unit pairs

VERIFICATION.

  • Lemma 96.1: verified the chord-length formula and the fact $\sin(k\pi/n)=\sin(\pi/n)$ has only the symmetric solutions $k=1,n-1$ in $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$.
  • Lemma 96.2: relies only on Lemma 90.2, whose proof uses the circle-intersection ($\le2$) property and a $K_{2,3}$ extremal bound.
  • Boundary case $n=3$: the regular triangle has 3 unit sides, consistent with Lemma 96.1.

FINAL. UNRESOLVED

  1. (i) Strongest proved partial result. There are convex $n$-gons with $\ge n$ unit distances (Lemma 96.1). Conversely, any convex $n$-gon has $O(n^{3/2})$ unit distances by the general bound (Lemma 96.2).
  2. (ii) First gap (crisp). Use convexity to improve the $O(n^{3/2})$ bound, ideally proving $U_\mathrm{conv}(A)=O(n)$ for all convex $n$-gons.
  3. (iii) Top 3 next moves.
    • Prove a per-vertex bound: show that for a convex $n$-gon, each vertex can be at unit distance from at most $O(\log n)$ (or $O(1)$) other vertices, then sum over vertices.
    • Translate unit distances into incidences between vertices and unit circles, and exploit the cyclic order on the convex hull to strengthen the $K_{2,3}$-free extremal-graph estimate.
    • Search computationally for convex $n$-gons with many unit distances to guess extremal patterns (e.g. mixtures of arcs/chords), which may suggest the right combinatorial decomposition.
  4. (iv) Likely structure of a minimal counterexample. A counterexample to linearity would require a convex $n$-gon with superlinear many unit chords. Such a configuration would likely place many vertices on a few arcs of circles of radius 1 around many other vertices, creating a highly constrained "almost-circular" structure with many repeated chord lengths.
