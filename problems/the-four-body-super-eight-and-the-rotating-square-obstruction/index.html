Definitions and notation.
In the Newtonian $n$-body problem, positions $q_i\in\mathbb R^d$ and masses $m_i>0$ obey $m_i\ddot q_i=\nabla_{q_i}U$, where $U(q)=\sum_{i<j}m_im_j/|q_i-q_j|$ and the gravitational constant is normalized to one. Here $T=\tfrac12\sum_i m_i|\dot q_i|^2$, $E=T-U$, and a collision has $q_i=q_j$. The center of mass can be fixed at zero; moment of inertia is $I=\sum_i m_i|q_i|^2$. A choreography has all bodies traverse one closed curve with prescribed phase shifts. The super-eight is the named four-body equal-mass periodic orbit drawn and discussed in the workshop. Its detailed symmetry and topological class are part of that geometric specification; an arbitrary four-body periodic solution is not enough.

An explicit competing choreography.
Put four equal masses $m>0$ at the vertices of a rotating square of circumradius $R$: $q_j(t)=R(\cos(\omega t+j\pi/2),\sin(\omega t+j\pi/2))$, $j=0,1,2,3$. For the vertex $(R,0)$, the opposite vertex contributes acceleration $-m/(4R^2)$ in the radial direction. Each adjacent vertex contributes radial acceleration $-m/(2\sqrt2R^2)$; transverse components cancel. Hence the configuration solves Newton's equations exactly when \[\omega^2=\frac{m}{R^3}\left(\frac14+\frac1{\sqrt2}\right). \] The other equations follow by rotation. It is collision free, periodic, and all bodies follow the same circle with equal phase shifts.
Why a generic choreography proof is insufficient.
The potential is $U=m^2(2\sqrt2+1)/R$, by counting four edges of length $\sqrt2R$ and two diagonals of length $2R$. The kinetic energy is $T=2m\omega^2R^2=U/2$, which independently checks the force coefficient. Thus a variational scheme that imposes only equal masses and cyclic choreography has this exact competitor; existence in that broad class does not identify the super-eight's curve, crossings or topological/symmetry data.
