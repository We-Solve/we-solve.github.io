Literature and context check

This is the retained duplicate of the unequal degree-utility question. The original report and the two listed degree-optimization abstracts were consulted. A different failed algorithmic route is investigated here, without presenting the duplicate as a new problem.

Research attempt: Independent degree optimization can choose a nongraphical vector

One might optimize degrees independently subject only to $0\le d_i\le n-1$ and $\sum_i d_i=2m$, then realize the resulting sequence. Those necessary constraints are insufficient. For $n=4,m=3$, the vector $(3,3,0,0)$ satisfies both, yet is not the degree vector of a simple graph: a degree-three vertex must be adjacent to all other vertices, contradicting either vertex of degree zero.

This obstruction can be forced as the unique optimizer of the relaxed utility problem. Give vertices 1 and 2 utility one exactly at degree three and zero at other degrees; give vertices 3 and 4 utility one exactly at degree zero and zero at other degrees. The relaxed vector $(3,3,0,0)$ has objective four, and any different vector loses at least one of the four indicator rewards, so it is uniquely optimal in the relaxation. No graph attains value four by the preceding contradiction.

The exact graph optimum is one. To prove an upper bound, suppose a graph has a degree-three vertex among 1 and 2. All other vertices then have positive degree, so neither zero-degree reward can occur. If both vertices 1 and 2 had degree three, their incident edges would include their common edge and four further distinct edges, at least five edges, impossible with $m=3$. Thus this case yields at most one reward. If neither distinguished vertex has degree three, only vertices 3 and 4 can give rewards. They cannot both be isolated, since the remaining two vertices support at most one simple edge, fewer than three. Again there is at most one reward. A three-edge star centered at vertex 1 gives one reward, proving attainability.

The gap four versus one is not an integrality gap for a claimed sophisticated polyhedral formulation; it belongs to the explicitly weakened degree-sum relaxation. Adding a realization oracle after optimization detects the failure but does not by itself find the best realizable sequence. The first missing algorithmic step is how to enforce joint graphicality while optimizing all unequal utility tables, rather than assuming the handshake constraint is enough.

Verification and adversarial audit

The degree-sum and degree-bound checks are explicit, the relaxed optimum is unique, and the graph optimum is proved by exhausting the reward possibilities. Both the five-edge obstruction and the isolated-pair obstruction use the simple-graph convention.
Audit corrections.
The relaxation is named precisely, and nongraphical degree vectors are not treated as feasible graph certificates. The explicit optimum comparison avoids an unsupported hardness inference.

Literature and context check

The report asks about different vertex functions. The consulted 2017/2018 paper abstract treats identical functions and other special cases; the 2019 Deza--Onn abstract explicitly leaves arbitrary functions over complete graphs broadly unresolved there. Those abstracts do not establish a complete present-day classification, and no current-status certification is claimed. Here functions are supplied as finite rational tables on degrees 0 through n-1.

Research attempt: Linear utilities are easy but greedy nonlinear gains can be arbitrarily bad

For the linear subclass $f_i(d)=a_i d+b_i$, the objective for a simple graph $E$ is \[ \sum_i b_i+\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}(a_i+a_j). \] This follows by counting the contribution $a_i$ once for each edge incident to $i$. Thus, for $0\le m\le\binom n2$, choosing the $m$ largest edge weights $a_i+a_j$ is optimal. If a proposed set omits a larger weight than one it contains, exchanging the two improves the sum; repeating exchanges yields the top-$m$ set. This provides an exact polynomial-time subclass under the stated table/arithmetic model.

A natural attempted extension adds the currently highest marginal-gain edge repeatedly for nonlinear utilities. It can fail by an unbounded factor. Let $n=4,m=2$, set $f_1(0)=f_1(1)=0$, $f_1(2)=M$ for $M>2$, and set $f_i(d)=d$ for $i=2,3,4$. Values at unused degrees can be chosen arbitrarily, for example $f_1(3)=2M$. Initially each edge among vertices 2,3,4 has gain two, while each edge incident to vertex 1 has gain one. After selecting any edge among 2,3,4, every remaining edge among those three still has gain two and every edge incident to 1 still has gain one. The greedy rule therefore chooses two edges avoiding vertex 1 and obtains objective four.

Two distinct edges incident to vertex 1 instead give it degree two and the other vertices total degree two, for objective $M+2$. This is optimal: if vertex 1 has degree zero the value is four; if it has degree one the remaining vertices' degree sum is three, giving value three; if it has degree two the value is $M+2$. These exhaust its possible degrees when $m=2$. The ratio $(M+2)/4$ diverges with $M$, although the integer M has a short binary encoding. Thus local marginal choice does not extend the linear solution, even for a nondecreasing convex table at the distinguished vertex.

Verification and adversarial audit

The greedy choices are forced by strict gain inequalities, not adverse tie-breaking. Every possible degree of vertex 1 is checked to prove the exact optimum, and all graphs are simple with precisely two edges.
Audit corrections.
A bad greedy approximation is not used as a proof of NP-hardness. Results stated in consulted abstracts are not generalized beyond their hypotheses.
