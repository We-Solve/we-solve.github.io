Multiplicity Decoding for Order-Domain Codes

Status Partially solved · unsolvedmath.comLLM status Open unverified

Statement

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

List decoding uses interpolation constraints at received symbols. Multiplicity requires specified derivatives or algebraic orders of vanishing in those constraints. An order domain supplies an algebra with an ordering/weight function used to construct evaluation codes.

A code $C\subseteq\Sigma^N$ is a set of permitted length-$N$ words. Its Hamming minimum distance is the minimum number of unequal coordinates of two distinct words. For a linear $[N,K,D]_q$ code, rate is $K/N$ and relative distance is $D/N$; decoding recovers a codeword from corrupted observations.

List decoding returns every codeword within a prescribed distance of the received word, allowing several answers. Efficient list decoding requires polynomial runtime and a controlled output-list size; the intended capacity and alphabet are those in the source.

Statement recorded in the supplied source.
Extend the Geil–Matsumoto Sudan-type list-decoding algorithm for order-domain codes to use multiplicities. In particular, develop an appropriate notion of multiplicity in order-domain theory. [S2]

Verbatim supplied catalogue question

Extend the Geil–Matsumoto Sudan-type list-decoding algorithm for order-domain codes to use multiplicities. In particular, develop an appropriate notion of multiplicity in order-domain theory.

Attempted multiplicity construction

For a polynomial $Q$ at a point $a$, define its multiplicity as the smallest total degree of a nonzero term in $Q(a+U)$, and give zero polynomial infinite multiplicity. Equivalently this is the largest $s$ with $Q\in(X_1-a_1,\ldots,X_r-a_r)^s$. This Taylor-coefficient definition works in every characteristic. Ordinary derivatives alone would fail, for example on $X^p$ in characteristic $p$, whose first derivative is zero even though its multiplicity at zero is the finite number $p$. We first prove the polynomial-ring zero estimate used by the report, then locate what does not transfer to an arbitrary order domain.
Complete multiplicity bound on a grid.
For a nonzero polynomial $P$ in $r$ variables of total degree at most $D$ and a set $T$ of $n\ge1$ distinct field elements, \[ \sum_{a\in T^r}\operatorname{mult}(P,a)\le Dn^{r-1}. \] For $r=1$, distinct linear factors are coprime and their product, with root multiplicities, divides $P$ by successive polynomial division; their total degree is at most $D$. For the induction write $P(X,Y)=\sum_{j=0}^lP_j(X)Y^j$, with $P_l\ne0$ and $r-1$ variables $X$. At each $a\in T^{r-1}$ let $m=\operatorname{mult}(P_l,a)$ and choose a multi-index $\alpha$ of size $m$ whose coefficient in $P_l(a+U)$ is nonzero. In the expansion $P(a+U,Y)=\sum_\beta U^\beta R_\beta(Y)$, the polynomial $R_\alpha$ is nonzero of degree exactly $l$. For every $b\in T$, one of the terms at total degree $m+\operatorname{mult}(R_\alpha,b)$ has a nonzero coefficient, so \[ \operatorname{mult}(P,(a,b))\le m+\operatorname{mult}(R_\alpha,b). \] Sum first over $b$ and use the univariate bound to get at most $nm+l$. Then sum over $a$. Since $\deg P_l\le D-l$, the induction hypothesis bounds the result by $n(D-l)n^{r-2}+l n^{r-1}=Dn^{r-1}$.
Interpolation consequence, with exact equation counts.
Let messages be polynomials $f(X)$ of total degree at most $h\ge1$, evaluated on $T^r$. Seek a nonzero $Q(X,Z)$ of weighted degree at most $D$, with weights $(1,\ldots,1,h)$, that lies in $(X-a,Z-y_a)^s$ at every received point $(a,y_a)$, where $s\ge1$. There are exactly $\binom{s+r}{r+1}$ monomials of total degree less than $s$ in $r+1$ local variables, so each point imposes at most that many homogeneous linear equations. The number of available monomials is exactly \[ M(D)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor D/h\rfloor}\binom{D-hj+r}{r}. \] If $M(D)>n^r\binom{s+r}{r+1}$, rank-nullity guarantees a nonzero solution of these linear equations. This imposes multiplicity at least $s$, not an unjustified equality exactly $s$. If $f(a)=y_a$, substituting $Z=f(X)$ sends every generator of the local maximal ideal into $(X-a)$; hence $Q(X,f(X))$ has multiplicity at least $s$ at each agreement point. Its degree is at most $D$ by the weighted bound. If there are $A$ agreements and $sA>Dn^{r-1}$, the grid theorem forces $Q(X,f(X))=0$ identically. Distinct polynomial solutions $f$ are distinct roots of the nonzero polynomial $Q$ in $Z$ over the field $\mathbb F(X)$, so there are at most $\deg_ZQ$ of them, by univariate root division. This proves containment and a list-size bound; it does not by itself supply a factorization algorithm or an order-domain extension.
A direct local-ideal transfer loses additivity at a singular point.
Consider $R=\mathbb F[t^2,t^3]$ and $\mathfrak m=(t^2,t^3)$. Define $\mu(g)=\max\{s:g\in\mathfrak m^s\}$ for nonzero $g$. Every monomial in $\mathfrak m^s$ has exponent at least $2s$, so $t^2,t^3\notin\mathfrak m^2$, and both have $\mu=1$. But $(t^3)^2=t^6=(t^2)^3$ lies in $\mathfrak m^3$ and not in $\mathfrak m^4$, giving $\mu(t^6)=3>2\mu(t^3)$. The usual order-of-vanishing-on-a-smooth-curve argument, which adds multiplicities under products, therefore cannot be transplanted unchanged using this local-ideal order. This calculation does not deny useful multiplicity notions on order domains, or their possible nonadditive bounds. It identifies the false inference in the attempted direct transfer.
