inverse of an integer matrix

Status Open · unsolvedmath.comLLM status Solved unverified

Statement

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
$\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, and $\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$ denote the integers, rationals, reals and complex numbers. Any other conventions are specified locally.

Let $m,n\geq1$ with $m\leq n$, let $D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_m)$ with integers $d_i\geq2$, and let $X\in\mathbb Z^{m\times n}$. Set $M=[D\ X]\in\mathbb Z^{m\times(m+n)}$. An integer right inverse is $Y\in\mathbb Z^{(m+n)\times m}$ with $MY=I_m$.

Statement recorded in the supplied source.
Give necessary and sufficient conditions on $D,X$ for an integer right inverse to exist. In particular, is the necessary rowwise condition \[\gcd(d_i,X_{i1},\ldots,X_{in})=1\quad(1\leq i\leq m)\] also sufficient? [S2]

Short English statement

Characterize when the indicated integer matrix has an integer right inverse, and decide whether coprime entries in each row suffice.

Integer right inverses of [D X]: a finite rank criterion

Integer right inverses of $[D\ X]$: a finite rank criterion

OpenAI GPT 6.1 Sol (Max reasoning)

This research was conducted under the direction and oversight of Wentao Li.

12 October 2026

Abstract

For the full recorded range $1\le m\le n$, an integer right inverse of $[D\ X]$ exists precisely when, for each prime $p$ dividing some $d_i$, the rows of $X$ indexed by $\{i:p\mid d_i\}$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb F_p$. We prove this criterion and construct an inverse from Bézout coefficients and adjugates. Rowwise coprimality alone fails, already for $m=n=2$. These are applications of established integer linear algebra; no mathematical novelty is claimed.

Original target and provenance

The recorded target, UnsolvedMath ID 3030 (OPG-50149), comes from Steven A. Gregory's question posted on Open Problem Garden on 27 May 2013 [garden,catalogue]. For every pair of integers $1\le m\le n$, every diagonal matrix $D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_m)$ with integers $d_i\ge2$, and every $X\in\mathbb Z^{m\times n}$, determine necessary and sufficient conditions for \[ M=[D\ X]\in\mathbb Z^{m\times(m+n)} \quad\text{to admit}\quad Y\in\mathbb Z^{(m+n)\times m},\qquad MY=I_m. \] The source also asks whether $\gcd(d_i,X_{i1},\ldots,X_{in})=1$ for each row $i$ is sufficient. Both questions are answered below. The original formulation is retained: entries of $X$ may be zero or negative, the $d_i$ need not be distinct or coprime, and $X$ need not have full row rank even over $\mathbb Q$.

The general criterion by maximal minors is standard, equivalently expressible by Smith normal form. Smith's original paper [smith] and Stanley's account [stanley, Theorems 2.1$4$ and 2.4] document the relevant theory. A related 2024 MathOverflow answer by loup blanc states the integer right-inverse criterion in Smith-form language [mo]. Our main proof supplies the determinant argument, specialization and inverse construction explicitly, rather than assuming Smith normal form. This is a complete answer to a catalogue gap using known mathematics, not a claim to solve a newly open research problem.

The characterization

Write $[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}$ and set \[ P=\prod_{i=1}^m d_i, \qquad S_p=\{i\in[m]:p\mid d_i\}. \] For row and column sets $I,J$, $X_{I,J}$ means the submatrix in increasing index order. Write $X_{S_p,:}$ for its indicated rows and all $n$ columns. Empty matrices have rank zero and determinant one; an empty product equals one. Gcds are nonnegative, and signs and zero entries are allowed in their arguments.
Theorem: Under all the original hypotheses, the following are equivalent:
  1. $M$ has an integer right inverse.
  2. Its column lattice is $D\mathbb Z^m+X\mathbb Z^n=\mathbb Z^m$.
  3. The gcd of all $m\times m$ minors of $M$ is one; explicitly, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-77-65-69-67-68-74-65-64} \gcd_{\substack{I\subseteq[m],\ J\subseteq[n]\\|I|=|J|}} \left(\left(\prod_{i\notin I}d_i\right)\det X_{I,J}\right)=1. \end{equation}
  4. For every prime $p\mid P$, \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-6c-6f-63-61-6c} \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb F_p}(X_{S_p,:}\bmod p)=|S_p|. \end{equation}
Thus factoring $P$ and doing finitely many Gaussian eliminations over prime fields decides the question. No tests modulo higher prime powers are required.
Lemma (Standard maximal-minor criterion, with construction): Let $A\in\mathbb Z^{r\times q}$ with $1\le r\le q$. For each increasing $r$-element column set $J$, let $E_J\in\mathbb Z^{q\times r}$ have those standard basis columns, and let $A_J=AE_J$. Then $A$ has an integer right inverse if and only if $\gcd_J\det A_J=1$. Whenever integers $c_J$ satisfy $\sum_J c_J\det A_J=1$, one such inverse is \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-63-6f-6e-73-74-72-75-63-74} R=\sum_J c_J E_J\operatorname{adj}(A_J). \end{equation}
Proof: If $AR=I_r$, determinant multilinearity gives Cauchy--Binet: \[ 1=\det(AR)=\sum_{|J|=r}\det(A_J)\det(R_{J,:}). \] For completeness, expand each column of $AR$ as a linear combination of columns of $A$. Terms with a repeated column vanish. Grouping the remaining terms by their increasing index set $J$, the signed sum of the coefficients is precisely $\det(R_{J,:})$. The displayed identity therefore expresses one as an integer combination of the minors.

Conversely, if their gcd is one, the extended Euclidean algorithm, applied successively to this finite list, supplies the integers $c_J$. The cofactor expansion gives $A_J\operatorname{adj}(A_J)=\det(A_J)I_r$, including when $A_J$ is singular. Consequently (eq:construct) is integral and $AR=\sum_J c_J\det(A_J)I_r=I_r$. ∎

Proof[Proof of Theorem Theorem]: The columns of an integer right inverse give integer preimages of the standard basis of $\mathbb Z^m$. Conversely, choosing such preimages and putting them in columns gives an integer right inverse. This proves the equivalence of $1$ and $2$; Lemma Lemma (Standard maximal-minor criterion, with construction) proves $1$$\iff$$3$. To obtain (eq:weighted), a maximal minor selects $m-r$ diagonal columns, indexed by $T\subseteq[m]$, and $r$ columns of $X$, indexed by $J$. Expansion along the diagonal columns gives \[ \pm\left(\prod_{i\in T}d_i\right)\det X_{[m]\setminus T,J}. \] Conversely, every pair $I,J$ in (eq:weighted) arises in this way by taking $T=[m]\setminus I$. Signs do not affect the gcd.

Let $\delta$ be that gcd. The minor consisting of all diagonal columns is $P>0$, so $\delta>0$ and $\delta\mid P$. Thus $\delta=1$ if and only if no prime $p\mid P$ divides every maximal minor. Reduction modulo $p$ commutes with determinants, and a matrix over a field has row rank $m$ precisely when some maximal minor is nonzero. Hence $3$ is equivalent to $\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb F_p}(M\bmod p)=m$ for every $p\mid P$.

Fix such a prime. The diagonal columns of $M\bmod p$ span exactly the coordinate subspace \[ W_p=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb F_p}\{e_i:i\notin S_p\}. \] After quotienting $\mathbb F_p^m$ by $W_p$, the remaining column images are exactly the columns of $X_{S_p,:}\bmod p$. Therefore \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-72-61-6e-6b} \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb F_p}(M\bmod p) =m-|S_p|+\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb F_p}(X_{S_p,:}\bmod p). \end{equation} This can also be seen by subtracting multiples of the nonzero diagonal columns from each $X$ column, to zero all coordinates outside $S_p$. Equation (eq:rank) proves $3$$\iff$$4$. A prime power cannot divide $\delta=1$; excluding each prime divisor already suffices. ∎

Constructing a right inverse or an obstruction

Lemma Lemma (Standard maximal-minor criterion, with construction) is a finite algorithm: enumerate the maximal minors, compute their gcd and Bézout coefficients, and use (eq:construct). A smaller certificate follows directly from the prime tests. When (eq:local) holds, for each $p\mid P$ select a set $K_p$ of $|S_p|$ columns such that $\det(X_{S_p,K_p})\not\equiv0\pmod p$. Let $J_p$ select the diagonal columns outside $S_p$ and these columns of $X$, in their original order, and put $a_p=\det(M_{:,J_p})$. Then \[ a_p=\pm\left(\prod_{i\notin S_p}d_i\right)\det X_{S_p,K_p}, \qquad p\nmid a_p. \] It follows that $\gcd(P,(a_p)_{p\mid P})=1$: a prime divisor of this gcd would divide $P$ and would contradict its own selected $a_p$. Use these Bézout coefficients in (eq:construct), including the diagonal minor. This requires at most one plus the number of distinct prime divisors of $P$ selected minors. The minor chosen for one prime may differ from the one chosen for another.

If a prime test fails, Gaussian elimination supplies a nonzero vector $\lambda\in\mathbb F_p^{S_p}$ with $\lambda^{\mathsf T}X_{S_p,:}=0$. Extend it by zeros outside $S_p$ to $a\in\mathbb F_p^m$. Then $a^{\mathsf T}M=0$, while $a\ne0$; an integer identity $MY=I_m$ would reduce to $a^{\mathsf T}=a^{\mathsf T}MY=0$, a contradiction. This is a directly checkable failure certificate.

Existence does not require any single maximal minor to be $\pm1$. For example, take $m=n=2$ and \[ D=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix},\quad X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad M=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&1&0\\0&3&1&0\end{pmatrix}. \] In lexicographic column-set order the maximal minors are $6,2,0,-3,0,0$, with gcd one. Direct multiplication verifies \[ Y=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\-1&1\\3&-2\\0&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad MY=I_2. \]

The rowwise condition

Necessity follows by looking at the $(i,i)$ entry of $MY$: \[ 1=d_iY_{i,i}+\sum_{j=1}^n X_{ij}Y_{m+j,i}. \] Every common divisor of the indicated row entries must divide one. However, rowwise coprimality only ensures that each individual row in $S_p$ is nonzero modulo $p$; it does not ensure their independence.

An explicit counterexample meeting every original hypothesis is \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-63-6f-75-6e-74-65-72-65-78-61-6d-70-6c-65} m=n=2,\qquad D=2I_2,\qquad X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad M=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&1&0\\0&2&1&0\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation} Each row has gcd one. For any integer column $v=(a,b,c,e)^{\mathsf T}$, $Mv=(2a+c,2b+c)^{\mathsf T}$ has coordinates of the same parity. It cannot equal $e_1=(1,0)^{\mathsf T}$, so no right inverse exists. Equivalently, $S_2=\{1,2\}$ and $X_{S_2,:}$ has rank one modulo two; the maximal-minor gcd is two.

Corollary: Fix any original diagonal matrix $D$ and any $n\ge m$. The rowwise condition is sufficient for every $X\in\mathbb Z^{m\times n}$ if and only if the $d_i$ are pairwise coprime. In particular, for $m=1$ it is necessary and sufficient for every $n\ge1$.
Proof: Pairwise coprimality means $|S_p|\le1$ for every prime $p$. The rowwise condition makes this single row nonzero modulo $p$, so all the tests in Theorem Theorem pass. For $m=1$, this is exactly the ordinary Bézout condition.

If $p$ divides two diagonal entries, choose every row of $X$ equal to $(1,0,\ldots,0)$. All row gcds are one, but the rows indexed by $S_p$ have rank one, less than $|S_p|$. This proves the converse for every allowed $n$. It also gives failures in every dimension $m\ge2$, for example with $D=2I_m$. ∎

Noncoprime diagonal entries do not prevent existence for particular $X$: taking $X=[I_m\ 0]$ always gives the inverse with zero top block and bottom block $\begin{pmatrix}I_m\\0\end{pmatrix}$. On the other hand, $X=0$ never works under $d_i\ge2$. These cases, $m=n$, singular $X$, and arbitrary prime powers are all covered by the same theorem.

Smith normal form and all inverses

For comparison with the established literature, $\mathbb Z$ is a principal ideal domain and $M$ has rational row rank $m$ because $P\ne0$. The standard Smith theorem gives unimodular integer matrices $U\in\mathbb Z^{m\times m}$ and $V\in\mathbb Z^{(m+n)\times(m+n)}$ such that \[ UMV=[\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\ldots,s_m)\ 0], \qquad 1\le s_1\mid s_2\mid\cdots\mid s_m. \] Existence is Theorem 2.1$4$ of [stanley]; Theorem 2.4 states that $s_1\cdots s_m$ equals the gcd of the maximal minors. Their hypotheses apply over $\mathbb Z$, and all $s_i$ are nonzero by the rank observation. Thus the criterion is precisely $s_1=\cdots=s_m=1$ (equivalently $s_m=1$). Indeed, transforming any right inverse yields $[\operatorname{diag}(s_i)\ 0](V^{-1}YU^{-1})=I_m$, forcing $s_i=1$.

When the criterion holds, the complete set of integer right inverses is \begin{equation}\label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-61-6c-6c} Y=V\begin{pmatrix}I_m\\Z\end{pmatrix}U, \qquad Z\in\mathbb Z^{n\times m}\ \text{arbitrary}. \end{equation} To verify both directions, $MY=U^{-1}[I_m\ 0]\begin{pmatrix}I_m\\Z\end{pmatrix}U =I_m$, and any inverse makes the top $m$ rows of $V^{-1}YU^{-1}$ equal to $I_m$. This supplementary description uses Smith existence; the substantive prime criterion and adjugate construction above have an elementary proof independent of that theorem.

The dedicated standard-library checker scripts/check_integer_right_inverse_3030.py ran with Python 3.13.2 and seed 3030. It passed 21,020 exhaustive residue cases with $1\le m\le3$ and $m\le n\le3$, and 500 seeded cases with $1\le m\le5$, $m\le n\le m+2$, diagonal entries up to 49, and $X$ entries from $-8$ to $8$. It compared modular ranks and maximal-minor gcds; exhaustive cases and 231 small seeded cases also used direct generation of the finite group $\prod_i\mathbb Z/d_i\mathbb Z$. It checked $MY=I_m$ exactly for every accepted case, for both the full-minor and prime-selected constructions, and checked modular left-null vectors for every rejected case. It also passed 2,000 comparisons of Bareiss and permutation determinant computations, 300 Cauchy--Binet checks, and 500 residue-invariance checks under $X\mapsto X+DK$. These finite computations support the proof, not its universal validity.

Equation (eq:counterexample) disproves the proposed rowwise sufficiency, and Corollary Corollary identifies its uniform range of validity.
