Verbatim supplied catalogue question

For every instance of the ring-loading problem, let $L$ be the optimum unsplittable load, let $L^*$ be the optimum splittable load, and let $D$ be the maximum demand. Is $L\leq L^*+11D/10$?

Attempt and exact family

We try to round a fractional routing by one shared uniform threshold: a demand whose clockwise fraction is $p_i$ chooses clockwise precisely when $\theta<p_i$. This preserves each edge load in expectation. An expectation alone need not supply one outcome with small maximum load; the following family quantifies the failure even when the fractional routing is optimal.

Take a simple cycle with vertices $0,\ldots,2m-1$, $m\ge2$, and a unit demand between $i$ and $i+m$ for each $0\le i<m$. Clockwise paths have exactly $m$ edges, as do their complements. Every routing, split or unsplit, therefore has sum of edge loads $m^2$, so maximum load is at least $m/2$. Splitting every demand equally gives load $m/2$ on every edge: each demand contributes $1/2$ to each edge. Thus $L^*=m/2$ and $D=1$.

All clockwise paths contain the edge $\{m-1,m\}$, and all counterclockwise paths contain $\{2m-1,0\}$. With $p_i=1/2$ for every demand, a common threshold chooses one of these two unanimous routings, each with maximum load $m$. Its additive error is $m/2$, unbounded relative to $D$. Nevertheless every edge retains its correct expected load. This is a rounding-rule counterexample, not a counterexample to the original $11D/10$ conjecture.

The exact integral optimum.
For this family, \[ L=\begin{cases}(m+1)/2,&m\text{ odd},\\m/2+1,&m\text{ even}.\end{cases} \] For odd $m$, integrality of loads and the average bound give $L\ge\lceil m/2\rceil$. For even $m$, equality with the average would require all edge loads to equal $m/2$. At each vertex there is exactly one demand endpoint; crossing that vertex changes the load by $+1$ or $-1$ in an unsplit routing. Hence constant loads are impossible and $L\ge m/2+1$.

For the upper bound choose clockwise and counterclockwise directions alternately, starting clockwise at demand zero. Write $\sigma_i=+1$ for clockwise and $-1$ otherwise, $C=\lfloor m/2\rfloor$, and $S_j=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\sigma_i$, $1\le j\le m$. The edge immediately before vertex zero has load $C$. The first half of the cycle has loads $C+S_j$. Here $S_j=1$ for odd $j$ and zero for even $j$. For every edge, its antipodal edge is used by precisely the complementary set of demands, since each route is a semicircle. Its load is therefore $m-(C+S_j)$. If $m$ is odd, these loads are the two integers $(m-1)/2,(m+1)/2$. If $m$ is even they lie between $m/2-1$ and $m/2+1$. This proves the stated upper bounds and the formula. The genuine optimum has gap only $1/2$ or $1$, so it satisfies the conjectured $11/10$ bound.

Context, audit, and unresolved step

Skutella's original report, printed pages 2916--2917, distinguishes rounding an arbitrary split routing from comparing the two optimum objective values. That distinction is preserved here. The later Daubel abstract, arXiv:1904.02119v2, reports the stronger upper bound $13D/10$; the preserved catalogue discussion mentioning only $19D/14$ is therefore not used as a complete current survey. Neither that proof nor the general $11D/10$ target is proved in this manuscript.

The exact script combinatorial_checks.py enumerated all $2^m$ unsplit routings for each $m=2,\ldots,10$ and verified the formula and the common-threshold load; the all-$m$ argument is independent of those checks. The adversarial audit checked the even-$m$ strict-average argument, clockwise path indexing, and the antipodal complement identity. The failed proof step is replacing control of each expected edge load by simultaneous control of the maximum. A new correlated rounding construction with a universal additive bound is still required. No original-target progress or novelty is claimed.
