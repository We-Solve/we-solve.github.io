Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

A spatial polygon is a closed polygonal chain in $\mathbb R^3$ with positive edge lengths. Its unsigned turning angles are the angles between consecutive oriented edge directions, in $(0,\pi)$. An angle sequence is realizable if such a polygon exists. In the cited work, crossing counts refer to self-intersections of the polygonal chain, with generic realizations excluding coincident vertices, overlaps, and degenerate intersections; they are not crossings of an arbitrary planar projection.

Verbatim supplied catalogue question

Given a realizable angle sequence for a polygon in $\mathbb{R}^3$, can an efficient algorithm compute the minimum number of crossings among its generic realizations?

Source convention and the attempted route

The original report, printed pages 1512--1514, asks about actual self-intersections in space, not crossings of a planar projection or the crossing number of a knot. The primary polygon paper's introduction and concluding section were inspected as well; its authors are Efrat, Fulek, Kobourov and Tóth, as correctly recorded in the preserved definition, despite different names in the supplied row's link labels. Angles are unsigned turns $\alpha_i\in(0,\pi)$. We give a complete construction when their sum is $2\pi$, then encode the general fixed-crossing-pattern search and identify the missing efficient feasibility step. No general perturbation of vertices is assumed to preserve all angles.

A complete convex construction when the total turn is two pi

Suppose $\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\alpha_i=2\pi$, with all $0<\alpha_i<\pi$. Choose unit outward normals $v_i=(\cos\theta_i,\sin\theta_i)$ in cyclic order such that the angle from $v_{i-1}$ to $v_i$ is $\alpha_i$; cumulative sums of the angles construct them. Let $L_i$ be the line $v_i\cdot x=1$. The intersection $p_i=L_{i-1}\cap L_i$ is \[ p_i=\frac{1}{\cos(\alpha_i/2)} \left(\cos\frac{\theta_{i-1}+\theta_i}{2}, \sin\frac{\theta_{i-1}+\theta_i}{2}\right), \] with angles lifted cyclically for the last pair. This formula is verified by taking its dot product with each adjacent normal.

Every other normal lies outside the open circular arc of length $\alpha_i<\pi$ between those adjacent normals. Its circular distance from the midpoint is therefore at least $\alpha_i/2$, so its dot product with $p_i$ is at most one. Thus all $p_i$ belong to the convex intersection $K=\{x:v_j\cdot x\le1\text{ for all }j\}$. Equality holds only for the two adjacent normals, since other distinct normals have strictly larger circular distance from that midpoint.

On $L_i$, orient the unit tangent by rotating $v_i$ counterclockwise through $\pi/2$. In this tangent coordinate, $p_i$ and $p_{i+1}$ are respectively $-\tan(\alpha_i/2)$ and $\tan(\alpha_{i+1}/2)$ relative to the tangency point $v_i$. Hence the edge from $p_i$ to $p_{i+1}$ has positive length $\tan(\alpha_i/2)+\tan(\alpha_{i+1}/2)$ and the prescribed cyclic tangent direction. No two nonadjacent edges meet: a point on an edge lies on its supporting line, and the endpoints of every nonadjacent edge satisfy that line's inequality strictly, hence so does their entire connecting segment. Similarly the vertices are distinct. The angle between successive tangent directions is precisely $\alpha_i$. Therefore the constructed polygon is simple and realizes the sequence in the plane, and hence in $\mathbb R^3$. Its crossing count is zero, which is minimal because crossing counts are nonnegative. The construction uses $O(n)$ real arithmetic and trigonometric evaluations; this is not a bit-complexity statement for arbitrary real angles.

Encoding a specified crossing pattern exactly

For general angles, suppose additionally that the input supplies exact algebraic values $c_i=\cos\alpha_i\in(-1,1)$ with isolating data. This explicit representation is an auxiliary computational assumption. Introduce vertices $p_i\in\mathbb R^3$, positive lengths $l_i$, and unit edge directions $u_i$, with cyclic indices, and impose \[ l_i>0,\quad u_i\cdot u_i=1,\quad p_{i+1}-p_i=l_i u_i,\quad u_{i-1}\cdot u_i=c_i, \quad \|p_i-p_j\|^2>0\ (i\ne j). \] These polynomial equations and inequalities exactly impose closed positive-length edges, the angles in $(0,\pi)$, and distinct vertices. Fix a set $E$ of nonadjacent unordered edge pairs designated as crossings. For each pair in $E$, require parameters $s,t\in(0,1)$ with \[ p_i+s(p_{i+1}-p_i)=p_j+t(p_{j+1}-p_j), \quad\|(p_{i+1}-p_i)\mathbin{\times}(p_{j+1}-p_j)\|^2>0. \] Thus their interiors meet and the two lines are not parallel, so the intersection is one proper pairwise crossing. For every nonadjacent pair outside $E$, enforce strict separation of their closed segments by a plane: introduce $w\in\mathbb R^3,h\in\mathbb R$ satisfying \[ w\cdot p_i,w\cdot p_{i+1}<h, \qquad w\cdot p_j,w\cdot p_{j+1}>h. \] Here the comma notation means both indicated inequalities. Add the same strict-separation certificate between each vertex and every nonincident closed edge. Finally require crossing locations for distinct pairs in $E$ to be distinct, by a strictly positive squared distance. This last condition specifies the usual ordinary-double- crossing generic subcase explicitly; it excludes multiple crossings at one point rather than silently choosing how to count them.
Proof of the separation encoding.
Disjoint compact segments have closest points $a,b$ with $a\ne b$. Minimization over a segment gives $(a-b)\cdot(x-a)\ge0$ for every point $x$ on the first segment and $(b-a)\cdot(y-b)\ge0$ on the second, by differentiating squared distance along those line segments. Taking $w=b-a$ gives $w\cdot x\le w\cdot a<w\cdot b\le w\cdot y$. Choose $h$ strictly between the middle values to get the desired inequalities. Conversely those inequalities are preserved under convex combinations, so the segments cannot share a point. The same argument works for a point and a segment. This proves that no genuine disjoint configuration is lost by the introduced separation witnesses.

The entire system is existential and polynomial over the given algebraic coefficients, and is feasible exactly when that prescribed ordinary-double-crossing pattern has a realization. Necessity follows by the geometric constructions of the witnesses; sufficiency follows by reading the equations as the constructed polygon and the pairwise separation/crossing alternatives. Adjacent edges already meet only at their common vertex because their angle is strictly between zero and $\pi$. A nonadjacent straight edge pair can cross at most once.

Why this is not yet the requested efficient algorithm

One can list the at most $n(n-3)/2$ nonadjacent pairs and all candidate sets $E$ in increasing size. The feasibility encoding is explicit, but the list has exponential size in general, and an existential real-algebraic feasibility solver is not supplied with a polynomial runtime bound here. Even counting polynomially many variables and constraints for a fixed $E$ does not imply polynomial-time feasibility. We establish the exact encoding, not a new quantifier-elimination theorem or a polynomial algorithm. Also the source's generic convention must be checked if it permits coincident crossing locations; the encoding above explicitly handles ordinary double crossings.

The tempting alternative of perturbing every spatial polygon to remove all crossings has another gap: unconstrained perturbations change the dot products $u_{i-1}\cdot u_i$. The source paper itself discusses angle data forcing planar self-intersections. No proof is supplied that the angle-constrained realization space admits the required perturbation. The zero-crossing construction above uses the extra total-turn equality and cannot be extended by dropping that hypothesis.

Model-assisted audit and remaining scope

The audit checked normal indexing, positivity of every edge length, strict versus weak segment separation, vertex-edge incidences, and the representation of the angles. The primary paper's concluding conjecture uses $n\ge4$, whereas the workshop summary prints $n\ge3$; the triangle endpoint cannot be used to claim a negative solution to this minimum-crossing algorithm question. These are elementary restricted constructions and an encoding, with no novelty claim. No numerical experiment or theorem prover was used. The general efficient minimum-crossing algorithm remains unresolved.
