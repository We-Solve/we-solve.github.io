Second neighborhoods under uniform lexicographic substitution

Second neighborhoods under uniform lexicographic substitution

Problem: prb_e00f84a76def4b2ea4f7d56e06c4c804

Claim type: Partial result.

Exact scope: Prove closure of the second-neighborhood inequality under uniform lexicographic products; use Fisher’s credited tournament theorem and direct cycle/edgeless base cases to obtain explicit non-tournament families.

A closure statement with exact neighborhood sets

For a finite nonempty oriented graph G, call a vertex good if |N_G^{++}(v)|≥|N_G^+(v)|, with second neighborhood defined by shortest directed distance exactly two. Let G,H each have a good vertex, and put m=|V(H)|. Form the uniform lexicographic product D=G[H]: replace each vertex g by a copy of H, and include every arc from the fiber of g to the fiber of g′ whenever g→g′ is an arc of G. Within each fiber keep H’s arcs. This is an oriented graph, since neither factor has a loop or an opposite pair of arcs.

For any vertex (g,h), its first neighborhood consists of all m vertices in each fiber over N_G^+(g), plus N_H^+(h) in its own fiber. Its second neighborhood is exactly all m vertices in each fiber over N_G^{++}(g), plus N_H^{++}(h) in its own fiber. To verify the second assertion, consider a directed walk of length two. If it changes quotient fiber twice, its quotient is g→a→b. It cannot return to g because G has no opposite arcs. If b is already a one-step outneighbor of g, all of that fiber was reached in one step and is excluded. If it changes fiber only once, its endpoint again lies in a fiber already reached in one step. If it stays in the original fiber throughout, shortest distances are exactly those in H. Conversely every displayed vertex has the required two-step walk, and is neither the start nor a first neighbor.

Therefore

\[
|N_D^{++}(g,h)|-|N_D^+(g,h)|=
m\bigl(|N_G^{++}(g)|-|N_G^+(g)|\bigr)
+|N_H^{++}(h)|-|N_H^+(h)|.
\]

Choosing good g and good h makes the right side nonnegative. Thus the property “has a good vertex” is closed under this uniform substitution. No assumption that all vertices of either factor are good is needed.

Credited and elementary base cases

Fisher’s published 1996 theorem states that every tournament has a good vertex (equivalently, some outdegree at least doubles in the directed square). The publisher abstract was checked. Here that result is a credited input, not a new proof of the tournament case.

An edgeless nonempty graph has every vertex good, with both counts zero. Every consistently directed cycle C_ℓ with ℓ≥3 has exactly one first and one second neighbor at each vertex; the two-step endpoint is distinct from the start and first neighbor. Thus cycles provide a self-contained base family as well.

The product lemma proves the conjectured inequality for every iterated uniform lexicographic product with factors drawn from tournaments, directed cycles of lengths at least three, and nonempty edgeless graphs. For example T[C₄] is generally not a tournament, and C₄[C₅] supplies a further explicit non-tournament family. Arbitrary unequal-size substitutions are outside this formula: a block’s size then weights quotient neighbors nonuniformly, and the unweighted base inequality alone is insufficient.

Checks and relation to prior attempts

The checker directly verifies the good-vertex property for every labeled tournament through six vertices. It then checks the displayed exact neighborhood-set formulas for explicit products involving directed cycles, transitive and cyclic tournaments, and edgeless fibers, and confirms each chosen product witness. Distance-two sets are obtained by graph traversal and have first neighbors removed, avoiding the common “walks of length two” overcount.

The archived attempt-1 already proves the special case of independent clone fibers, corresponding to H edgeless. The present identity adds the internal first/second-neighborhood terms and allows nontrivial oriented fibers. The archived attempt-2 reports a broad 2026 announced general theorem; this report does not independently certify that announcement. Its added content is the fully proved uniform-product reduction and explicit non-tournament certificates. No claim that the general problem remains open is inferred from the older Fisher reference, and no full-solution claim is made here.

Second Neighborhood Problem

OpenAI's claim

Seymour second neighborhoods: The paper claims that every finite nonempty oriented graph has a vertex whose second out-neighborhood is at least as large as its first. The second neighborhood excludes vertices already reachable in one step.

Mathematical statement of the claimed result

Mathematical claim 1.
For a nonempty finite oriented graph $D$, with no loops or oppositely directed pair of edges, let $N_1^+(v)$ be the out-neighbors and $N_2^+(v)$ the vertices at directed distance exactly two. The claimed theorem gives \[\exists v\in V(D):\quad|N_1^+(v)|\le|N_2^+(v)|.\] For triangle-free $D$ a corollary yields a vertex with $d^+(v)\le|Z(v)|$ and $n-1\ge2d^+(v)+d^-(v)$, where $Z(v)$ consists of non-neighbors excluding $v$. Hence $\delta^+\ge n/3$ and $\delta^-\ge n/3$ force a directed triangle; a digraph regular in both directions with degree $r$ and girth four has $n\ge3r+1$. These consequences require the specified in-degree conditions and do not give the full out-degree-only Caccetta--Häggkvist assertion.

Outline of the argument

Seymour second neighborhoods: A smallest counterexample is pruned to yield strict neighborhood inequalities for all proper vertex sets. Replacing vertices by transitive tournaments strengthens these to a discrete concavity inequality. Extremal families of ordered pairs and an augmented bipartite matching are then controlled by two factorizations of one matrix, contradicting that inequality.

The Second Out-Neighbourhood Conjecture

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Unless a section says otherwise, ${\mathbb{N}}=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, ${\mathbb{Z}}$ is the ring of integers, ${\mathbb{Q}},{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}$ are the rational, real, and complex fields, $[N]=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor N\rfloor\}$, and $\log$ is the natural logarithm. Short English statement

Must some vertex in every finite oriented graph reach at least as many vertices for the first time in two steps as it reaches in one step?

Research attempt

Status and convention.
This is an unresolved attempt at Seymour's conjecture. In particular, $N^{++}(v)$ excludes both $v$ and $N^+(v)$, even when some out-neighbor also has a two-step walk from $v$. The calculation below proves the target when the minimum out-degree is at most two and for orientations of triangle-free graphs, and gives a general half-size bound. These are elementary subcases, not improvements on the literature. The June 2026 preprint of Sadhukhan, Sandeep and Sen reports the case of minimum out-degree seven, with part of its proof verified by CP-SAT; its abstract also records the previous threshold six. Those external results are distinguished from the local proofs and enumerations here. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30588
A local counting inequality.
Choose a minimum-out-degree vertex $v$, set $d=d^+(v)$, $A=N^+(v)$ and $B=N^{++}(v)$, and write $b=|B|$. If $d=0$, the target holds at $v$. Suppose $d>0$. Every out-neighbor of a vertex $u\in A$ lies in $A\cup B$. It cannot be $v$, because the digraph has no pair of opposite arcs; if it is outside $A\cup\{v\}$ it lies at distance exactly two from $v$. Summing the out-degrees of vertices of $A$ gives \[ d^2\leq\sum_{u\in A}d^+(u)=e(A)+e(A,B) \leq\binom d2+db. \] Here $e(A)$ counts arcs internal to $A$ once, and the last inequality uses at most one arc per unordered pair inside $A$, and at most one arc from each vertex of $A$ to each vertex of $B$. Thus \[ |N^{++}(v)|\geq\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{2}\right\rceil. \] For $d=1,2$ this lower bound is $d$, proving the conjecture in those cases. More precisely, retaining the actual internal arc count yields $b\geq\lceil d-e(A)/d\rceil$. That refinement identifies the loss in the argument: out-degree can be absorbed by arcs inside the first neighborhood instead of producing new second neighbors.
Triangle-free underlying graphs.
If the underlying undirected graph is triangle-free, there are no arcs between vertices of $A$: any such edge, together with their edges to $v$, would form a triangle. Fix $u\in A$. All $d^+(u)$ out-neighbors then lie in $B$, so $b\geq d^+(u)\geq d$. This proves the target directly, without regularity or a bound on the number of vertices. It also shows why bounding internal structure in $A$ can improve the half-size bound. For an arbitrary oriented graph, however, $A$ may itself be a tournament; no triangle-free conclusion is available.
Reduction to a terminal strongly connected component.
Contract the strongly connected components to obtain a finite acyclic digraph. It has a terminal component $C$ with no arc leaving it. For every $v\in C$, its first and second out-neighborhoods computed in the whole digraph are exactly those computed in the induced graph on $C$. Thus a good vertex of $C$ is good in the whole graph. A singleton terminal component is a sink and already settles the question. Consequently it would suffice to prove the conjecture for strongly connected oriented graphs. This is a valid reduction, but it does not bound their orders or degrees and is not itself a proof of the target.
Why a degree threshold alone cannot finish the problem.
Given an oriented graph $G$ and an integer $t\geq1$, replace every vertex by an independent set of $t$ clones and replace each arc $u\to v$ by all $t^2$ arcs from the $u$-clones to the $v$-clones. No loops or opposite arcs appear. For a clone $x$ of $v$, its first neighborhood is the union of the clone classes of $N^+(v)$, and its second neighborhood is exactly the union of those of $N^{++}(v)$. In particular, \[ d^+(x)=t d^+(v),\qquad |N^{++}(x)|=t|N^{++}(v)|. \] There is no new two-step route into $v$'s own clone class: such a route would require opposite arcs in $G$. A hypothetical counterexample would therefore produce counterexamples with arbitrarily large minimum out-degree by increasing $t$. This conditional construction is not a counterexample; it explains why a fixed verified degree threshold does not remove the general obstruction.
Exact finite check.
The companion script enumerates all labeled oriented graphs on one through five vertices. For each unordered pair it chooses no arc or one of the two directions, giving $3^{\binom n2}$ graphs at order $n$. It forms $N^{++}(v)$ as the union of the out-neighborhoods of $N^+(v)$, then removes $v$ and $N^+(v)$. It checks both the existence of a good vertex and the displayed bound at every minimum-out-degree vertex. This is a complete finite enumeration at those orders, including disconnected graphs, rather than a random search. It offers no uniform inference for larger orders.
Remaining obstruction.
The local estimate falls short when many arcs lie inside $A$ or when arcs from different vertices of $A$ repeatedly hit the same vertices of $B$. Summing out-degrees counts those repetitions, whereas the conjecture counts distinct endpoints. A full proof would need a rule for selecting a different vertex or exploiting these overlaps that forces $b\geq d$ somewhere. No such rule is established here, and the conjecture remains unresolved by this attempt.
