Kourovka Notebook Problem 2.25

Status Partially solved · unsolvedmath.comLLM status Open unverified

Top Open Problems Not reported cataloguedPartially solvedOpen

Statement

Definitions and mathematical statement

Let $G$ be a group. A strict total order on $G$ is an irreflexive, transitive relation $<$ such that, for distinct $x,y\in G$, exactly one of $x<y$ and $y<x$ holds. Such an order is left invariant if \[ \forall g,x,y\in G,\qquad x<y\Longrightarrow gx<gy, \] and two-sided invariant if \[ \forall a,b,x,y\in G,\qquad x<y\Longrightarrow axb<ayb. \] Write $\mathcal O_L(G)$ and $\mathcal O_B(G)$ for the sets of left-invariant and two-sided invariant strict total orders, respectively. Distinct orders are counted as distinct relations on the underlying group, without identifying them under automorphisms.

The supplied record does not define the compatibility intended by "linearly orderable." Consequently the following formulations are conditional on that convention: take $\sigma=B$ if two-sided invariance is intended, and $\sigma=L$ if left invariance is intended. These are alternative readings, not two separately attributed problems.

  1. (a) (Attributed to L. Fuchs.) Describe, up to group isomorphism, all groups $G$ such that \[ 0<\lvert\mathcal O_\sigma(G)\rvert<\aleph_0. \] The positive lower bound expresses the requirement that the group be linearly orderable.
  2. (b) (Supplied archived solved part.) Does there exist a group $G$ such that \[ \lvert\mathcal O_\sigma(G)\rvert=\aleph_0? \] This uses "countably many" to mean countably infinitely many. If the source instead means at most countably many compatible orders, the corresponding condition for an orderable group is $0<\lvert\mathcal O_\sigma(G)\rvert\leq\aleph_0$. The supplied excerpt does not explicitly distinguish these meanings.

The record labels the overall problem partially solved, retains part (a) in the main section, and reports an affirmative answer to part (b), attributed to R. N. Buttsworth. This is a report of the supplied archive assessment; it does not verify that answer or resolve the ordering convention.

Short English statement

Part (a), attributed to L. Fuchs, asks for a classification of groups admitting a positive finite number of compatible linear orders. Part (b) asks whether a group can admit countably infinitely many such orders; the supplied archive reports an affirmative answer. The intended invariance of the orders and the precise meaning of "countably many" require source clarification.
