Lebesgue measure of the boundary of the Mandelbrot set

Status OpenLLM status Open unverified

Top Open Problems openaigpt 6 astra ultra Not reported cataloguedOpenOpen

Statement

Definitions and mathematical statement

For $c\in{\mathbb{C}}$, let $f_c(z)=z^2+c$ and define the Mandelbrot set by \[ \mathcal M=\{c:\sup_{n\ge0}|f_c^{\circ n}(0)|<\infty\}. \] Its boundary $\partial\mathcal M$ is taken in the Euclidean topology of ${\mathbb{C}}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^2$. Determine whether its two-dimensional Lebesgue measure satisfies $\operatorname{Leb}_2(\partial\mathcal M)=0$ or is positive. The quantity is planar area, not Hausdorff dimension, and refers only to the boundary rather than the whole Mandelbrot set, whose interior contributes area. Establishing high fractal dimension or finding complicated boundary geometry does not by itself distinguish zero from positive area.

Formulation and concept references: [S1].

Short English statement

Does the boundary of the Mandelbrot set occupy zero planar area, or does it have positive area?

Research attempt

Process and exact question.
This attempt was generated by GPT-6 Astra Ultra on 27 September 2026. We construct rigorous outer approximations to the Mandelbrot set, exhibit an open region inside it, and isolate an area estimate that would imply a zero-area boundary. We then test two tempting shortcuts: passing zero boundary area through nested limits and replacing Lebesgue area by Hausdorff dimension. Both fail as general arguments.

The introduction of [S1] asks the boundary-area question explicitly in parameter space. Its results about rational-map families and bifurcation measures must be read with their stated parameter spaces and hypotheses. They do not provide an answer for the planar Lebesgue measure of the boundary in this catalogue problem. In particular, a statement about the Julia set of one polynomial concerns a subset of the dynamical $z$-plane, whereas this boundary is a subset of the parameter $c$-plane.

An escape criterion with no numerical approximation.
Write $P_0(c)=0$ and $P_{n+1}(c)=P_n(c)^2+c$. If $|c|>2$, then $|P_1(c)|=|c|$, and whenever $|z|\ge|c|$, \[ |z^2+c|\ge|z|^2-|c|\ge |z|(|c|-1)>|z|. \] The critical orbit therefore grows at least geometrically and escapes. If $|c|\le2$ and an orbit value has modulus $r>2$, then its next modulus is at least $r^2-2>r$. Subsequent growth is again geometric, since the ratio is bounded below by $r-2/r>1$ at that first value and increases afterward. Thus \[ M=\{c:|c|\le2,\ |P_n(c)|\le2\text{ for every }n\ge1\}. \tag{420.1} \] The converse is immediate from boundedness by $2$. This also proves compactness, since the set is closed and lies in the closed disk of radius $2$.

Define \[ K_N=\{c:|c|\le2,\ |P_j(c)|\le2\text{ for }1\le j\le N\}. \tag{420.2} \] The sets are compact and nested, and $\bigcap_NK_N=M$. For $|c|\le2$, escape past radius $2$ is irreversible, so one could equivalently keep only the last iterate inequality along with $|c|\le2$. The cumulative version makes nestedness explicit.

Since all $K_N$ have finite planar measure, continuity from above gives \[ \operatorname{area}(K_N)\downarrow\operatorname{area}(M). \tag{420.3} \] This is a convergence theorem for the total set area. It does not yet involve the area of the boundary.

Every finite approximant has a zero-area boundary.
For $n\ge1$, $P_n$ is a nonconstant polynomial with degree $2^{n-1}$. The set $\{|P_n(c)|=2\}$ is the zero set of a nonzero real polynomial in $\operatorname{Re}c,\operatorname{Im}c$. Such a zero set has planar measure zero. To justify the last assertion, view a nonzero bivariate polynomial as a polynomial in the second variable whose coefficients are polynomials in the first. Except at finitely many first-coordinate values where all coefficients vanish, each vertical line meets its zero set in finitely many points. Fubini's theorem then gives area zero.

The boundary of $K_N$ is contained in the finite union of these level sets and $\{|c|=2\}$. A point where all its defining inequalities are strict has a neighborhood in $K_N$, so cannot be a boundary point. Consequently \[ \operatorname{area}(\partial K_N)=0\quad\hbox{for every finite }N. \tag{420.4} \] The inference from $420.4$ to zero area of $\partial M$ is nevertheless invalid. Boundaries do not in general commute with decreasing intersections, and boundary measure has no continuity-from-above principle of the form used in $420.3$.

A direct countertest for that limit argument.
Construct a closed nowhere dense subset $C\subset[0,1]$ of measure $1/2$ by repeatedly deleting middle open intervals: at stage $n$, delete from each of the $2^{n-1}$ retained intervals a middle interval of length $4^{-n}$. The total deleted length is \[ \sum_{n\ge1}2^{n-1}4^{-n}=\frac12. \] At every stage there are finitely many retained closed intervals; their maximum lengths tend to zero because each successor has length less than half its parent's. Hence the limiting set has empty interior. Let $C_N$ denote the stage-$N$ set and put $F_N=C_N\times[0,1]$.

Each $F_N$ is a finite union of closed rectangles, so its boundary has area zero. The sets decrease to $F=C\times[0,1]$, which has empty interior and area $1/2$. Being closed, it satisfies $\partial F=F$, and therefore \[ \operatorname{area}(\partial F)=1/2, \qquad \operatorname{area}(\partial F_N)=0\quad\hbox{for all }N. \tag{420.5} \] This is not a model for Mandelbrot dynamics. It is a rigorous counterexample to the proposed set-theoretic step, using the same nested compactness and finite-boundary-area properties. Additional dynamical information is indispensable.

An explicit interior region and the correct area identity.
For $|c|<1/4$, set \[ r=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4|c|}}2<\frac12. \] Then $r^2+|c|=r$, so $z\mapsto z^2+c$ maps the closed disk $|z|\le r$ to itself. Starting from zero, the critical orbit remains in this disk. Moreover the parameter condition $|c|<1/4$ is open, so this entire parameter disk belongs to $\operatorname{int}M$. In particular $\operatorname{area}(M)\ge\pi/16$.

This positive total area gives no positive lower bound for boundary area. For a closed measurable set of finite area, the disjoint decomposition into its interior and its boundary gives exactly \[ \operatorname{area}(\partial M) =\operatorname{area}(M)-\operatorname{area}(\operatorname{int}M). \tag{420.6} \] Thus $420.3$ addresses only one of the two terms. The explicitly constructed disk addresses only part of the second. Neither calculation determines their difference.

A sufficient certificate scheme and its missing estimate.
One can rigorously certify other interior parameter regions using trapping neighborhoods. For example, let $D$ be a closed disk in the dynamical plane and let $p\ge1$. If, uniformly for parameters in a small closed parameter box, $f_c^p(D)$ lies strictly inside $D$ and a fixed iterate of the critical point enters its interior, every such critical orbit remains bounded. Strict inequalities persist in a slightly larger parameter neighborhood by continuity, so the parameter box lies in $\operatorname{int}M$.

Let $H_j$ be an increasing sequence of finite unions of such certified boxes, also including any previously certified interior regions. We have $H_j\subset\operatorname{int}M\subset M\subset K_N$ and hence \[ \operatorname{area}(\partial M) \le\operatorname{area}(K_N\setminus H_j) =\operatorname{area}(K_N)-\operatorname{area}(H_j). \tag{420.7} \] A proof that these gaps tend to zero along some sequence of pairs $(N,j)$ would prove zero boundary area. The present attempt has no such bound. Nor is it shown that trapping certificates exhaust every interior component. A positive residual gap in a computation could contain boundary, unrecognized interior, or both. It is therefore not a certificate of positive boundary area either.

Finite images that classify a pixel by one sampled parameter introduce another issue: nearby parameters can have different escape behavior. A certified box method must enclose the whole box through the relevant polynomial inequalities. Even if this is done at every finite stage, the limiting residual estimate in $420.7$ remains a separate mathematical problem.

Dimension does not substitute for area.
A planar set can have Hausdorff dimension two and still have area zero. To see the distinction concretely, fix binary digits at square-numbered positions to zero and leave the other positions free, obtaining a null subset $E\subset[0,1]$. The natural fair-digit measure assigns a depth-$n$ allowed cylinder mass $2^{-n+\lfloor\sqrt n\rfloor}$, which is at most $C_s2^{-sn}$ for each $s<1$. The resulting interval mass bound proves $\dim_H E=1$. Its product with Lebesgue measure on $[0,1]$ has ball masses bounded by $C_sr^{s+1}$, so $E\times[0,1]$ has dimension two, but Fubini gives area zero. The positive-area set in $420.5$ also has dimension two. Thus even a full-dimension statement cannot choose between the two area outcomes asked here.
Verification and outcome.
Exact checks verify the first iterate polynomials, their degrees, the escape inequalities on the stated ranges, and the retained-length formula in the countertest. No finite orbit sample is reported as a boundary-area computation. The compactness, measure limits, and counterexample are justified by the arguments above.

UNRESOLVED. We obtain certified outer approximations, an interior disk, and the sufficient area-gap criterion $420.7$. No vanishing estimate for that gap or positive lower bound for boundary area is proved, so the catalogue's alternative remains undecided.
