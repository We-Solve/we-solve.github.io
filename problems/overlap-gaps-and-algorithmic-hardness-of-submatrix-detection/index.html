Verbatim supplied catalogue question

In the Gaussian $n\times n$ submatrix problem, suppose near-optimal solutions above a threshold $\alpha^*$ exhibit an overlap gap: their pairwise overlaps avoid a nonempty interval. Does this property imply that no polynomial-time algorithm can find a $k\times k$ submatrix of average at least $(1+o(1))\alpha\sqrt{2\log n/k}$ when $\alpha>\alpha^*$ and $k\to\infty$?

The primary two-coordinate overlap model

Gamarnik's report, printed pages 1211--1213, uses an $n\times n$ matrix of independent standard Gaussian entries. A choice consists of $k$ rows and $k$ columns, without a principal-submatrix restriction. For two choices define $x$ and $y$ as their shared-row and shared-column counts divided by $k$. The report analyzes a two-dimensional overlap region; the catalogue's one-interval summary must not replace this geometry. Its numerical threshold is $\alpha_* =5\sqrt2/(3\sqrt3)$, so $\alpha_*^2=50/27$. We rederive a sufficient forbidden strip by a complete first moment and test the proposed inference from such a strip to arbitrary polynomial-time hardness. No novelty is claimed for this overlap mechanism.

A complete forbidden-strip calculation

Fix $\alpha>\alpha_*$, $1\le k\le n$, $n\ge2$, and call a submatrix good if its average is at least $t=\alpha\sqrt{2\log n/k}$. Its average $U$ has variance $1/k^2$. For two choices sharing $r$ rows and $s$ columns, their averages have covariance $rs/k^4=xy/k^2$, where $x=r/k,y=s/k$. This follows by expanding both averages: exactly $rs$ entries occur in both, independent distinct entries have covariance zero, and each shared standard Gaussian has variance one.

A centered Gaussian $G$ of variance $v$ has moment-generating function $\mathbb E e^{\lambda G}=e^{v\lambda^2/2}$, by completing the square in its density integral (for $v=0$ the identity is immediate). Markov's inequality and the choice $\lambda=a/v$ give $\Pr(G\ge a)\le e^{-a^2/(2v)}$ for $a>0,v>0$. Applying this to the sum of the two averages yields \[ \Pr(U\ge t,V\ge t) \le\Pr(U+V\ge2t) \le\exp\!\left(-\frac{k^2t^2}{1+xy}\right) =n^{-2\alpha^2k/(1+xy)}. \]

The exact number of ordered pairs with these intersections is \[ \binom nk^2\binom kr\binom{n-k}{k-r} \binom ks\binom{n-k}{k-s}. \] The formula chooses the first row and column sets, then shared and new elements of the second; impossible binomial coefficients mean zero. Using $\binom nk\le n^k$, $\binom kr\le2^k$, and $\binom{n-k}{k-r}\le n^{k-r}$ bounds it by $4^k n^{4k-r-s}$. Hence the expected number of good ordered pairs with overlaps $(x,y)$ is at most \[ \exp\{k\log4+k\log n\,h(x,y)\},\qquad h(x,y)=4-x-y-\frac{2\alpha^2}{1+xy}. \]

Put $\kappa=2\alpha^2-100/27>0$ and $\eta=\min\{1/3,\kappa/2\}$. For $u=x+y$, the elementary square $(x-y)^2\ge0$ gives $xy\le u^2/4$. Therefore \[ (4-x-y)(1+xy)\le H(u):=(4-u)(1+u^2/4). \] On $0\le u\le2$, $H'(u)=-1+2u-3u^2/4$ lies between $-1$ and $1/3$, as is seen by completing the square. Thus $|H'(u)|\le1$, and $H(2/3)=100/27$. Whenever $|x+y-2/3|\le\eta$ we obtain \[ (4-x-y)(1+xy)\le100/27+\eta \le2\alpha^2-\kappa/2, \qquad h(x,y)\le-\kappa/4, \] where the last inequality uses $1+xy\le2$. Summing over the at most $(k+1)^2$ possible intersection-count pairs, and using Markov's inequality for their total count, bounds the probability of any good pair in this strip by \[ (k+1)^2\exp\{k\log4-\kappa k\log n/4\}\longrightarrow0. \] The convergence holds uniformly for $1\le k\le n$: since $\log(k+1)\le k$, the logarithm is at most $k(2+\log4-\kappa\log n/4)$, which tends to minus infinity. This proves a forbidden overlap strip with high probability. It does not assert that good choices exist on both sides, or even that any good choice exists, in every scaling of $k$.

Exactly what the barrier says about a local path

On a realization having that forbidden strip, fix a good reference choice $A$. Consider a sequence of good choices in which each step replaces one row or one column. The overlap sum $x+y$ with $A$ changes by at most $1/k$ per step, because only one intersection count changes and it changes by at most one. If $1/k<2\eta$, such a sequence cannot begin with overlap sum above $2/3+\eta$ and end below $2/3-\eta$: at the first crossing it must either hit the forbidden interval or jump by more than $2\eta$. The former contradicts goodness and the latter contradicts the step bound.

This is a rigorous obstruction for a specified small-step path that stays entirely above the target value. A polynomial-time algorithm need not produce such a path. It may inspect many low-value choices, make large changes, or output a good choice directly. Even a local method can leave the good set temporarily. The missing inference is a theorem forcing every allowed algorithm into the restricted path model; polynomial runtime alone gives no such continuity condition.

An easy matrix family with a genuine overlap gap

To make that logical gap concrete, let $n=2k$, $k\ge2$, split rows and columns into their first and last $k$ positions, and set entries to one in the two matching diagonal blocks and to $-k$ off those blocks. If a choice uses $a$ first-block rows and $b$ first-block columns, the number of off-block entries is $c=a(k-b)+(k-a)b$; its average is $1-(k+1)c/k^2$. For fixed $a$, the expression $c=ka+(k-2a)b$ is affine in $0\le b\le k$, so its minimum is $k\min(a,k-a)$. If $0<a<k$, then $c\ge k$. If $a=0$ or $k$, any choice other than the corresponding matching whole block also has $c\ge k$. Thus precisely the two matching blocks have average above $1/2$: they have value one and all other choices have value at most $-1/k$. Their pairwise overlap vectors are $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$, with an unambiguous gap, yet the first matching block is found immediately from its labels. Entrywise perturbations of magnitude at most $1/4$ preserve this conclusion, since any average changes by at most $1/4$.

This deterministic family is not drawn from the source Gaussian law and does not refute its average-case conjecture. It only refutes the distribution-free inference that overlap separation by itself establishes algorithmic hardness. Under the Gaussian law, the relevant gap-to-hardness step still needs a further algorithmic argument.
