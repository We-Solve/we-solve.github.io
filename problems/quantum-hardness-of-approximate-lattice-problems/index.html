Why a small coefficient box does not solve the lattice problem

The source asks about quantum hardness of lattice problems in the context of learning with errors and reductions. We test a basic proposed search route: enumerate short integer coefficient vectors in the supplied basis and use them to find linearly independent short lattice vectors. This is a route for the independent-vectors task, not a quantum lower bound or a shortest-vector impossibility theorem.
Proposition: For the basis $b_1=(1,0)$ and $b_2=(M,1)$ with integer $M>0$, the lattice is $\mathbb Z^2$ and its second successive minimum in Euclidean norm is 1. If coefficients satisfy $|z_1|,|z_2|\le B<M$, any independent pair of enumerated vectors contains a vector of norm at least $M-B$.
Proof: The supplied vectors generate $(1,0)$ and $b_2-Mb_1=(0,1)$, so they generate exactly $\mathbb Z^2$. Its nonzero vectors have norm at least 1, and these two unit vectors are independent, proving that the second successive minimum is 1. An enumerated vector is $(z_1+Mz_2,z_2)$. If $z_2\ne0$, its first coordinate has absolute value at least $M|z_2|-|z_1|\ge M-B$, so its norm is at least that large. Any independent pair must include such a vector, because all vectors with $z_2=0$ lie on one line. ∎
Taking $M=2^b$ uses only $O(b)$ input bits. Every fixed polynomial coefficient cutoff $B(b)$ is eventually much smaller than $M$, so this box search can miss every short independent pair even in dimension two. However the elementary basis replacement $b_2\leftarrow b_2-Mb_1$ immediately repairs this example, with a coefficient representable in $O(b)$ bits. Consequently the example obstructs the box restriction itself and supplies no hardness result for basis reduction, classical algorithms or quantum algorithms. The shortest input vector already is a shortest lattice vector, another reason not to claim a shortest-vector lower bound from this example.

Literature qualification and verification

Recent primary preprints illustrate why current claims need care: ePrint 2026/1591 claims quantum lattice consequences via a dihedral-coset algorithm, while ePrint 2026/1693 presents a contrary analysis of that proposed procedure. Their abstracts and revision information were inspected; the competing proofs and formalization were not independently checked here. This disagreement is not used as a theorem, an established resolution, or a conclusion about every quantum algorithm. For $M=1024$ and $B=10$, exhaustive coefficient enumeration confirms that every enumerated vector off the horizontal axis has norm at least $\sqrt{1014^2+1}$, while $(0,1)$ requires coefficients $(-1024,1)$. The intended lattice problem, approximation regime and reduction still matter; no general quantum upper or lower bound is obtained. The failed step is equating coordinate size in an arbitrary basis with geometric shortness.
