Problem/

What the displayed approximations do not provide

The source gives heuristic-looking approximate expressions without a uniform error estimate, a rule for multiple zeros, or a cost for computing the zeros and derivatives. We do not assert either printed asymptotic, its sign or its error term as a proved theorem here. Instead we test whether the proposed arithmetic information would be sufficient if it could be obtained with a specified error, then locate the remaining computational gap.
A certified von Mangoldt approximation would suffice.
Suppose an oracle outputs a real $A(n)$ with $|A(n)-\Lambda(n)|<\log2/3$ for every integer $n>1$. By definition, $\Lambda(n)$ is zero unless $n$ is a prime power, and otherwise is at least $\log2$. Comparing $A(n)$ with $\log2/2$ therefore separates prime powers from other integers. One must still reject proper powers to get primality. This can be done deterministically by checking exponents $2\le k\le\lfloor\log_2n\rfloor$: for each $k$ binary-search the integer $k$th root and compare its exact $k$th power with $n$. Every proper power has such an exponent, and all searches and repeated multiplications have polynomially many operations on $O(\log n)$-bit integers (intermediate powers may be stopped when they exceed $n$). A prime power that is not a proper power is prime. Thus a sufficiently accurate oracle is enough, but the zero-sum expression has not been shown to supply that oracle.
Semiprime information and a rigorous rounding threshold.
Let $n=pq$ with distinct primes $p<q$. Put $L=\log n$, $S=(\log p)(\log q)$ and $\Delta=\log(q/p)>0$. The numbers $\log p,\log q$ are exactly the roots of $z^2-Lz+S$, so \[ \log p=\frac{L-\sqrt{L^2-4S}}2. \] If $\widehat S=S+e$, $|e|\le\varepsilon\le\Delta^2/8$, the new discriminant is at least $\Delta^2/2>0$. Rationalizing the square-root difference gives \[ \left|\frac{L-\sqrt{L^2-4\widehat S}}2-\log p\right| =\frac{2|e|}{\sqrt{\Delta^2-4e}+\Delta} \le\frac{2\varepsilon}{\Delta}. \] If also $\varepsilon\le\Delta/(16p)$, this logarithmic error is at most $1/(8p)$. For $|u|\le1/2$, $|e^u-1|\le2|u|$: the positive exponential series is bounded by $1/(1-|u|)$, and the negative case is smaller. Exponentiating the approximated smaller root therefore gives an absolute error at most $1/4$ in $p$, so rounding to the nearest integer recovers it. The equal-prime case is detected separately by an integer square-root check.

This conditioning bound does not require exponentially many precision bits. Since $\Delta=\int_p^q dt/t\ge(q-p)/q\ge1/n$ and $p\le n$, $\varepsilon=1/(32n^2)$ satisfies both sufficient inequalities. It has $O(\log n)$ bits of precision. The argument treats $L$ as its exact mathematical value; an implementation must also bound logarithm, square-root and exponential evaluation errors inside the rounding margin. The real unresolved issue is obtaining the arithmetic statistic with such a certificate and cost, not merely writing the quadratic formula.

How errors in zero locations enter the unweighted sum.
For a proposed zero $\rho$ and perturbation $\delta$ with $|\delta|\le\eta$ and $\eta\log n\le1/2$, \[ |n^{\rho+\delta}-n^\rho| \le n^{\operatorname{Re}\rho}(e^{\eta\log n}-1) \le2n^{\operatorname{Re}\rho}\eta\log n. \] The first inequality follows from $n^z=\exp(z\log n)$ and the absolute exponential series; the second is the preceding bound. For $M$ terms with real parts at most one, the total location error is at most $2Mn\eta\log n$. This controls only approximated included terms, not omitted zeros, an incorrect window endpoint, multiplicities, or the analytic remainder relating the sum to an arithmetic function. The weighted expression has an additional denominator issue: a simple zero of a function does not imply its second derivative there is nonzero; $f(z)=z+z^3$ at zero is an elementary example. This does not assert that a zeta zero has that behavior. It shows that simplicity alone cannot justify division by $\zeta''(\rho)$ or a numerical lower bound for that denominator.

Audit and remaining target

The primary Question 8 was inspected. The audit checked prime-power ambiguity, closely spaced prime factors, the double-root case, and the distinction between precision bits and the work needed to compute a long zero sum. No zeta zeros, derivatives or numerical factors were computed. A uniform effective remainder, reliable denominator treatment and certified polynomial-time evaluation remain missing for both proposals. The conditional information calculations are elementary known-type results, not a zeta-based primality or factoring algorithm.
