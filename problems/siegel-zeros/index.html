Definitions and mathematical statement

A primitive quadratic character $\chi$ has minimal modulus, its conductor $q$, and values in $\{0,\pm1\}$. Using its Dirichlet $L$-function, the uniform zero-free assertion is \[ \exists\delta>0\ \forall q\ge3\ \forall\chi\text{ primitive quadratic of conductor }q, \quad L(\beta,\chi)\ne0\quad\left(1-\delta/\log q<\beta<1\right). \] The same $\delta$ must work for all conductors, and only real $\beta$ are quantified here. A separate positive zero-free radius for each individual character would not establish the target.

Formulation and concept references: [S1].

Short English statement

Can one uniformly exclude exceptionally close real zeros of quadratic Dirichlet $L$-functions near one?

Definitions and mathematical statement

A primitive quadratic character $\chi$ has minimal modulus, its conductor $q$, and values in $\{0,\pm1\}$. Using its Dirichlet $L$-function, the uniform zero-free assertion is \[ \exists\delta>0\ \forall q\ge3\ \forall\chi\text{ primitive quadratic of conductor }q, \quad L(\beta,\chi)\ne0\quad\left(1-\delta/\log q<\beta<1\right). \] The same $\delta$ must work for all conductors, and only real $\beta$ are quantified here. A separate positive zero-free radius for each individual character would not establish the target.

Formulation and concept references: [S1].

Short English statement

Can one uniformly exclude exceptionally close real zeros of quadratic Dirichlet $L$-functions near one?

Research attempt

Current frontier.
Lu--Zaman--Zhao report a rigorous computational exclusion of real zeros in $\sigma\ge1-1/(5\log q)$ for quadratic characters with $q\le10^{10}$ [S1], Theorem 1.1. Their verified finite range is not an all-conductor theorem. Their algorithm's asymptotic termination analysis uses GRH, separately from the finite verifications. The established zero-free-region theorem allows at most one exceptional real zero rather than excluding that zero. The distinction between lower bounds for $L(1,\chi)$ and a zero-free interval of width $1/\log q$ is analyzed by Friedlander--Iwaniec [E1], Sections 1--2. These are the relevant inputs; a general proof claim retained in [S2] is not used as a theorem.
Attack route.
Three possible routes are: control the variation of $L$ relative to its value at one; exploit positivity of the coefficients of $\zeta L$; or obtain a uniform lower bound for the positive prime sums used by the numerical exclusion method. The first requires an estimate proportional to $L(1,\chi)$, not just an absolute derivative estimate. The second must continue through a pole without illegally continuing a positive series. The third requires uniform information on splitting primes in a range where exceptional zeros are precisely an obstruction. We carry out the first route explicitly and test the positivity shortcut against a model.
Attempt: an unconditional derivative calculation.
Let $q\ge3$, $\ell=\log q>1$, and let $\chi$ be primitive quadratic. It is nonprincipal. Set $A(t)=\sum_{n\le t}\chi(n)$. Periodicity and the zero sum of a nonprincipal character over one full period give $A(q)=0$ and $|A(t)|\le q$. Partial summation yields, for real $s>0$, \[ L(s,\chi)=\sum_{n\le q}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^s} +s\int_q^\infty A(t)t^{-s-1}\,dt. \tag{43.1} \] The integral converges absolutely and agrees with the continuation from $s>1$. On any compact subset of $s>0$, its derivative is dominated by an integrable multiple of $(1+\log t)t^{-1-\sigma_0}$, where $\sigma_0>0$. Differentiation is therefore justified, giving \begin{align*} L'(s,\chi)={}&-\sum_{n\le q}\frac{\chi(n)\log n}{n^s} +\int_q^\infty A(t)t^{-s-1}\,dt\\ &-s\int_q^\infty A(t)(\log t)t^{-s-1}\,dt. \tag{43.2} \end{align*} Take $1-1/(4\ell)\le s\le1$. Then $s\ge3/4$ and $q^{1-s}\le e^{1/4}<2$. The finite sum has absolute value at most \[ e^{1/4}\sum_{n\le q}\frac{\log n}{n} \le e^{1/4}\ell(1+\ell)<4\ell^2. \] The two integral terms in $43.2$ together have absolute value at most \[ q^{1-s}\left(\frac1s+\log q+\frac1s\right) \le2\left(\ell+\frac83\right)<8\ell^2. \] In particular the deliberately nonoptimal bound \[ \sup_{1-1/(4\ell)\le s\le1}|L'(s,\chi)|\le16\ell^2 \tag{43.3} \] is uniform in $q$ and $\chi$. The order of this estimate is familiar from [E1], equation $1.4$; the calculation above supplies an elementary proof, not an improvement on that literature.

If a real zero $\beta$ lies in this interval, the fundamental theorem of calculus and the classical fact $L(1,\chi)>0$ imply \[ 0<L(1,\chi)=\int_\beta^1 L'(s,\chi)\,ds \le16(1-\beta)\ell^2. \tag{43.4} \] Here positivity at one is the usual nonvanishing and positivity theorem for real nonprincipal Dirichlet characters; it is also implicit in the special-value bounds discussed in [S1] and [E1]. Thus even a hypothetical uniform estimate $L(1,\chi)\ge c/\ell$ would yield from this route only $1-\beta\ge c/(16\ell^3)$ inside the working interval. That is two powers of $\log q$ short of the required scale. Increasing the finite verification range does not repair this asymptotic loss.

Attempt: a relative-variation criterion.
Here is a sufficient hypothesis that identifies the missing cancellation without assuming a value for $L(1,\chi)$: \[ \exists C>0\ \ \forall\chi,q:\quad \sup_{1-1/(4\log q)\le s\le1}|L'(s,\chi)| \le C(\log q)L(1,\chi). \tag{43.5} \] If $43.5$ holds, the target follows with $\delta=\min\{1/4,1/(2C)\}$. Indeed, a zero with $0<1-\beta<\delta/\log q$ would give \[ L(1,\chi)\le C(1-\beta)(\log q)L(1,\chi) <\tfrac12 L(1,\chi), \] a contradiction. This is a complete proof of the conditional implication, not a proof of $43.5$. A one-sided integral estimate could be enough; absolute control over the whole interval is stronger than what the contradiction uses. In particular $43.5$ should not be described as an established equivalence or as an independently easier conjecture.

To seek $43.5$, separate the principal part of $43.2$ into sign classes. Its finite sum is \[ -\sum_{\substack{n\le q\\\chi(n)=1}}\frac{\log n}{n^s} +\sum_{\substack{n\le q\\\chi(n)=-1}}\frac{\log n}{n^s}. \] Bounding both sums separately destroys the cancellation that $43.5$ requires when $L(1,\chi)$ is small. Character periodicity supplies $43.3$ but does not relate this cancellation to the small special value. Friedlander--Iwaniec's more refined relation to a smoothed positive coefficient sum explains why that link is substantive [E1], Proposition 2.1. No such uniform relative estimate has been proved in this attempt.

Attempt: testing positivity across the pole.
For $s>1$ the product has the absolutely convergent expansion \[ Z(s)=\zeta(s)L(s,\chi)=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{a(n)}{n^s}, \qquad a(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\chi(d)\ge0. \tag{43.6} \] The last assertion follows prime by prime. If $\chi(p)=1$, then $a(p^j)=j+1$; if $\chi(p)=-1$, it equals one for even $j$ and zero for odd $j$; if $\chi(p)=0$, it equals one. Multiplicativity proves the claim, including $a(m^2)\ge1$. Hence $Z(s)>0$ for $s>1$ and its alternating real derivatives have the signs supplied by a positive Laplace transform. This calculation is checked on small conductors in the accompanying exact script.

An attempted continuation of these signs to $s<1$ is invalid. To expose the exact logical failure, put $t=s-1$ and, for any $\epsilon,C>0$, define \[ Z_\epsilon(t)=C\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{t}\right) =C\int_{[0,\infty)}e^{-tu} (\delta_0+\epsilon\,du),\qquad t>0. \tag{43.7} \] It is positive and completely monotone for $t>0$, has a positive-residue simple pole at zero, and nevertheless its meromorphic continuation has a zero at $t=-\epsilon$. After removing the pole, $L_\epsilon(t)=tZ_\epsilon(t)=C(t+\epsilon)$ has $L_\epsilon'(0)/L_\epsilon(0)=1/\epsilon$. Thus positivity of the transform alone cannot control the relative variation. This model is neither a Dirichlet $L$-function nor a counterexample to the problem: it lacks the arithmetic Euler factors and functional equation. It falsifies only the proposed inference from positivity and a simple pole.

Stress test.
The finite computations in the cited paper prove a finite statement, not termination of a conductor-by-conductor argument for all $q$. Applying its GRH-conditional termination estimate here would assume a substantially stronger unresolved zero-location statement. Likewise, excluding all but one exceptional character in a range does not exclude that last character. The derivative calculation covers the stipulated real interval and uses uniform constants; it does not claim complex zero-freeness. Every exchange of derivative and integral in $43.2$ was made in $s>0$ with an explicit integrable majorant, whereas no use of the series $43.6$ was made left of its convergence domain.

For any fixed finite collection of characters, positivity and continuity at one give a positive minimum zero-free radius after rescaling by $\log q$. That elementary compactness argument applies to a finite set, not to an unbounded sequence of conductors. The potential counterexample regime is precisely a sequence for which the rescaled radii tend to zero. None of the inequalities derived above excludes that regime.

Outcome.
Outcome: Conditional reduction. The target follows from the explicit relative-variation estimate $43.5$. The supplied proof of the implication handles all conductors uniformly. What was proved unconditionally is a coarse derivative bound and the resulting weaker special-value-to-zero-distance estimate, together with a countermodel to a positivity-only shortcut. These auxiliary calculations are not claimed to be historically new. The missing cancellation in $43.5$, or an adequate one-sided substitute, has not been established; there is no unconditional exclusion of all Landau--Siegel zeros here.

From interpolation determinants to Wronskians: a literature report on Landau–Siegel zeros

From interpolation determinants to Wronskians: a literature contribution on Math-77

1. The common problem and the purpose of this report

The target in We-Solve's Math-77 is a **single constant that works for every conductor**. In the notation used here, it is

$$
\exists c>0\quad \forall q\ge3\quad
\forall\chi\text{ primitive, real and nonprincipal of conductor }q:\qquad
L(\beta,\chi)=0,\quad 0<\beta<1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (1-\beta)\log q\ge c.
$$

All five manuscripts concern this assertion. It excludes exceptionally close real zeros near one; it does not exclude every real zero in the critical strip or establish the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. A separate positive gap for each character would not suffice.

The existing Math-77-A2 literature report documents OpenAI's claims. This contribution concentrates on how the subsequent presentations change the algebraic argument, especially the replacement of Green's resultant calculation by Barreto's Wronskian. References below distinguish sources read directly from background results consulted through those sources.

2. What changes across the five presentations

**OpenAI: a weighted interpolation determinant.** The manuscript dated 1 October 2026 [OAI] works, apart from a fixed exceptional field handled separately, in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt d,\sqrt2)$. A four-dimensional integer box supplies points in three conjugate coordinates. An interpolation lemma establishes enough rank, and a greedy weighted selection produces a nonzero square determinant. Frobenius congruences at primes with $\chi(p)=-1$ give divisibility incompatible with its size when a hypothetical zero forces sufficient prime bias. The key scale comparison is between the number of points and the degree required for interpolation.

The determinant's entries are **monomial evaluations**; the derivative matrices below should not be attributed to this construction. The paper also discusses an interpretation in terms of derivatives, but that is distinct from saying its displayed evaluation matrix has derivative entries. Its absolute zero-gap constant is not given numerically. See OAI, Sections 2–6.

**Banks: explicit constants and an expanded direct proof.** Banks [Ba] retains the biquadratic interpolation method but reorganizes the proof and tracks its estimates quantitatively. His Theorem 1.1 states the admissible values $q_0=10^{10}$ and $\delta_0=0.003$. For the smaller conductors, he invokes the computation of Lu–Zaman–Zhao [LZZ], whose Theorem 1.1 excludes real zeros in $\sigma\ge1-1/(5\log q)$ for $q\le10^{10}$. The resulting stated bound is $(1-\beta)\log q\ge0.003$ for all $q\ge3$.

The contribution is principally explicitness and exposition, not a replacement of the algebraic mechanism. Banks credits OpenAI with the underlying ideas, describes Claude's assistance, and states that he personally checked the entire argument. That is the author's verification statement, not a check independently performed by this report. See Ba, introduction and Theorem 1.1.

**Kerr: replace determinant analysis by pigeonholing.** Kerr [Ke] keeps the biquadratic setting but counts bounded-height polynomials instead of selecting an interpolation determinant. A pigeonhole argument produces a nonzero polynomial subject to strong modular constraints. An algebraic-norm bound then upgrades divisibility to exact vanishing on the projected integer box. A degree lower bound attributed to Masser [Ma], stated as Kerr's Lemma 6, contradicts the degree of this polynomial.

Thus the replacement is not simply a shorter determinant computation: the auxiliary object becomes a vanishing polynomial, and the final obstruction is a polynomial nonvanishing theorem. Kerr credits ChatGPT assistance and Banks's exposition. See Ke, Lemmas 5–7 and the concluding proof. Masser's original theorem is not reproved or independently audited here.

**Green: quadratic-field jets and resultants.** Green [Gr] removes the auxiliary $\sqrt2$. Derivatives in one variable of bivariate monomials, evaluated at quadratic-conjugate pairs, form rows of an integral Gram-matrix construction. Row independence is proved by polynomial zero counting and a resultant bound for specialized root multiplicities; determinant size and prime divisibility then give the prime-sum estimate. Version 2 separates the lattice-size and derivative-order parameters and states, for $X=q^C$,

$$
\sum_{\substack{p\le X\\\chi(p)=-1}}\frac{\log p}{p}
\le\left(\frac12+O(1/C)\right)\log X+O_C(1).
$$

Importantly, **Green's version 2 already remarks that a nonsingular minor can replace the Gram determinant**. That alternative must not be presented as a new idea originating with Barreto. Green's provenance statement credits ChatGPT with the arguments and himself with the exposition. See Gr, Theorem 1.1, the remark before Section 3, and Appendix A.

**Barreto: the Wronskian replacement.** The publicly shared three-page manuscript [Br] expressly replaces Green's resultant-based independence argument by an ordinary polynomial Wronskian. It combines this with a weighted triangular set of monomials and a rectangle chosen to keep both quadratic conjugates small. The next section explains why these choices work together, rather than merely observing that a Wronskian appears in the proof.

3. Barreto's three-page argument: the essential steps

The manuscript has two parts: **Theorem 1 bounds a prime sum**, and **Corollary 2 converts that bound into a restriction on real zeros**. The first part does not assume that an exceptional zero exists. This separates the algebraic estimate from its analytic application. All implied constants in the manuscript are absolute.

Its Theorem 1 states, uniformly for $x\ge2$,

$$
A_\chi(x):=\sum_{\substack{p\le x\\\chi(p)=-1}}\frac{\log p}{p}
\le \frac12\log x+\frac14\log q+\log\log(3x)+O(1).
\tag{1}
$$

The nontrivial point is the coefficient $1/2$ of $\log x$, with controlled conductor dependence. Bounding by the sum over all primes would instead give $\log x+O(1)$. The account below follows [Br, pp. 1–3], expanding the logical connections while omitting its routine summation calculations.

**3.1. Small conjugates and weighted monomials.** Associate $\chi$ with a squarefree $d\ne1$, $|d|\le q$, so that $\chi(p)=(d/p)$ for odd $p\nmid q$. The manuscript chooses a rectangle of integer pairs $(u,v)$ of area $2x$, balanced between the sizes of $u$ and $v\sqrt q$. This supplies at least $2x$ points

$$
\alpha_i=u+v\sqrt d,\qquad \bar\alpha_i=u-v\sqrt d,
\qquad |\alpha_i|,|\bar\alpha_i|<B,
\qquad B=2\sqrt{2x}\,q^{1/4}.
$$

Here the bar means **quadratic-field conjugation**, not necessarily complex conjugation. Each of the two lists is pairwise distinct because $\sqrt d$ is irrational. The balancing matters quantitatively: $\log B=\tfrac12\log x+\tfrac14\log q+O(1)$, which will supply the principal terms in (1).

Give $z$ weight $1$ and $w$ weight $k$, where $3\le k\le x$ is chosen later. Use the monomials indexed by

$$
T=\{(r,s)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^2:r+ks<x\}.
$$

The row for $(r,s)$ consists of $\partial_z^j(z^rw^s)(\alpha_i,\bar\alpha_i)$ for every point and every integer $0\le j<2x/k$. These are ordinary derivatives, sometimes called jets: the value and its successive derivatives. Splitting each entry into its two integer coefficients in the basis $1,\sqrt d$ gives an integer matrix.

There are two reasons for this setup. A small degree in $w$ will control the dimension of a family of specialized polynomials. The weighted support will also remain closed under the substitutions needed for prime divisibility. The same parameter $k$ therefore connects the rank argument to the arithmetic argument.

**3.2. Why a Wronskian proves row independence.** Suppose the integer rows were dependent. Since the matrix is rational, a dependence can be chosen over $\mathbb Q$, producing a nonzero polynomial $F(z,w)$ supported on $T$. It has $\deg_zF<x$ and $\deg_wF<x/k$, and every specialization $F(z,\bar\alpha_i)$ has all the prescribed derivatives zero at $z=\alpha_i$.

Some specializations could vanish identically, but there are fewer than $x/k$ such values of $w$: any nonzero coefficient polynomial of $F$, viewed as a polynomial in $z$, already bounds their number. Consequently, more than $x$ specializations are nonzero. Their span is a space $V$ of polynomials in $z$ of dimension $\ell<x/k+1$.

Choose a monic basis $g_1,\ldots,g_\ell$ of $V$ with distinct degrees, by successively cancelling leading terms. Consider

$$
W(z)=\det\bigl(g_b^{(a)}(z)\bigr)_{
0\le a<\ell,\;1\le b\le\ell}.
\qquad W\ne0,\qquad \deg W<\ell x.
\tag{2}
$$

Why is it nonzero? For distinct degrees $n_b=\deg g_b$, its leading-coefficient determinant has entries given by the falling factorials $n_b(n_b-1)\cdots(n_b-a+1)$. These are monic polynomials of the successive degrees $a=0,\ldots,\ell-1$, evaluated at distinct numbers $n_b$. Their determinant is a nonzero Vandermonde determinant. This is a polynomial argument in characteristic zero; it does not invoke an unrestricted claim about Wronskians of arbitrary differentiable functions.

Now fix any nonzero specialization. Write $m=\lceil2x/k\rceil$ for the number of derivative conditions. The specialization has a zero of order at least $m$ at its associated $\alpha_i$. Make it a member of a new basis of $V$. Such a basis change multiplies the Wronskian by a nonzero constant. In the column corresponding to this basis member, derivatives through order $\ell-1$ still contain the factor $(z-\alpha_i)^{m-\ell+1}$.

The dimension bound gives $2\ell-2<2x/k$, hence $m-\ell+1\ge\ell$. Therefore **the same Wronskian has a zero of multiplicity at least $\ell$ at each of more than $x$ distinct points**. Its degree would exceed $\ell x$, contradicting (2).

This is the decisive simplification: instead of tracking multiplicities of irreducible factors through resultants, one packages the specializations into one finite-dimensional polynomial space and counts zeros of one nonzero Wronskian. The integer matrix consequently has full row rank, so a nonzero square integer minor $D$ using all rows exists. No particular column-selection algorithm is needed.

**3.3. Why prime divisibility survives differentiation.** Let $k\le p\le x$ and $\chi(p)=-1$. These primes are odd and do not divide $q$. Euler's criterion and the binomial theorem give $\alpha_i^p\equiv\bar\alpha_i\pmod{p\mathbb Z[\sqrt d]}$. Thus $\phi(z,w)=z^p-w$ takes values divisible by $p$ at the chosen points.

The essential additional fact is that **every positive ordinary $z$-derivative of $\phi$ also has coefficients divisible by $p$**; derivatives beyond its degree are zero. Consequently, differentiating a product of $h$ copies of $\phi$ does not destroy its $p^h$ divisibility at these points. This explains why the derivative matrix remains compatible with Frobenius. It is not merely a congruence for the undifferentiated entries.

For $h=\lfloor r/p\rfloor$, replace the row arising from $z^rw^s$ by the row arising from

$$
z^{r-ph}(z^p-w)^h w^s
=z^rw^s+\sum_{j=1}^{h}(-1)^j\binom hj z^{r-pj}w^{s+j}.
\tag{3}
$$

Every added monomial stays in $T$, since
$r-pj+k(s+j)=r+ks-(p-k)j\le r+ks<x$.
It also has smaller $z$-degree. Ordering rows by that degree makes (3) an integer triangular row transformation with diagonal entries one. The chosen minor is unchanged, while its transformed $(r,s)$ row is divisible by $p^{\lfloor r/p\rfloor}$. Therefore

$$
p^{\sum_{(r,s)\in T}\lfloor r/p\rfloor}\mid D.
\tag{4}
$$

The role of the weights is now visible: replacing a block $z^p$ by $w$ does not increase weight when $p\ge k$. This is the support-preservation fact that lets the full exponent $\lfloor r/p\rfloor$ be used.

**3.4. Comparing divisibility with size.** Put $M=|T|$ and $U=\sum_{(r,s)\in T}r$. The manuscript's lattice-point estimates give $M\ll x^2/k$ and $U\gg xM$. The latter relation absorbs two kinds of error.

First, replacing $\lfloor r/p\rfloor$ by $r/p$ loses at most $M$ per prime. Chebyshev's bound makes the total logarithmic loss $O(xM)=O(U)$. Second, determinant expansion and the derivative bounds also fit within $O(U)$ after choosing the cutoff appropriately. Primes below $k$ contribute at most $\log k+O(1)$ by Mertens' estimate.

For the size estimate, the row indexed by $(r,s)$ is bounded using $B^r(x^2B)^{x/k}$. Choosing $k=\lceil\log(x^2B)\rceil$ limits the extra factor to $e^x$ per row. The factorial in determinant expansion is likewise absorbed. The manuscript consequently obtains $\log|D|\le U\log B+O(U)$. Combining this with (4) gives

$$
A_\chi(x)
\le\frac{\log|D|}{U}+\log k+O(1)
\le\log B+\log k+O(1).
\tag{5}
$$

For this step the manuscript first reduces to $x\ge\max(e^3,\sqrt q)$, where $3\le k\le x$ and $\log k=\log\log x+O(1)$. The omitted small range follows directly from Mertens' estimate. Substitution of the value of $B$ in (5) proves the stated bound (1).

Thus the conductor term comes from controlling both conjugates, the logarithmic-logarithmic term from the prime cutoff, and the saving in the main prime sum from comparing the divisibility of a nonzero integer with its size. These are explanations of the manuscript's choices, not additional claimed improvements.

**3.5. From the prime sum to real zeros.** Corollary 2 applies classical analytic number theory to (1). For $0<\eta\le1$, partial summation gives

$$
S_\eta:=\sum_{\chi(p)=-1}\frac{\log p}{p^{1+\eta}}
\le\frac{1}{2\eta}+\frac14\log q+O(\log(2/\eta)).
\tag{6}
$$

The manuscript controls the integrated $\log\log(3t)$ error by Jensen's inequality. Higher prime powers have a uniformly bounded contribution, while primes dividing $q$ contribute $O(\log\log(3q))$. The Euler products then give

$$
\frac{L'}{L}(1+\eta,\chi)
=2S_\eta-\frac1\eta+O(\log\log(3q))
\le\frac12\log q+O\bigl(\log(2/\eta)+\log\log(3q)\bigr).
\tag{7}
$$

The important cancellation is $2\cdot(1/(2\eta))-1/\eta=0$. This explains the significance of the coefficient $1/2$ in the prime-sum bound, rather than treating the passage to zeros as a black box.

For a real zero $\beta\in(0,1)$, retain its contribution in the Hadamard-product formula. At the real point $1+\eta>1$, the other nontrivial zeros contribute nonnegative real parts; the gamma term is uniformly bounded for both parities. Hence

$$
\frac{1}{1-\beta+\eta}
\le\frac{L'}{L}(1+\eta,\chi)+\frac12\log q+O(1)
\le\log q+O\bigl(\log(2/\eta)+\log\log(3q)\bigr).
\tag{8}
$$

Taking $\eta=(\log q)^{-2}$, inverting the upper bound for sufficiently large $q$, and subtracting $\eta$ yields the manuscript's conclusion

$$
(1-\beta)\log q
\ge1-O\!\left(\frac{\log\log(3q)}{\log q}\right).
\tag{9}
$$

This gives a positive uniform gap for large conductors. There are only finitely many remaining characters, each with finitely many real zeros in the relevant compact interval, so decreasing the constant covers them as well. This final finiteness argument is not a new finite-range numerical computation.

The asymptotic leading constant in (9) must not be confused with a demonstrated numerical bound $c=1$ for every conductor. Banks's all-conductor explicit constant and Barreto's asymptotic lower bound are different kinds of quantitative statement.

4. What is actually simplified, and what is retained

The central replacement is **resultant-based multiplicity control by a Wronskian degree argument**. Barreto does not discard the determinant method: the Wronskian proves that an arithmetic determinant is nonzero, after which the same size-versus-divisibility principle remains essential.

There are useful accompanying design changes. Barreto chooses the minor route already mentioned by Green, uses coefficient splitting to work with an ordinary integer determinant, balances the rectangle of algebraic integers, and uses the weighted triangular support $r+ks<x$. The algebra in (3) explains directly why that support is convenient. It would be misleading to credit every one of these ingredients as a separately new theorem; the report identifies how they are assembled in the supplied manuscript.

The contrast with Kerr is also precise. Kerr avoids the interpolation determinant by constructing an impossible vanishing polynomial and invoking Masser. Barreto retains a nonzero minor and proves the needed rank statement using elementary polynomial linear algebra. Neither description should be collapsed into the vague statement that both papers “use a simpler determinant.”

Finally, “elementary” describes the streamlined algebraic portion and presentation, not the removal of the classical theory of Dirichlet $L$-functions. Barreto still uses Chebyshev and Mertens estimates, the quadratic-character correspondence, Euler products and the Hadamard-product formula, with references to Montgomery–Vaughan [MV].
