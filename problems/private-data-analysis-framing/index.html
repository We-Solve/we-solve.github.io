A joint release can fail despite private marginals

A proposed simplification is to verify each released statistic separately and then combine them using common randomness. Here is an exact obstruction in a one-record model. Let the private record be $b\in\{0,1\}$, let $\varepsilon>0$, and let $Z$ have Laplace scale $1/\varepsilon$.
Proposition: Both coordinates of $(b+Z,Z)$ are individually pure $\varepsilon$-private, but their joint release is not $(\varepsilon',\delta)$-private for any finite $\varepsilon'$ and any $\delta<1$. Replacing the second $Z$ by an independent copy gives a joint pure $\varepsilon$-private release.
Proof: For the first coordinate, the change in location between the two records is 1. The triangle inequality bounds the ratio of its two densities by $e^\varepsilon$. The second coordinate is independent of the private record and therefore 0-private. In the joint common-noise release the measurable event $A=\{(u,v):u-v=0\}$ has probability 1 when $b=0$ and probability 0 when $b=1$. The privacy inequality would require $1\le\delta$. For independent noises $Z_1,Z_2$, the joint density is $f(u-b)f(v)$, where $f$ is the Laplace density. In its ratio between records, the second factor cancels, leaving $f(u)/f(u-1)$ or its reciprocal, each bounded by $e^\varepsilon$. Integrating proves the assertion for all joint events. ∎
This example does not contradict standard sequential composition: marginal privacy alone is weaker than the conditional privacy hypotheses used there. It identifies precisely why a shared-noise attempt fails. Independence is sufficient in this example, but is not asserted necessary for every useful correlated-noise construction.
