Verbatim supplied catalogue question

Can one formulate and prove the geometric analogue of the finite effective-complexity statement for points and lines on the projective plane, together with the corresponding sphere-area inequality?

What the source already proves

Shen's primary report, printed pages 1177--1178, states a finite-field complexity result, then an approximation-scale lower bound $n+s/2$, and already proves the spherical area inequality below. Its final open question follows a discussion of why a stronger effective-dimension statement uses a liminf across scales. Thus reproving the area inequality is not a solution of that final question. The catalogue does not specify a new geometric statement with all its quantifiers. We attempt a uniform-neighbourhood interpretation, establish its consequence, and exhibit why its exponent and quantifier order do not yield the stronger statement.

Complete rederivation of the source area inequality

Let $\mu$ be normalized area on the unit sphere $S^2$ in $\mathbb R^3$. Suppose measurable $A,B\subseteq S^2$ satisfy \[ b\in B,\quad a\cdot b=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a\in A. \] Then $\mu(B)\le\mu(A)^2$. Take independent uniform points $\xi,\eta$ on $S^2$ and put $\zeta=(\xi\times\eta)/\|\xi\times\eta\|$ whenever defined. Conditional on $\xi$, both singletons $\xi,-\xi$ have zero area, so the exceptional event $\eta=\pm\xi$ has probability zero. The law of $(\xi,\eta)$ is invariant under every rotation, and the cross product commutes with rotations. Thus $\zeta$ has rotation-invariant law, which is normalized spherical area. One elementary way to verify the last uniqueness fact is to average any continuous function over a uniform rotation: its value averaged at any fixed point is its spherical average, independent of that point. Integrating this identity against any rotation-invariant probability law yields the same average for all continuous functions, hence the same probability measure. Uniform rotations here can be constructed by first selecting a uniform axis image and then a uniform angle around that image. Now $\zeta\in B$ implies both $\xi,\eta\in A$, so independence gives the claimed inequality. This is the source's already-known argument, including the antipodal exceptional case; its printed letter $N$ in the probability line is interpreted as $B$.

A rigorous neighbourhood consequence

For a nonempty subset $E$ of the sphere write $E_\delta=\{x:\operatorname{dist}_{\rm chord}(x,E)<\delta\}$, where $0<\delta<1$. Under the same incidence hypothesis, \[ \mu(B_\delta)\le\mu(A_\delta)^2. \] Indeed, if $b'\in B_\delta$, choose $b\in B$ with $\|b-b'\|<\delta$. The rotation $R$ through the smaller angle from $b$ to $b'$ in their span fixes the perpendicular axis and satisfies $\|R-I\|_{\rm op}=\|b-b'\|<\delta$. If $a'\cdot b'=0$, then $a=R^{-1}a'$ is perpendicular to $b$, hence in $A$, and $\|a'-a\|<\delta$. Thus the whole great circle normal to $b'$ lies in $A_\delta$, and the preceding area inequality applies. The open neighbourhoods are measurable even if the original sets need only be measurable. If $B$ is empty the inequality is trivial; otherwise $A$ contains a circle and all logarithms below are finite.

Define the volume exponents explicitly by \[ d_\delta(E)=2-\frac{\log\mu(E_\delta)}{\log\delta}, \qquad \underline d(E)=\liminf_{\delta\downarrow0}d_\delta(E), \qquad \overline d(E)=\limsup_{\delta\downarrow0}d_\delta(E). \] These are the usual tubular-volume versions of lower and upper Minkowski dimension on a two-dimensional compact surface; only the displayed definitions are needed here. Since $\log\delta<0$, the preceding inequality yields, with its direction checked, \[ d_\delta(A)\ge1+\tfrac12d_\delta(B),\quad \underline d(A)\ge1+\tfrac12\underline d(B),\quad \overline d(A)\ge1+\tfrac12\overline d(B). \] For the limsup assertion take a sequence attaining the right-hand limsup; the pointwise inequality then bounds the left-hand limsup. For liminf, use the eventual lower bound from its definition. This does not yield the stronger expression $\min(1+s,2)$ for $0<s<2$, nor does a volume exponent identify individual effective Hausdorff dimensions.

Why simply strengthening the area exponent fails

Let $0<r<\pi/2$. Take $B_r$ to be the two closed spherical caps of angular radius $r$ about the north and south poles, and let $A_r$ be the equatorial closed band $|a_3|\le\sin r$. Every great circle perpendicular to a vector in $B_r$ lies in $A_r$: if $b$ makes angle at most $r$ with a pole, the length of the projection of that pole onto $b^\perp$ is at most $\sin r$, which bounds the absolute third coordinate of every unit vector in $b^\perp$. Conversely every point in that band belongs to one of these circles, by choosing the normalized projection of the north pole onto its perpendicular plane (with an appropriate sign and the equatorial boundary handled directly). Spherical coordinates, for which normalized $a_3$ is uniform on $[-1,1]$, give \[ \mu(A_r)=\sin r,\qquad \mu(B_r)=1-\cos r. \] Hence for any fixed $p>2$ and constant $C<\infty$, $\mu(B_r)\le C\mu(A_r)^p$ fails for small $r$, since \[ \frac{1-\cos r}{(\sin r)^p}\sim\tfrac12 r^{2-p}\longrightarrow\infty. \] The asymptotic follows from $1-\cos r=2\sin^2(r/2)$ and $\sin r/r\to1$. Thus an improved universal area power cannot supply the missing dimension bound. For each fixed positive $r$ both sets have interior, so this family is not itself a counterexample to a Hausdorff-dimension assertion.

The unproved exchange of point and scale

A statement producing a point at each resolution need not produce one point good at infinitely many appropriate resolutions, much less at all resolutions needed for a liminf. Abstractly, let $I_m$ be pairwise disjoint nonempty subintervals of $[0,1]$ and $a_m(x)=\boldsymbol{1}_{I_m}(x)$. Then \[ \sup_x\liminf_m a_m(x)=0, \qquad \liminf_m\sup_x a_m(x)=1. \] Every point belongs to at most one interval, while every interval contains a point, proving both assertions. This is a quantifier counterexample, not an example of actual prefix complexities. The area proof and its neighbourhood version give no additional coherence that would rule out the relevant escape of witnesses across scales. Establishing the right geometric formulation and that coherence is the remaining task.
