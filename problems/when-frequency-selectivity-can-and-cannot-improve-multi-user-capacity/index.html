CS-473 · Math-5008

When frequency selectivity can and cannot improve multi-user capacity

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$. Specialized notation and index ranges follow the cited source and the definitions below.

A channel maps transmitted signals to noisy received signals. Channel capacity is the maximum reliable information rate under a specified input-power and channel-information model; spectral efficiency normalizes rate by bandwidth. Delay spread describes variation in arrival times of channel paths. Spatial focusing concentrates energy spatially, and time-reversal transmission uses a reversed channel response. Multi-user capacity is a region of simultaneously achievable user rates.

Statement recorded in the supplied source.
1. Spectral efficiency AP: One can consider spectral efficiency (bps/Hz) for (a) a single user (b) multiple users within the same cell (c) multiple cells AP: HDB cannot improve capacity. (This fact has not been proven for the multi-user case, although it is believed to be true.) BH: In the multi-user case, HDB provides frequency diversity. 2. Coverage (SNR + fade margins) AP: SF does not buy coverage. HDB only buys coverage through di-versity. GP: There is a trade-off between coverage and transmission rate. 3. Reliability 14 AP: Reliability is a measure on the statistics of the link and HDB helps, but there is no benefit from SF. AP: Tx processing does not gain over Rx processing. CT: The capacity of a SIMO channel is the same as the capacity of a MISO channel (if CSI is available at Tx), but the SIMO channel does not have SF. 4. Channel estimation AP: For the same amount of power, you have to estimate a lot more parameters in a HDB channel than in a non-HDB channel. This is problematic, especially for the weaker taps. AP: What is the interaction with SF? This relates to iterative TR. 5. Signaling overhead AP: Current systems have about 25-30 % signaling overhead, which eats up spectral efficiency. In these scenarios, iterative TR would not be worth its cost in delay. However, channel estimation is especially important if there is fading. 6. Low probability of intercept AP: In this case, SF is very important. In the context of TR, it can be achieved even with 1 transmit antenna. CDMA technology is not a competitive contender in this regard, because pseudo-random sequences are well-known. LPI applications are probably prepared to throw away bandwidth. In UWB applications, e.g., cable replacement applications, one is not interested in spectral efficiency. Also, the transmit power is limited. GP: In each LPI transmission, the power delivered can be very low, but the power from several transmissions will add up. GP: Are there commercial applications for LPI? Probably not, because they wouldn't tolerate the high rate back-off. In these cases, se-curity is achieved with higher layer mechanisms. BH: You don't have to do a complete rate back-off, but only to the point which the equalizer can handle the channel. 15 VI. Further Discussion on Topics from Open Discussion Session on Thursday [S2]

Literature and context check

The primary AIM time-reversal discussion was consulted. It bundles capacity, coverage, overhead and several qualitative comparisons without fixing one channel law or power allocation convention. The following real Gaussian two-tone calculation tests a possible universal reading, not those whole agendas.

Research attempt: User-dependent spectral gains can reverse a flat-channel comparison

Take two real orthogonal tones with independent unit-variance Gaussian noise and two users, each with total power $P>0$. For Gaussian inputs, the sum mutual information on a tone with power gains $g_i$ (channel amplitudes $\sqrt{g_i}$) and input powers $p_i$ is $\tfrac12\log(1+\sum_i g_ip_i)$. This follows by subtracting the noise entropy from the output entropy; a real Gaussian of variance $v$ has entropy $\tfrac12\log(2\pi e v)$, obtained by integrating its log density.

In the flat case each user's gain is one on both tones. For any power allocation let the aggregate tone powers be $s_1,s_2$, so $s_1+s_2\le2P$. The Gaussian sum-information is $\tfrac12\log((1+s_1)(1+s_2))\le\log(1+P)$, since their product is maximized at equal powers (expand the squared difference). Equality is attainable while respecting both users' budgets.

Now give user one gains $(2,0)$ and user two gains $(0,2)$. Each user's total spectral gain is still two, matching the flat case. Put each user's full power on its own nonzero-gain tone. The resulting sum information is $\log(1+2P)>\log(1+P)$. Thus with user-specific selectivity and adaptive allocation, the assertion that frequency selectivity cannot improve this sum-rate benchmark is false. The improvement comes from orthogonal favorable frequencies; it does not arise in every selective channel.

For contrast, with one user and forced equal powers on two tones, concavity gives $\frac12\log(1+g_1P/2)+\frac12\log(1+g_2P/2) \le\log(1+(g_1+g_2)P/4)$, so equal gains maximize this different benchmark. The elementary inequality is again the product bound at fixed sum. These two opposite conclusions show why power allocation, user dependence and the preserved channel statistic must be fixed before making the source's comparison.

No assertion about arbitrary coding, finite-block reliability, training overhead, interception, or actual time-reversal hardware follows from these chosen Gaussian-input mutual-information calculations.
