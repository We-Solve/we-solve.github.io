Statement

For $n\in\mathbb{N}$, consider the Diophantine equation \begin{equation} \label{wsref-0-65-71-3a-32-36-31} \frac{n}{2^n}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{a_k}{2^{a_k}}, \end{equation} where $t\ge 2$ and $a_1,\dots,a_t$ are distinct positive integers.

Questions:

  1. Are there infinitely many $n$ for which (eq:261) is solvable?
  2. Is (eq:261) solvable for all $n$?
  3. Does there exist a rational $x$ for which \[ x = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_k}{2^{a_k}} \] has at least $2^{\aleph_0}$ distinct solutions (i.e. uncountably many distinct increasing sequences $(a_k)$)?

Restatement from Math-5536-A2 r1, with the source manuscript’s qualifications.

Erdős Problem #261

1) ROUND-2 OBJECTIVE

I pursue path (C) in the fail-safe sense: isolate the exact finite-combinatorial structure left open by Round 1, and exploit it to produce new infinite families beyond the Cusick--Borwein--Loring family already proved in Round 1.

2) Round-1 FOUNDATION USED

From Round 1 I use only the already-proved contiguous-block identity: \[ \frac{n}{2^n}=\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+m}\frac{k}{2^k} \qquad\text{for}\qquad n=2^{m+1}-m-2. \] This is the Borwein--Loring realization of Erdős' remark about Cusick's argument.

3) NEW INSIGHT / TOOL (ROUND-2)

The new tool is an exact offset parametrization of every solution.

If \[ \frac{n}{2^n}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{a_i}{2^{a_i}} \qquad (t\geq 2,\ a_1<\cdots<a_t), \] then necessarily every $a_i>n$, so after writing \[ a_i=n+d_i \qquad (1\leq d_1<\cdots<d_t), \] the equation becomes \[ n=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{n+d_i}{2^{d_i}}. \] Equivalently, if $D=\{d_1,\dots,d_t\}$ then \[ n\left(1-\sum_{d\in D}2^{-d}\right)=\sum_{d\in D} d\,2^{-d}. \] Thus \[ n=\frac{\sum_{d\in D} d\,2^{-d}}{1-\sum_{d\in D}2^{-d}}. \tag{261.1} \] This is a necessary and sufficient condition for solvability.

4) ATTACK PLAN (ROUND-2)

The exact gap left by Round 1 was this: we had one explicit infinite family, but no structural reduction of the full problem. The plan is now:

  1. prove the offset parametrization $261.1$;
  2. use it to reinterpret the problem as a finite-subset existence problem;
  3. build new infinite families by choosing $D$ of the special form \[ D=\{1,2,\dots,M\}\setminus\{M-r,M\}. \]

This overcomes the earlier gap because it turns a problem about arbitrary exponents $a_i$ into a problem about finite binary-offset sets.

5) WORK (ROUND-2)

Step 1: every exponent lies strictly above $n$.

Let \[ f(m):=\frac{m}{2^m}\qquad (m\geq 1). \] On positive integers, $f$ is nonincreasing, with the only tie $f(1)=f(2)=1/2$, and it is strictly decreasing for $m\geq 2$.

Suppose \[ \frac{n}{2^n}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{a_i}{2^{a_i}} \] with $t\geq 2$ and distinct $a_i$. If some $a_i\leq n$, then \[ \frac{a_i}{2^{a_i}}\geq \frac{n}{2^n}, \] and the inequality is strict unless we are in one of the obvious one-term equality cases. Since all summands are positive and $t\geq 2$, this is impossible. Therefore \[ a_i>n\qquad\text{for all }i. \tag{261.2} \]

Step 2: exact offset parametrization.

Define $d_i:=a_i-n$, so by $261.2$ each $d_i$ is a positive integer. Then \[ \frac{n}{2^n} = \sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{n+d_i}{2^{n+d_i}}. \] Multiplying by $2^n$ gives \[ n=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{n+d_i}{2^{d_i}} = n\sum_{i=1}^{t}2^{-d_i}+\sum_{i=1}^{t} d_i\,2^{-d_i}. \] Hence \[ n\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{t}2^{-d_i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{t} d_i\,2^{-d_i}, \] and therefore \[ n=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{t} d_i\,2^{-d_i}}{1-\sum_{i=1}^{t}2^{-d_i}}. \] Conversely, if $D=\{d_1<\cdots<d_t\}$ is any finite set of positive integers such that the right-hand side of $261.1$ is a positive integer $n$, then setting \[ a_i:=n+d_i \] gives \[ n\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{t}2^{-d_i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{t} d_i\,2^{-d_i}, \] which rearranges back to \[ \frac{n}{2^n}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{a_i}{2^{a_i}}. \] So $261.1$ is an exact necessary-and-sufficient parametrization.

Step 3: reinterpretation of the open all-$n$ problem.

The question "is there a solution for every $n$?" is now exactly equivalent to:

Equivalent formulation. For every positive integer $n$, does there exist a finite set \[ D\subset \{1,2,3,\dots\} \] such that \[ \sum_{d\in D}2^{-d}<1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad n=\frac{\sum_{d\in D} d\,2^{-d}}{1-\sum_{d\in D}2^{-d}}\, ? \]

Step 4: a new two-omission family.

Fix integers $M>r\geq 1$ and take \[ D=\{1,2,\dots,M\}\setminus\{M-r,M\}. \] Then \[ \sum_{d=1}^{M}\frac{n+d}{2^{n+d}} = \frac{n+2}{2^n}-\frac{n+M+2}{2^{n+M}} \] by the same dyadic-tail identity used in Round 1.

Subtracting the omitted terms gives \[ \sum_{d\in D}\frac{n+d}{2^{n+d}} = \frac{n+2}{2^n} -\frac{n+M-r}{2^{n+M-r}} -\frac{n+M+2}{2^{n+M}} -\frac{n+M}{2^{n+M}}. \] Requiring this to equal $n/2^n$ and multiplying by $2^{n+M}$ yields \[ 2^{M+1} = (2^r+2)n+(2^r+2)M-r\,2^r+2. \] Hence \[ n= \frac{2^{M+1}-(2^r+2)M+r\,2^r-2}{2^r+2}. \tag{261.3} \] Whenever the right-hand side is a positive integer, we get a valid solution \[ \frac{n}{2^n} = \sum_{\substack{1\leq d\leq M\\ d\neq M-r,\ d\neq M}} \frac{n+d}{2^{n+d}}. \]

Step 5: two explicit infinite families.

Family A (new in this round). Take $r=3$. Then $261.3$ becomes \[ n=\frac{2^{M+1}-10M+22}{10} =\frac{2^M-5M+11}{5}. \] This is an integer exactly when \[ 2^M\equiv -11\equiv 4 \pmod{5}, \] i.e. when \[ M\equiv 2\pmod{4}. \] Therefore, for every $M\equiv 2\pmod{4}$, \[ n=\frac{2^M-5M+11}{5} \] has a solution with \[ D=\{1,2,\dots,M\}\setminus\{M-3,M\}. \] The first values are \[ n=9,\ 197,\ 3265,\dots \] For instance, when $M=6$, \[ \frac{9}{2^9} = \frac{10}{2^{10}}+\frac{11}{2^{11}}+\frac{13}{2^{13}}+\frac{14}{2^{14}}. \]

Family B (new in this round). Take $r=4$. Then $261.3$ becomes \[ n=\frac{2^{M+1}-18M+62}{18} =\frac{2^M-9M+31}{9}. \] This is an integer exactly when \[ 2^M\equiv -31\equiv 5 \pmod{9}. \] Since the powers of $2$ modulo $9$ have period $6$, this happens exactly when \[ M\equiv 5\pmod{6}. \] Therefore, for every $M\equiv 5\pmod{6}$, \[ n=\frac{2^M-9M+31}{9} \] has a solution with \[ D=\{1,2,\dots,M\}\setminus\{M-4,M\}. \] The first values are \[ n=2,\ 220,\ 14550,\dots \] For instance, when $M=5$, \[ \frac{2}{2^2} = \frac{4}{2^4}+\frac{5}{2^5}+\frac{6}{2^6}. \]

Remark. The Round 1 Borwein--Loring family is the case $r=1$ in $261.3$, because then \[ D=\{1,2,\dots,M-2\} \] and \[ n=2^{M-1}-M=2^{(M-2)+1}-(M-2)-2. \]

6) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION

  • Monotonicity issue at $1,2$. The only tie for $m/2^m$ is $1/2=2/4$. This does not invalidate Step 1, because the right-hand side has at least two positive summands.
  • Necessity versus sufficiency. Formula $261.1$ was proved both ways. No hidden assumption remains.
  • Distinctness of exponents. Distinct $d_i$ give distinct $a_i=n+d_i$ automatically.
  • Size of $t$. In Family A we have $t=M-2\geq 4$ for $M\geq 6$; in Family B we have $t=M-2\geq 3$ for $M\geq 5$.
  • Arithmetic checks. For $r=3$ the congruence condition is exactly $M\equiv 2\pmod 4$; for $r=4$ it is exactly $M\equiv 5\pmod 6$.

7) FINAL (EXACTLY ONE)

UNRESOLVED (BUT STRICTLY ADVANCED)

Round 2 gives a full parametrization of all finite solutions and two new explicit infinite families beyond the Round 1 contiguous-block family. The open "for every $n$?" question is now reduced exactly to the finite-subset existence problem encoded by $261.1$.

Problem 261: an exact residual search for distinct dyadic representations

1) OBJECTIVE AND SOURCE REVIEW

Attempt dated 2026-09-23. The target is to represent every $n/2^n$ as $\sum_{a\in E}a/2^a$ with distinct positive integers $a$ and $|E|\ge2$. I read 261.tex and 261_v2.tex fully; they repeat the offset identity and two infinite families. I add an exact bounded search with a proved pruning rule, rather than treating those families as coverage of every $n$.

2) WORK

In a representation with at least two positive terms, all exponents exceed $n$: the function $a/2^a$ decreases after its initial tie at $a=1,2$, and an exponent at most $n$ already contributes at least the target. Write the selected offsets as $d=a-n>0$. The equation becomes \[ n=\sum_{d\in D}(n+d)2^{-d}. \] Define $r_0=n$ and, scanning $d=1,2,\ldots$, \[ r_d=2r_{d-1}-\epsilon_d(n+d),\qquad\epsilon_d\in\{0,1\}. \] A finite representation ends at $r_d=0$. Every viable intermediate state satisfies \[ 0\le r_d<n+d+2, \] because the total available future contribution, after multiplying by $2^d$, is $\sum_{h\ge1}(n+d+h)2^{-h}=n+d+2$. Equality would require selecting the entire infinite tail and cannot lead to a finite representation. Negative residuals cannot be repaired by positive terms.

Consequently a complete search to offset $M$ needs only the integer states $r$ in this interval, together with the number of selected terms capped at two. For states with the same residual and capped count, retaining one witness loses no possible continuation. This gives $O(M(n+M))$ transition work, apart from storing witnesses, instead of enumerating all $2^M$ subsets.

The accompanying exact-rational program searched $1\le n\le100$ through offset $200$. It found witnesses for 49 values and none within that bound for 51 values. For example, \[ \frac1{2}=\frac4{2^4}+\frac5{2^5}+\frac6{2^6},\qquad \frac3{2^3}=\frac4{2^4}+\frac6{2^6}+\frac8{2^8}. \] Every found witness was independently summed using rational arithmetic. The largest first successful offset in this run was $104$.

3) ADVERSARIAL VERIFICATION

In particular, $n=10$ had no witness through offset $200$; this is a finite exclusion, not a counterexample. The state count must distinguish zero, one, and at least two selected terms to exclude the one-term equality for $n=1$. A further necessary check is that the largest exponent in any valid representation is even: clearing the largest power of two leaves all other terms, including the target, even.

4) FINAL

UNRESOLVED. The search is exhaustive within its stated bound and yields reproducible certificates. There is no uniform termination proof and no finite certificate of impossibility for any $n$.
