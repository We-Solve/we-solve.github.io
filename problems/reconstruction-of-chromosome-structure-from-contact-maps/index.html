What does a chromosome contact map look like? represent and analyze the structure of proteins. They are closely related to re- currence networks obtained from recurrence plots of dynamical systems, and the same reconstruction methods can be used. The main steps of the reconstruction are (i) to derive a complete distance matrix using shortest-path distance on the weighted contact network, (ii) to use a result of distance geometry based on the three dominant eigenvectors of the metric matrix (yielding the Gram matrix of the structure) (iii) or approximating the 3D structure using multidimensional scaling techniques. Analogies between the algebraic analysis (principal eigenvector of the correla- tion matrix) and motif analysis (topological domains seen as squares along the diagonal) of the corresponding contact matrices can also be drawn. Research attempt: An exact Euclidean-distance test and a shortest-path counterexample

When a complete distance array admits an exact embedding.
Let $D=(d_{ij})$ be a symmetric nonnegative $N\times N$ array with zero diagonal, $N\geq1$. Put $S_{ij}=d_{ij}^2$, $H=I-\mathbf1\mathbf1^T/N$, and $B=-\frac12HSH$. Then $D$ is the distance array of points in a Euclidean space if and only if $B\succeq0$. The least embedding dimension is $\operatorname{rank}B$. For the forward implication, center the points so their coordinate matrix $Z$ has $Z^T\mathbf1=0$. Their squared distances are $S_{ij}=\|z_i\|^2+\|z_j\|^2-2z_i^Tz_j$, whence $B=ZZ^T\succeq0$. For the converse, factor the PSD matrix $B=ZZ^T$ with $\operatorname{rank}B$ columns by its nonnegative eigenvalues. Since $B\mathbf1=0$, one has $\|Z^T\mathbf1\|^2=\mathbf1^TB\mathbf1=0$, so the points are centered. Let $S'$ be their squared-distance matrix. Then $HS'H=-2B=HSH$. Writing $E=S-S'$, the equality $HEH=0$ means $E_{ij}=r_i+r_j-r$, where $r_i$ is its $i$th row mean and $r$ is the overall mean. Its zero diagonal gives $2r_i=r$ for every $i$. Averaging gives $2r=r$, hence every $r_i=0$ and $E=0$. Thus the constructed distances are exactly $D$. Any embedding has centered Gram matrix $B$ and needs at least its rank many coordinates, proving the dimension claim.
A shortest-path metric that fails the test.
Take the unweighted four-cycle. Its shortest-path distances are one between adjacent vertices and two between opposite vertices, so \[ S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&4&1\\1&0&1&4\\4&1&0&1\\1&4&1&0\end{pmatrix}. \] The vector $v=(1,-1,1,-1)^T$ has sum zero and $Sv=2v$. Therefore $Bv=-v$ and $v^TBv=-4<0$. Although shortest-path distances satisfy symmetry, nonnegativity and the triangle inequality, they are not Euclidean in any dimension in this example. The remaining centered eigenvectors $(1,0,-1,0)$ and $(0,1,0,-1)$ have $S$-eigenvalue $-4$, hence $B$-eigenvalue $2$; the constant vector has $B$-eigenvalue zero. The spectrum is exactly $2,2,0,-1$. Keeping the positive eigenspaces gives points $(1,0),(0,1),(-1,0),(0,-1)$. Their opposite distances are two but adjacent distances are $\sqrt2$, not one. Spectral truncation has produced an approximation with explicitly changed measurements, not exact recovery.
Even a PSD matrix can need more than three coordinates.
If $N=5$ and all off-diagonal distances are one, then $S=J-I$ and $B=\frac12H$ has rank four, as $H$ is identity on the four-dimensional zero-sum subspace. It is a legitimate Euclidean distance array but cannot be represented exactly in three dimensions. Thus neither taking three dominant eigenvectors nor taking all nonnegative eigenvalues universally justifies an exact 3D reconstruction claim. A source-specific noise objective and dimension assumption are needed to evaluate the approximation.

Verification and adversarial audit

The squared-distance array, rather than the distance array itself, is centered. The four-cycle failure has an exact negative quadratic-form witness and all four eigenvalues are checked symbolically. The regular-simplex example separates PSD feasibility from the rank-three requirement. The $N=1$ case has zero Gram matrix and embeds as a single point.
