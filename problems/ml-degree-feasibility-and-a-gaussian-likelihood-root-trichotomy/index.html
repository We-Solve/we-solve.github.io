Research attempt

Literature and scope

Question 41 concerns algebraic likelihood roots and statistical admissibility without a fixed model. I tested whether the sign of a real parameter root alone determines its statistical meaning. Two elementary Gaussian parameterizations show why the parameter-to-distribution map must be retained.
Proposition: For independent centered bivariate Gaussian observations with covariance $\Sigma(t)=\operatorname{diag}(t,t^2)$, $t>0$, and empirical second moments $s_1,s_2>0$, the cleared score equation has exactly two simple real roots, one admissible and one inadmissible. In contrast, a negative root for the parameterization of a scalar variance by $t^2$ can be fully admissible.
Proof: Ignoring constants and multiplying by the positive sample size does not change maximizers. The per-observation log likelihood in the first model is \[ \ell(t)=-\tfrac12(3\log t+s_1/t+s_2/t^2),\qquad \ell'(t)=-\frac{3t^2-s_1t-2s_2}{2t^3}. \] The roots are $(s_1\pm\sqrt{s_1^2+24s_2})/6$. They are distinct, nonzero, and have opposite signs. The positive root is the unique maximum: the derivative is positive before it and negative after it on $(0,\infty)$, and $\ell$ tends to $-\infty$ at both ends. At the negative root the first diagonal entry of $\Sigma$ is negative, so the covariance is not positive definite and no Gaussian law is represented. Since neither root is zero, clearing $t^3$ did not create either root.

For a scalar zero-mean Gaussian with variance $t^2$ and domain $t\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$, second moment $s>0$ gives $\ell(t)=-\log|t|-s/(2t^2)$. The score is $-1/t+s/t^3$, whose roots $\pm\sqrt{s}$ both represent the same valid maximum-likelihood variance $s$. Thus negative coordinate values alone have no invariant statistical interpretation. ∎

Verification and remaining gaps

At $s_1=s_2=1$, the first score factors as $(t-1)(3t+2)$ and its roots are $1,-2/3$. The second example at $s=1$ has roots $\pm1$. These exact factorizations test the failed sign-only classification. They do not bound the complex roots for arbitrary models, analyze singular likelihood equations, or supply a general meaning for complex solutions. No novelty or general root-count theorem is claimed.
