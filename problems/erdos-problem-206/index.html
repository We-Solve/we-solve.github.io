Erdős Problem #206

1. FORMAL RESTATEMENT

For $x>0$, let $R_n(x)$ be the largest sum of $n$ distinct positive unit fractions strictly below $x$. Is it true for almost every $x$ that the optimal underapproximations are eventually obtained by successively appending the largest permissible unused unit fraction? Equivalently, after a finite initial stage there is one increasing denominator sequence whose $n$-term partial sums equal $R_n(x)$ for every subsequent $n$.

2. LITERATURE AND SCOPE

Kovač proved the stronger negative statement that the eventually greedy set has measure zero. We reconstruct his two-term obstruction and measure contraction, adding details on maxima, interval partitions and denominator compatibility. This is an attributed reconstruction, not a new theorem. The separate rational-number question mentioned as open on the current problem page has a later affirmative preprint by Kovač and Tang $2026$; it is not a premise of the argument below.

3. OPTIMAL VALUES AND THEIR INTERVALS

Write $H_n=1+1/2+\cdots+1/n$. The maximum defining $R_n(x)$ exists. Indeed, take a sequence of $n$-term sums tending to its supremum and pass to a subsequence along which each increasing denominator is either fixed or tends to infinity. The limiting sum $q$ has $l\le n$ fixed terms. If $l<n$ and $q=x$, every sum in that subsequence exceeds $q$, a contradiction. If $l<n$ and $q<x$, adding $n-l$ sufficiently small distinct fractions to the fixed terms gives a sum strictly between $q$ and $x$, contradicting the supremum. Thus $l=n$, the sum is attained, and it is below $x$.

For each attained optimal value $q$, the points with $R_n(x)=q$ form an interval $(q,r]$, or $(H_n,\infty)$ when $q=H_n$. To see this, no $n$-term sum can lie strictly between $q$ and such an $x$; the next boundary is the infimum of sums larger than $q$, and that boundary, when finite, still has optimal value $q$ because equality with $x$ is forbidden. These intervals form a countable partition $\mathcal I_n$.

The partition $\mathcal I_n$ refines $\mathcal I_{n-1}$. If $u<v$ have the same $R_n=q$ but different optimal $(n-1)$-term values, then $b=R_{n-1}(v)$ satisfies $u\le b<v$. Adding a sufficiently small unused fraction to a representation of $b$ gives an $n$-term sum between $q$ and $v$, a contradiction.

Every bounded interval in $\mathcal I_n$ has length at most $1/(n(n+1))$. Here is a direct mesh argument. Greedily choose $n$ distinct fractions below any $x\le H_n$, with increasing denominators $m_1<\cdots<m_n$. At some stage $j\le n$ the sequence first skips an integer, since otherwise its sum would be $H_n\ge x$. At that stage $m_j-1$ was unused and could not be chosen, so the residual after choosing $1/m_j$ is at most $1/(m_j(m_j-1))$. This inequality persists: at the next stage either the preceding integer is unused and infeasible, or it is the previous chosen denominator and the existing residual bound is at most its reciprocal. The final denominator satisfies $m_n\ge n+1$, so the final residual is at most $1/(n(n+1))$. Since $R_n$ is at least the greedy sum, the same bound holds for $x-R_n(x)$. Apply it at the right endpoint of a bounded partition interval, which is at most $H_n$.

4. A UNIFORM TWO-TERM LOSS

For every $i\ge1000$, a subset of measure at least \[ \frac{1}{1000}\left(\frac1{i-1}-\frac1i\right) \tag{1} \] in $(1/i,1/(i-1)]$ has a better two-term underapproximation than its greedy one.

Put $A=i(i+1)$, and consider \[ b_k=\frac1{i+1}+\frac1{A/2+k} =\frac1i+\frac1{x_k},\qquad x_k=A\frac{A+2k}{A-2k},\quad 0\le k\le\lfloor A/10\rfloor. \] The $j_k=\lfloor x_k\rfloor+1$ are distinct, since \[ x_{k+1}-x_k=\frac{4A^2}{(A-2k)(A-2k-2)}>1. \] On the interval \[ J_k=\left(\frac1i+\frac1{x_k},\frac1i+\frac1{\lfloor x_k\rfloor}\right] \] the feasible sum $b_k$ beats the greedy sum $1/i+1/j_k$. These intervals are disjoint and lie inside $(1/i,1/(i-1)]$; $x_k\ge A>i(i-1)$ ensures that containment. Their two denominators are distinct.

Take integers $l\in[A/100,3A/200]$. There are at least $i^2/200$ such integers when $i\ge1000$. Direct substitution gives \[ 4+\tfrac13\le x_{2l+1}-x_{2l}\le4+\tfrac23, \qquad x_{2l},x_{2l+1}<\tfrac65 i^2. \] For example, the denominator factors in the difference are between $(0.94A-2)$ and $0.96A$, and $A\ge10^6$; these bounds give the displayed inequalities. If both fractional parts were less than $1/3$, their difference would have distance less than $1/3$ from an integer, contradicting the first inequality. Thus from each disjoint index pair choose one $k$ with $\{x_k\}\ge1/3$. Its interval has length \[ |J_k|=\frac{\{x_k\}}{x_k\lfloor x_k\rfloor} \ge\frac{\{x_k\}}{x_k^2}>\frac{25}{108i^4}. \] Summing over at least $i^2/200$ disjoint intervals gives more than $1/(1000i(i-1))$, proving $1$.

5. CONTRACTION ALONG OPTIMAL PREFIXES

For $100\le s<t$, let $E_{s,t}$ be the set of $x\in(0,H_s]$ for which there are $m_1<\cdots<m_t$ such that the first $n$ fractions sum to $R_n(x)$ for every $s\le n\le t$. Because all the optimal values for $n\le t$ are constant on each interval of $\mathcal I_t$, the set $E_{s,t}$ is a union of such intervals. We prove \[ |E_{s,t+2}|\le\frac{1999}{2000}|E_{s,t}|. \tag{2} \]

Fix one bounded interval $I=(q,r]\subset E_{s,t}$, of length $\ell\le1/(t(t+1))$. Choose $i_0$ minimal with $1/i_0<\ell$. Split $I$ into an exceptional top interval $(q+1/i_0,r]$ and full pieces \[ I_i=q+(1/i,1/(i-1)],\qquad i>i_0. \] The top interval has length at most $1/(i_0(i_0-1))<10^{-4}\ell$, because $t>100$ gives $i_0>10^4$. Each full piece has $i>i_0\ge t(t+1)$.

We check explicitly that the new denominators are compatible with the old prefix. Suppose $x\in E_{s,t+2}\cap I_i$, and let $q$ have the $t$ denominators from its witnessing nested sequence. Its largest denominator $m$ is less than $i$. Otherwise one of the $t$ integers in $[m-t,m-1]$ is unused; replacing $1/m$ by the reciprocal of such an integer increases $q$ by at most \[ \frac{t}{m(m-t)}\le\frac{t}{i(i-t)}<\frac1i<x-q, \] contradicting $q=R_t(x)$. All these integers are positive since $i>2t$.

Therefore $1/i$ is unused and is the largest feasible next fraction; optimality and nesting force $R_{t+1}(x)=q+1/i$. The next fraction must be the greedy one for the remaining residual, with denominator at least $i(i-1)+1>i$. If $y=x-q$ belongs to one of the two-term bad intervals from Section 4, its improved pair has both denominators greater than $i$: any feasible pair with smallest denominator less than $i$ exceeds $y$, while a pair starting with $i$ cannot beat the greedy pair. The improved pair is consequently disjoint from the old prefix and would improve $R_{t+2}(x)$, a contradiction.

By $1$, at least $1/1000$ of each $I_i$ is excluded from $E_{s,t+2}$. Treat the short top interval as completely retained. This gives \[ |E_{s,t+2}\cap I| \le\left(1-10^{-3}(1-10^{-4})\right)|I| \le\frac{1999}{2000}|I|. \] Summing over the disjoint partition intervals proves $2$.

6. MEASURE ZERO AND FINAL

Iterating $2$, with the crude initial bound $|E_{s,t}|\le H_s$, shows \[ \left|\bigcap_{t>s}E_{s,t}\right|=0\qquad(s\ge100). \] Every eventually greedy $x$ belongs to one of these intersections for sufficiently large $s$: choose $H_s\ge x$ and begin after its exceptional finite prefix. Greedy added denominators increase; after finitely many additions they exceed all initial denominators, so the prefix can be sorted once to give the nested increasing sequence used above. A countable union of null sets is null.

SOLVED: almost every positive real number fails eventual greediness. This reproduces Kovač's known negative resolution with the required distinct-denominator checks. It provides no explicit irrational counterexample. The only external general facts are elementary measure theory, countable subadditivity, and subsequence extraction; no quantitative number-theoretic theorem is needed for the measure-zero proof.

For the later auxiliary rational result only: V. Kovač and Q. Tang, Eventually greedy best Egyptian underapproximations of rational numbers via optimal control, https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28387.
