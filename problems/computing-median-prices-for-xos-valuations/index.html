Computing median prices for XOS valuations

LLM status Open unverified

Statement

GT-046 | Online computational mechanisms | P: source-explicit computational question.

Definitions and mathematical statement

Let $n,m\geq1$ be integers, $M=[m]$, and $D=\bigotimes_{i=1}^nD_i$ a generic distribution over XOS valuations on $M$. XOS means that each realized valuation has a representation $v_i(S)=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq k_i}\sum_{g\in S}a_{i\ell g}$ for some finite integer $k_i\geq1$ and finite nonnegative real coefficients; the clauses need not be explicitly enumerated as input. For $p\in\R_{\geq0}^m$ and available set $A\subseteq M$, demand consists of all maximizers of $v_i(S)-\sum_{g\in S}p_g$ over $S\subseteq A$, including the empty set. Generic means that for every buyer $i$, each fixed $p$ and each fixed $A$, this maximizer is unique $D_i$-almost surely. Buyers arrive in the fixed order $1,\ldots,n$, choose a demanded subset of the remaining goods, and remove their purchases. Let $\pi_g(p)$ be the probability, over a fresh valuation profile drawn from $D$, that $g$ is sold at fixed prices $p$. Realized valuations are real-valued; the common rational-data convention below does not require them to be rational-valued. A nonnegative price vector $p$ is $\alpha$-median if \[ \pi_g(p)\leq1-\alpha,\qquad p_g>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \pi_g(p)\geq\alpha \quad(g\in M). \] The computational question is whether, from independent samples of $D$, one can output such a vector for $\alpha=1/2-\varepsilon$, with probability at least $1-\delta$ over the training samples and any algorithm randomness that all displayed bounds hold simultaneously for the returned vector, in time and sample size polynomial in $n,m,1/\varepsilon,\log(1/\delta)$ and the numerical input lengths. Valuations are accessed through demand and value oracles, and their oracle-call costs are counted; zero-price items need no lower sale-probability bound. The input accuracy parameters satisfy $0<\varepsilon<1/2$ and $0<\delta<1$.

Short English statement

Extend efficient median-price learning from unit-demand buyers to XOS buyers.

Variants and scope boundaries

Existence and polynomial sample complexity do not establish polynomial computation. An algorithm may randomize while returning a single price vector. Nongeneric distributions are outside the main question. An extension to them must specify witnessing choice rules and its own sale bounds: Definition 4.10 of the cited source imposes a lower bound for every item, without repeating Definition 4.2's zero-price exception. A mixture satisfying merely average sale bounds is a different output target.

Source relationship and status

In arXiv:2409.11091v1, Section 4.5 explicitly leaves the XOS extension of Theorem 4.9 open. The formal condition here is Definition 4.2, and the unit-demand proof constructs a single vector. Demand- and value-oracle access is distinct from a full explicit valuation table. A bit-complexity formulation still requires a numerical representation, precision and cost conventions, a representation of the output prices, and a runtime convention; the mathematical oracle descriptions alone do not fix these. Section 4.4 fixes a deterministic bundle ordering for empirical tie-breaking, whereas genericity alone does not determine demand-query answers at prices chosen adaptively from a sampled valuation.

Common conventions: Source mathematical conventions

Finite games. For a finite set $A$, $\Delta(A)$ is its probability simplex. Players choose independent mixed strategies in Nash equilibrium; correlation is allowed only when explicitly part of the solution concept. In a game with payoff functions $u_i$ and strategy profile $x$, an additive $\varepsilon$ Nash equilibrium satisfies \[ u_i(x)\geq u_i(x_i',x_{-i})-\varepsilon \quad\text{for every player $i$ and every admissible deviation $x_i'$}. \] For numerical approximation, payoffs are normalized as locally specified. An $\varepsilon$ payoff residual is distinct from distance at most $\varepsilon$ to the set of exact equilibria. Point convergence, convergence of empirical play, and convergence in distance to an equilibrium set are also distinct.

Computational representations. Unless stated otherwise, a complexity question takes rational data with binary encoding; polynomial time is measured in total bit length. A fixed-accuracy polynomial algorithm is not necessarily polynomial in $1/\varepsilon$, and neither is necessarily polynomial in $\log(1/\varepsilon)$. Succinct games and value, demand, or sampling oracles must declare their interfaces. Exact real solutions require an explicit representation; an unspecified list of arbitrary real numbers is not a finite computational output. A claimed algorithmic barrier is meaningful only for its specified model and reductions.

Dynamic games. Histories, information, observation, and allowable randomization are part of the model. A stationary strategy depends only on the current state; a positional strategy in a deterministic graph game chooses one action at each controlled vertex. Nash equilibrium at an initial state and equilibrium after every history are different requirements. An expectation of a pathwise $\liminf$ payoff, a $\liminf$ of expected averages, a limiting expected average, and a uniform finite-horizon equilibrium are different criteria. None is silently substituted for another.

Allocation and mechanisms. A complete allocation assigns every item exactly once unless otherwise stated. Zero-valued goods, negative values, capacity constraints, feasibility, lotteries, and copies of goods affect fairness definitions and are specified locally. Dominant-strategy incentive compatibility, Bayesian incentive compatibility, universal truthfulness, and truthfulness in expectation impose different inequalities. Whenever an objective ratio has zero denominator, its convention is stated or those instances are excluded.

Infinite-dimensional models. Expectations, integrals, stochastic equations, and optimization objectives require their local regularity, integrability, filtrations, and admissible domains. A backward--forward mean-field system is a boundary-value problem; it is not an initial-value semiflow without an additional construction. Long-time, large-population, and vanishing-noise limits require an order of limits and a convergence topology. If a minimum need not be attained, the objective is its infimum or supremum and the approximation requirement is stated separately.
