Math-12995

Is there a word which is unavoidable over a k letter alphabet, but not a (k-1) letter alphabet, for each integer k > 1

Statement

Definitions and mathematical statement

The supplied question asks whether, for every integer $k>1$, there is a word unavoidable over a $k$-letter alphabet but not over a $(k-1)$-letter alphabet. The meaning of "word" and "unavoidable" is not specified in the source, and the direction of comparison requires clarification.

For example, in the standard pattern interpretation, let $P$ be a nonempty finite word over a finite alphabet of variables. For a finite target alphabet $\Sigma$, an occurrence of $P$ in a word $w$ is a contiguous factor $h(P)$, where $h$ is a morphism assigning a nonempty word over $\Sigma$ to each variable. Write $U_k(P)$ if there is an integer $L\ge1$ such that every word in $\Sigma^L$, with $|\Sigma|=k$, contains such an occurrence. Under this interpretation the source's literal quantified target is \[ \forall k\ge2\ \exists P:\quad U_k(P)\ \text{and}\ \neg U_{k-1}(P). \] However, the standard meaning of a word over $\Sigma$ permits use of a subset of $\Sigma$. Consequently $U_k(P)$ implies $U_{k-1}(P)$ by restricting the target alphabet. Thus the stated direction is incompatible with the ordinary monotonicity convention. Reversing the two alphabet sizes would change the source question and is not done here. A literal fixed-factor meaning of "word" also differs from the pattern interpretation and is not selected without evidence.

Short English statement

The source asks for a word unavoidable over $k$ letters but avoidable over $k-1$ letters, for each $k>1$. Under the usual pattern definition the implication goes the other way, so the source's meaning or ordering needs confirmation; no corrected question is silently substituted.
