Math-14075

Authorship and Composition of Private Mathematical Manuscripts

Status Open · unsolvedmath.comLLM status Open unverified

Statement

Definitions and mathematical statement

A scriptor here means a person who writes the notes or manuscript under study. The supplied passage distinguishes at least two voices in reading or lecture notes: that of the note-taker and that of the text or speaker being recorded. It attributes the expression "several enunciative instances" to its reference [18], whose bibliographic details are not supplied. A private manuscript, in the sense used in the passage, is a manuscript intended only for its author's eyes. The passage observes that such manuscripts often depart from customary writing norms and suggests that methods from genetic criticism may help their analysis.

Formulation limitation. The supplied material presents methodological questions in the history of mathematics, rather than a mathematical conjecture or a theorem to be proved. It specifies neither a particular manuscript corpus nor mathematical objects, hypotheses, quantifiers, or a conclusion. Consequently, no precise mathematical statement can be recovered without adding information absent from the source. The following preserves the historical research questions instead of inventing a mathematical formulation.

For the reading notes, lecture notes, notebooks, slips of paper, and loose sheets considered in the passage, investigate the following questions using material evidence together with analysis of their content:

  1. Who is writing? Are several scriptors involved? Are there changes in pens or handwriting? How can the note-taker's voice be distinguished from that of the text or speaker being recorded?
  2. What is the lecturer's status, and what are the scriptor's objectives?
  3. Which writing acts can be observed, including underlining, titles, numbering, textual modifications, rewriting, and annotations?
  4. Are notebooks organized thematically, chronologically, or according to another discernible arrangement?
  5. When was a manuscript written? Reused paper and watermarks may provide dating clues, but the passage explicitly cautions that these must be used carefully.
  6. For whom was the manuscript written? In particular, how should the intended privacy of a manuscript inform its interpretation?
  7. What is the chronology of the writing process?

The passage situates these questions within historical studies of reading and lecture notes, while describing the history of mathematics as noticeably absent from most such studies. Its notebook examples concern Cartan; its examples involving slips and loose sheets concern Leibniz's and Dedekind's archives. It presents material analysis as a complement to content analysis, not as a guarantee of determinate answers. The supplied excerpt has a disrupted transition before its notebook discussion, so the missing wording cannot be reconstructed reliably.

The registry labels the item "open", but also marks its statement as unrecoverable and supplies the verification note that the official report passage concerns historical attribution and manuscript analysis rather than an open mathematical problem. Short English statement

The source asks how historians can identify writers, intended readers, dates, writing operations, and stages of composition in mathematical notes and private manuscripts. It gives methodological questions rather than a mathematical conjecture, and does not identify a specific corpus to investigate. A precise mathematical statement cannot be recovered from the supplied evidence; the registry's "open" label must be read alongside its unrecoverable statement classification.
