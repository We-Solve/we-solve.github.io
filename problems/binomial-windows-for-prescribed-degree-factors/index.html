Verbatim supplied catalogue question

Question 7. Let m ≥ n ∈ Z. Prove there is a polynomial g ∈ Fq[x] of degree

≤ 2 log n so that xm + g(x) has an irreducible factor of degree n. (Gao)

A cutoff that keeps the logarithm ambiguity visible

Let $n\ge1$, $m\ge n$ and an integer degree cutoff $L\ge0$ be fixed. The source requests $L=\lfloor2\log n\rfloor$ without specifying the logarithm base. Our statements retain $L$ explicitly. The proposed route is to choose an irreducible factor first and solve for the low degree perturbation in its quotient field.
Exact linear-algebra equivalence for a chosen factor.
Let $F\in\mathbb F_q[X]$ be monic irreducible of degree $n$, and let $\alpha$ be the class of $X$ in $K=\mathbb F_q[X]/(F)$. Then some $g$ of degree at most $L$ satisfies $F\mid X^m+g$ exactly when \[ -\alpha^m\in\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb F_q} (1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^L). \] Indeed, divisibility is equivalent to zero remainder modulo the monic $F$, which is the equation $\alpha^m+g(\alpha)=0$. The coefficients of $g$ give one direction and coordinates in the span give the other. If $L\ge n-1$ this always works, since the first $n$ powers form a basis by monic division and linear independence below degree $n$. For smaller $L$, dimension alone does not force membership. For example over $\mathbb F_2$, $F=X^3+X+1$ is irreducible: it has no root at zero or one, and any reducible cubic over a field has a linear factor. Here $\alpha^3=\alpha+1$ and $\alpha^4=\alpha^2+\alpha$, which is not in the span of $1,\alpha$. Thus a degree-one perturbation at $m=4$ cannot contain this selected factor. This is a counterexample to an arbitrarily chosen-factor route at cutoff one, not to the source's larger logarithmic cutoff.
An exact family of successful small windows.
Every element of a finite field with $q^n$ elements satisfies $\alpha^{q^n}=\alpha$: nonzero elements form a group of order $q^n-1$, so their orders divide that number by the coset proof of Lagrange's theorem; zero also satisfies the equation. It follows that if $s$ is a positive multiple of $n$, $r\ge0$ and $m=q^s+r$, then every monic degree-$n$ irreducible satisfies \[ F\mid X^m-X^{r+1}. \] To prove this, work in its $q^n$-element quotient field and repeatedly apply the $q^n$-power identity, obtaining $\alpha^{q^s+r}=\alpha^{r+1}$. The preceding remainder criterion then proves divisibility. Thus $g=-X^{r+1}$ works whenever $r+1\le L$. These windows are sparse special values of $m$; they are not all integers $m\ge n$.

For completeness, degree-$n$ irreducibles do exist over every finite field. In an algebraic closure the roots of $X^{q^n}-X$ are distinct (its derivative is $-1$), and form a field: the $q^n$-power map preserves sums, products and inverses. There are exactly $q^n$ roots. The orbit of a root under $\alpha\mapsto\alpha^q$ has length $d\mid n$; the product of its linear factors has coefficients fixed by that map, hence in $\mathbb F_q$ (the fixed elements are the $q$ roots of $X^q-X$). Its minimal polynomial has exactly that orbit: applying Frobenius to a root of a polynomial over $\mathbb F_q$ gives another root, while the displayed orbit product is itself such a polynomial. An element with degree less than $n$ lies among the roots of $X^{q^d}-X$ for a proper divisor $d$ of $n$. Their union has size at most $\sum_{d=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}q^d<q^n$ for $n\ge2$, by the finite geometric sum (including $n=2,q=2$). An element outside the union has degree $n$. The case $n=1$ is immediate. This justifies that the successful-window statement contains an actual factor rather than a vacuous universal quantifier.

The general step still missing.
For arbitrary $m$ and logarithmic $L<n-1$, one must vary $F$ and prove that at least one resulting power $\alpha^m$ lies in its short power span. Neither the dimension argument nor a count of candidate polynomials proves such an intersection. Exhaustive testing may find examples, but a failed test for one $F$ does not exclude all $F$, and an observed sequence of successes would not prove existence for all $m,n,q$. For $n=1$, the constant perturbation $g=-1$ already gives the linear factor $X-1$, consistent with the zero logarithmic cutoff.
