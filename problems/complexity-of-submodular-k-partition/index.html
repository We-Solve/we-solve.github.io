Complexity of Submodular $k$-Partition

Definitions and mathematical statement

Shared notation.
Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

A set function $f:2^V\to\mathbb R$ is submodular if $f(S)+f(T)\geq f(S\cup T)+f(S\cap T)$ for all $S,T\subseteq V$. A $k$-partition is a collection of pairwise disjoint nonempty sets $V_1,\ldots,V_k$ with union $V$. Its cost is $\sum_{i=1}^kf(V_i)$. The integer $k$ is fixed while $|V|$ grows.

Statement recorded in the supplied source.
For fixed $k$, what is the computational complexity of minimizing the sum of a submodular function over a partition into $k$ nonempty parts? [S2]
Scope qualification.
Complexity depends on the representation of $f$; a value-oracle model and an explicit finite representation are different problems. The supplied question does not impose monotonicity, symmetry, or a particular representation, so none is assumed here.

The general four-partition theorem and an exact obstruction to its naive extension

The general four-partition theorem and an exact obstruction to its naive extension

Problem: prb_ce5a761168264f5f8c8c29328a243463 — Complexity of Submodular $k$-Partition.
Claim type: **Literature report**.
Scope: A checked primary-source update for unrestricted submodular functions at $k=4$ and symmetric functions at $k=5$, plus an exact replay of the source's eight-vertex incompatibility example. This does not classify all fixed $k$ or certify the complete published proof independently.

Hirayama, Liu, Makino, Shi and Xu [1], Theorem 6.1, give an exact algorithm for minimum **four** nonempty parts of a general submodular function in $O(n^6\tau(n))$ time, where $\tau(n)$ is the time for submodular-function minimization. No monotonicity or symmetry hypothesis is imposed there. Their Corollary 6.1 gives $O(n^{14}\tau(n))$ time for **five** parts when the function is symmetric. These are credited published results. In a value-oracle arithmetic model they give polynomial oracle/arithmetic complexity; for a finite binary-value representation, the value-evaluation and submodular-minimization bit costs must also be included.

This updates the earlier CS-376-A1, which contains a two-part identity and a counterexample to repeated greedy splitting but does not cite this four-partition algorithm. It is not legitimate to infer the theorem simply from the existence of good two-part cuts. The new ingredient is a compatibility theorem for optimal three- and four-partitions, with size restrictions handled separately; the source's full case analysis is a credited input here.

We give a reproducible exact check of why the obvious next compatibility statement fails. Take vertices $0,\ldots,7$ and edges
$$01,12,02,15,23,24,25,34,56,57,67.$$
Every edge has weight one except $12$, whose weight is $1/2$. Let $c(S)$ be the weight of the graph cut of $S$, and use $f(S)=c(S)/2$. Then $f$ is a nonnegative symmetric submodular function. To see submodularity, check the contribution of one edge: its cut indicator satisfies the submodular inequality, and a nonnegative weighted sum preserves it. The half factor makes the official partition objective $\sum_i f(V_i)$ equal to the weight of edges crossing between different parts, since each crossing edge appears in exactly two cuts.

Exact enumeration gives the unique unordered optimal partitions
$$X=\{\{0\},\{1\},\{2,3,4\},\{5,6,7\}\},\quad f(X)=9/2,$$
$$Y=\{\{0,1,2,5\},\{3\},\{4\},\{6\},\{7\}\},\quad f(Y)=6.$$
Here $f(X)$ denotes the sum of $f$ on its parts. The source's relevant compatibility notion would require at least three of the four parts of $X$ to be contained in parts of $Y$. Only $\{0\}$ and $\{1\}$ are contained; each of the two larger parts meets three different $Y$-parts. Thus this unique minimum four-partition is incompatible with the unique minimum five-partition. The example is credited to §7 of [1], not presented as a new construction.

The finite certificate is exhaustive: `verify.py` generates unordered partitions by restricted-growth labels, so each partition is visited once. It evaluates all 1,701 four-partitions and 1,050 five-partitions. All weights are multiplied by two for integer comparisons; the minima are respectively nine and twelve in that scaling, with exactly one minimizer each. It additionally checks all 65,536 pairs of subsets for the defining submodular inequality and verifies the compatibility count of two. No floating-point comparisons or numerical optimization are used.

This obstruction also yields arbitrarily large examples. Replace each original vertex by a clique of $q$ labelled clones, put one original-weight edge between fixed representatives for each original edge, and give every internal clique edge weight ten. The displayed intact-clique partitions still cost $9/2$ and six. Splitting any clone clique crosses an internal edge of weight ten, so cannot be optimal for either objective. Every optimum therefore contracts to an optimum of the original graph, preserving uniqueness and incompatibility. This is a direct verification of the source's suggested clique-blowup mechanism, for all integers $q\ge1$.

The obstruction refutes that proposed extension of the compatibility property; it does not refute every polynomial algorithm for five or more parts. The symmetric five-partition result above uses a separate reduction and remains fully consistent with the graph example. The source's 2023 discussion leaves the all-fixed-$k$ question as future work; this report does not certify an exhaustive literature frontier through 2026. Nor does polynomial time for a fixed $k$ imply a polynomial whose exponent is independent of variable input $k$.

Verbatim supplied catalogue question

For fixed $k$, what is the computational complexity of minimizing the sum of a submodular function over a partition into $k$ nonempty parts?

The route tested

The source permits general submodular functions, not only graph cuts, and fixes the number of nonempty parts. We test whether a sequence of optimal two-part cuts suffices. The two-part objective has a useful exact submodular form, but the proposed repeated-cut extension fails on an explicit five-vertex graph.
The two-part objective remains submodular.
Define $g(S)=f(S)+f(V\setminus S)$. Applying submodularity to $A,B$ and then to their complements gives \[ g(A)+g(B)\ge g(A\cap B)+g(A\cup B). \] Indeed the unions and intersections of the complements are the complements of $A\cap B$ and $A\cup B$, respectively. Also $g(S)=g(V\setminus S)$. Its value is exactly the cost of the corresponding two-part partition when $S$ is nonempty and proper. Thus a two-part instance can be treated by minimizing this symmetric function subject to that exclusion. For example one may enumerate ordered distinct terminals $a,b$ and minimize on sets containing $a$ but not $b$; every nontrivial set occurs in one such subproblem. We do not here reprove or implement a polynomial submodular-minimization oracle. This identity alone is not an algorithm for the general fixed-$k$ case.
An exact counterexample to repeated optimal splitting.
Take vertices $0,1,2,3,4$. Vertices $1,2,3,4$ form a complete graph with all six edge weights equal to six. The only edge incident to $0$ is $01$, of weight fifteen. Let $f(S)$ be the total weight of edges with exactly one endpoint in $S$. This is nonnegative and symmetric. It is submodular: adding a vertex $v$ to a set changes its contribution from each incident edge $vu$ by $+w_{vu}$ if $u$ is outside and by $-w_{vu}$ if $u$ is inside. Enlarging the set can only decrease this marginal. Summing marginals while adding $A\setminus B$ one element at a time proves the submodular inequality for any $A,B$.

Every cut that splits the four clique vertices crosses at least three clique edges, so has weight at least eighteen. The cut $\{0\}$ has weight fifteen and is the unique minimum cut up to complementation. Thus an algorithm that first takes an optimal two-way split must isolate $0$. Splitting the remaining clique optimally then cuts eighteen more weight, producing a three-part partition with total inter-part edge weight thirty-three and partition cost sixty-six, since every crossing edge is counted once from each endpoint's part.

The true minimum three-part cost is sixty. To see this exactly, the total graph edge weight is $36+15=51$. For a partition into three nonempty parts, the part sizes are either $3,1,1$ or $2,2,1$. In the first case a three-vertex part containing $0,1$ has internal weight twenty-one, a part of three clique vertices has internal weight eighteen, and every other possibility has at most these amounts. In the second case a pair $\{0,1\}$ can contribute fifteen and the other pair six, totaling twenty-one; if that pair is absent, two pairs contribute at most twelve. Thus internal weight is at most twenty-one in every case. It is attained by $\{0,1\},\{2,3\},\{4\}$. Minimum crossing weight is therefore $51-21=30$ and partition cost is sixty. The repeated-splitting algorithm has strict ratio $66/60=11/10$.

First gap and audit

The primary opening talk on printed page 2901 was inspected. This counterexample concerns a natural algorithm even in the symmetric graph-cut subclass, where better methods may exist; it is not a hardness result or a claim about the current status of the three-part problem. Choosing each cut optimally does not optimize the interactions between the eventual parts. General algorithms would need a joint partition argument or a different representation of those interactions. The audit checked nonempty parts, the doubled cut objective, the unique first split, and both possible part-size patterns. No machine search or oracle experiment was used. The general fixed-$k$ complexity question remains unresolved in this attempt.
