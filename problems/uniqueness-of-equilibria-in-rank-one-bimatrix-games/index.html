A certificate route gives membership, not hardness

Assume the payoff matrices $A\in\mathbb Q^{m\times n}$ and $B\in\mathbb Q^{m\times n}$ are given in binary. The source already presents a polynomial-time algorithm for finding one rank-one equilibrium, and asks about uniqueness. We attempt to understand the certificate side of the decision problem.
Proposition: Nonuniqueness of Nash equilibrium in a rational bimatrix game has polynomial-length rational certificates verifiable in polynomial time. Therefore uniqueness belongs to co-NP, including on the rank-one subclass. This assertion alone proves no co-NP-hardness.
Proof: For nonempty supports $I\subseteq[m]$ and $J\subseteq[n]$, let $P_{I,J}$ consist of $(x,v)$ satisfying $x\ge0$, $\sum_i x_i=1$, $x_i=0$ outside $I$, $(B^Tx)_j=v$ for $j\in J$, and $(B^Tx)_j\le v$ for all $j$. Define $Q_{I,J}$ similarly by $y\ge0$, $\sum_jy_j=1$, $y_j=0$ outside $J$, $(Ay)_i=u$ for $i\in I$ and $(Ay)_i\le u$ for all $i$. They are bounded rational polytopes: the probabilities are bounded, and $u,v$ are convex combinations of the finitely many payoffs because their equality supports are nonempty. Every pair in $P_{I,J}\times Q_{I,J}$ is an equilibrium, since each player's positive-probability actions are best responses. Conversely, every equilibrium belongs to such a product using its actual nonempty supports. Thus the equilibrium set is a finite union of projections of these products; the projection forgets $u,v$, which are uniquely determined by $y,x$ respectively. Vertices of each nonempty rational polytope have polynomial bit length in the input size. Indeed a vertex is determined by a square full-rank subsystem of active linear constraints; clearing rational denominators and applying Cramer's rule expresses its coordinates as ratios of integer determinants. The determinant expansion bounds their bit lengths by the dimension times the maximum coefficient bit length plus the bit length of the factorial of that dimension, all polynomial in the original input length. A bounded nonempty polytope is the convex hull of its vertices. If its projection contains two distinct points, it therefore has two vertices with distinct projections. If the whole finite union contains two points, either one product has two such vertices or two different products supply distinct projected vertices. Hence two distinct equilibria with polynomial-length rational coordinates exist whenever uniqueness fails. A verifier checks the two probability vectors in each candidate, their distinctness, and that every action used with positive probability achieves the maximum expected payoff against the other strategy. All are polynomially many exact rational arithmetic comparisons on polynomial-length numbers. This verifies the certificate and proves membership. ∎
Rank one alone does not force uniqueness. Put $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $B=J-A$. Adding the constant 1 to the column player's zero-sum payoffs changes no best response, so this matching-pennies game has the unique equilibrium $(1/2,1/2)$ for both players; the indifference equations directly give these probabilities. By contrast, $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $B=J-A$ have every pair consisting of the first row and any column mixture as an equilibrium. In both cases $A+B=J$ has rank one.

Verification and remaining gaps

The two examples were checked by exact best-response equations and rational-grid enumeration. The certificate proof was audited for degenerate games: positive-dimensional equilibrium sets still contain distinct rational points through the support-polytope argument. Exhaustively visiting all support pairs involves exponentially many pairs, so it supplies no polynomial uniqueness algorithm. No reduction proving co-NP-hardness is constructed; membership and examples are known auxiliary facts, counted as zero original-target progress.
