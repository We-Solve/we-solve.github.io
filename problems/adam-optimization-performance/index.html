Statement recorded in the supplied source.
Why does Adam work so well? Adam has been shown to outperform gradient descent in optimizing large language transformers empirically, and by a larger margin than on other tasks, but it is unclear why this happens. We show that the heavy-tailed class imbalance found in language modeling tasks leads to difficulties in the optimization dynamics. When training with gradient descent, the loss associated with infrequent words decreases slower than the loss associated with frequent ones. As most samples come from relatively infrequent words, the average loss decreases slowly with gradient descent. On the other hand, Adam and sign-based methods do not suffer from this problem and improve predictions on all classes. To establish that this behavior is indeed caused by class imbalance, we show empirically that it persist through different architectures and data types, on language transformers, vision CNNs, and linear models. We study this phenomenon on a linear classification with cross-entropy loss, showing that heavy-tailed class imbalance leads to ill-conditioning, and that the normalization used by Adam can counteract it. Research attempt: Coordinate normalization and the rare-sample barrier

Consider a full-batch loss \[ F(w)=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^m p_i(w_i-a_i)^2,\qquad p_i>0. \] For gradient descent with constant step $\eta$, the error $e_{t,i}=w_{t,i}-a_i$ satisfies $e_{t+1,i}=(1-\eta p_i)e_{t,i}$. Thus when $0<\eta p_i<1$, the time to reduce its magnitude by a factor $\rho\in(0,1)$ is at least $\log\rho/\log(1-\eta p_i)$, which diverges as $p_i\downarrow0$. A step stable in every coordinate cannot simply be increased without regard to the largest $p_i$: if $\eta p_j>2$, nonzero error in coordinate $j$ grows geometrically.

Now consider bias-corrected Adam without weight decay, with $m_0=v_0=0$, $0\leq\beta_1,\beta_2<1$, and positive learning rates. For this full-batch separable loss, take denominator $\sqrt{\widehat v_{t,i}}$ with zero additive stabilization parameter. Compare runs for positive weights $p_i$ and for all weights equal to one, from the same initialization. Whenever updates are defined, induction gives identical $w$ in the two runs and \[ m_{t,i}=p_i m^{(1)}_{t,i},\qquad v_{t,i}=p_i^2v^{(1)}_{t,i}. \] Indeed, at an identical iterate $g_{t,i}=p_i e_{t,i}$, the moving-average recursions preserve these identities; bias corrections are independent of $p_i$; their ratio cancels $p_i$. At an identically zero coordinate gradient history set the zero-over-zero update to zero in both runs. This proves exact coordinate-scale invariance in this particular deterministic model.

The usual positive stabilization parameter $\epsilon$ breaks exact cancellation. Already the first update is \[ -\eta\frac{p_i e_{0,i}}{p_i|e_{0,i}|+\epsilon}. \] For fixed nonzero error and $\epsilon>0$ it tends to zero as $p_i\downarrow0$. Nor does normalization imply convergence with a fixed step: for sign descent on the scalar quadratic and $0<e_0<\eta$, the errors alternate between $e_0$ and $e_0-\eta$, never tending to zero. This is also the Adam update with $\beta_1=\beta_2=0$, $\epsilon=0$.

A sampling obstruction.
Suppose a rare category occurs independently with probability $p$ on each training draw, its unknown target bit is equally likely 0 or 1, and all nonrare examples and side information are independent of that bit. After $n$ draws the category is unobserved with probability $(1-p)^n$. Conditional on that event every learner, normalized or otherwise, has error $1/2$ when tested on the rare category. Therefore its average conditional rare-category risk is at least $\tfrac12(1-p)^n$, and one fixed target bit attains that lower bound. Normalization can cancel deterministic curvature weights in the preceding model but cannot manufacture absent observations. Applying the scale-invariance calculation directly to stochastic rare-token arrival would therefore be an invalid inference.

Verification and adversarial audit

The weighted and unweighted Adam trajectories are compared inductively, not by comparing gradients at unrelated iterates. Zero denominators receive an explicit convention only in the auxiliary zero-$\epsilon$ model. The sign-descent cycle checks that invariant speed is different from convergence. The stochastic lower bound is conditional category risk; overall population risk multiplies it by $p$.
Audit corrections.
Separated the zero-stabilizer full-batch invariance from practical positive-$\epsilon$ behavior and from the unavoidable rare-observation bottleneck.
