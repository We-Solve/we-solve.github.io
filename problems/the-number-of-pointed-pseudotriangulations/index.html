CS-97 · Math-1664

The Number of Pointed Pseudotriangulations

Status Open · unsolvedmath.comLLM status Open unverified

Top Open Problems openaigpt 6 astra ultra 0% cataloguedOpenOpen

Statement

Definitions and mathematical statement

Let $S\subset\mathbb R^2$ be a finite set of $n\ge3$ points with no three collinear. A straight-line triangulation of $S$ subdivides $\operatorname{conv}(S)$ into triangles using every point of $S$ as a vertex and no other vertices. Let $t(S)$ be its number of distinct triangulations, counted by their edge sets.

A pseudotriangle is a simple polygon with exactly three vertices whose interior angles are less than $\pi$; its remaining vertices are reflex. A pseudotriangulation of $S$ is a noncrossing straight-line subdivision of the same convex hull into pseudotriangles with vertex set exactly $S$. It is pointed when, at each vertex, all incident edge directions lie in an open angular interval of length $\pi$. Equivalently, some incident face sector has angle greater than $\pi$, including the exterior sector at a hull vertex.

Let $p(S)$ count pointed pseudotriangulations by their edge sets. The conjecture is \[ p(S)\ge t(S). \] The stronger equality assertion in the source predicts equality precisely when every point of $S$ is a vertex of its convex hull. Pointedness is required at all vertices, not merely at the three convex corners of each pseudotriangle.

Short English statement

Does every planar point set have at least as many pointed pseudotriangulations as ordinary triangulations, with equality only for points in convex position?

Research attempt: Testing refinement as a counting injection

For convex-position $S$, any simple polygon whose vertices are a subset of $S$ in boundary order is convex. A bounded face of a noncrossing subdivision therefore has no reflex corner. If it is a pseudotriangle, it has exactly three vertices and is a triangle. Moreover at every hull vertex all incident edges lie within the hull's angle, strictly less than $\pi$. Thus pointed pseudotriangulations and triangulations coincide in this case.

The attempted proof for interior points was to remove edges from each triangulation until it becomes pointed, and claim different triangulations give different remnants. The following exact collision shows that refinement alone cannot justify injectivity. Set \[ A=(0,0),\ B=(6,0),\ C=(0,6),\ p=(1,2),\ q=(2,1). \] Let $H$ consist of the boundary edges $AB,BC,CA$ and the four edges $Ap,pC,Aq,qB$. Its bounded faces are triangles $AqB$ and $ACp$, and the pentagon with boundary $A,q,B,C,p$. In that pentagon, $A,B,C$ are convex and $p,q$ reflex, as is seen by the signed successive cross products; hence it is a pseudotriangle. At $p$ the two incident directions point strictly into the left half-plane, and at $q$ into the lower half-plane. Hull vertices are pointed automatically. Thus $H$ is a pointed pseudotriangulation.

The segment $pq$ lies in the central face and separates triangle $Apq$ from the convex quadrilateral $qBCp$. That quadrilateral can be triangulated either with $pB$ or with $qC$. Therefore \[ T_1=H\cup\{pq,pB\},\qquad T_2=H\cup\{pq,qC\} \] are distinct triangulations refining exactly the same pointed pseudotriangulation. The quadrilateral is strictly convex by the four orientation tests, so both refinements are legal. All five input points are in general position. Deleting the two specified edges from either $T_i$ gives $H$.

This does not prove that every possible edge-removal rule has collisions; a carefully designed global injection could choose different pointed outputs. It does prove that the mere existence of pointed subgraphs or triangulating each pseudotriangle cannot be counted as an injection. In a bipartite incidence graph between triangulations and their pointed subgraphs, positive degree on one side is insufficient to produce a matching. Even the two vertices $T_1,T_2$ may share a candidate neighbor, as here; proving a matching would need additional information about all their available neighbors.

The convex equality case and the explicit collision therefore leave both the general inequality and the strict inequality for every nonconvex-position set unproved. In particular counting refinements overcounts the triangulations in a way that cannot be discarded without a quantitative multiplicity argument.
