Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.

In the source model a finite-dimensional quantum function $F:X\to A$ assigns positive-semidefinite $d\times d$ measurement operators $M_{a\mid x}$ satisfying $\sum_{a\in A}M_{a\mid x}^2=I$ for each $x$. Sequential query probabilities are $d^{-1}\|M_{a_1\mid x_1}\cdots M_{a_q\mid x_q}\|_{\mathrm F}^2$, with the order interpreted as in the source. The function is commutative if all its measurement operators commute. A property is a class of such functions; a property tester seeks to distinguish membership from being far from the class using few queries. The algebra generated by the measurements is the smallest unital complex matrix algebra containing the operators and their adjoints. A test forces commutativity or an algebraic property when every perfectly passing strategy has that property under the test's specified equivalence.

Verbatim supplied catalogue question

Scope of the attempted construction

The primary report, printed page 2974, asks several questions: characterizing tests forcing commutativity, testing graph properties, forcing specified algebras, and applications. We construct one exact three-query test in the literal positive measurement model. Its perfect-passing condition forces the stronger property of projective commutativity. It is therefore neither a characterization of all such tests nor a tester accepting every commuting positive-operator family. The distinction matters: commuting scalar fair-coin measurements are not projective and do not repeat their outcomes with certainty.

Two repetitions force projectivity

Take a finite input alphabet $X$ and finite output alphabet $A$, and positive operators $M_{a\mid x}$ with $\sum_aM_{a\mid x}^2=I$. Query $x$ twice and require the outputs to agree. Perfect passing means for every $a\ne b$ \[ 0=d^{-1}\|M_{a\mid x}M_{b\mid x}\|_{\rm F}^2. \] Thus the product is zero. Multiply the normalization by $M_{a\mid x}$ on the left; all other terms vanish and give $M_{a\mid x}^3=M_{a\mid x}$. Since this is a positive Hermitian matrix, each eigenvalue satisfies $t\ge0$ and $t^3=t$, hence is zero or one. The operators are therefore orthogonal projectors summing to the identity. Conversely a projective measurement repeats perfectly by orthogonality. Sampling every input with positive probability makes perfect passing enforce this conclusion for every input separately.

A three-query test forces exact pairwise commutativity

After establishing projectivity, write the projectors for two queries as $P_a,Q_b$. Query in the order $x,y,x$ and accept when the two outer outcomes agree. For $a\ne c$, the probability of outcomes $a,b,c$ is $d^{-1}\|P_aQ_bP_c\|_{\rm F}^2$. Perfect passing implies all such off-diagonal blocks vanish. Since $\sum_aP_a=I$, it follows that \[ Q_b=\sum_aP_aQ_bP_a, \] and hence $Q_b$ commutes with every $P_a$. Conversely, if the projectors commute, $P_aQ_bP_c=Q_bP_aP_c=0$ for $a\ne c$. Choose, with positive probability, a repeat test for each input and an outer-repeat test for each ordered input pair. Each branch uses at most three queries, and the combined tester passes with probability one exactly when all measurements are commuting projective measurements.

For already projective measurements the rejection probability for a fixed pair has the exact identity \[ \Pr\{a\ne c\} =\frac1{2d}\sum_{a,b}\|[P_a,Q_b]\|_{\rm F}^2. \] Indeed, for fixed $a,b$, the blocks $P_aQ_bP_c$, $c\ne a$, are orthogonal in the Frobenius inner product and sum to $P_aQ_b(I-P_a)$. The commutator is $P_aQ_b(I-P_a)-(I-P_a)Q_bP_a$; its two terms are adjoints supported on opposite blocks, so its squared Frobenius norm is twice the first term's squared norm. Summing proves the identity. An average rejection bound therefore controls an average of squared commutator norms. It does not by itself construct a nearby exactly commuting family, especially for many measurements or a different metric.

A precisely encoded graph consequence

Let $G=(V,E)$ be a finite graph without loops, and encode a $c$-colour assignment by a quantum function with inputs the vertices and outputs $\{1,\ldots,c\}$. Add tests that query the two endpoints of each edge and require different outputs, while retaining all repeat and commutativity tests above. A perfectly passing finite-dimensional strategy exists if and only if $G$ has a proper classical $c$-colouring.

For the nontrivial implication, the preceding proof makes all operators commuting projectors. Their common eigenbasis exists by repeatedly decomposing invariant eigenspaces. At every basis coordinate and every vertex exactly one colour projector has diagonal entry one, defining a deterministic colour for that vertex. The probability of an equal-colour edge is the number of coordinates with that violation divided by $d$, as follows by taking the trace of the two corresponding projectors. Perfect passing means that count is zero for every edge. Thus every coordinate defines a proper colouring; at least one coordinate exists since $d\ge1$. Conversely a proper colouring is a one-dimensional strategy with projectors zero or one and passes every test. Without the added global commutativity tests, this argument does not establish the same equivalence.

Why this does not force an arbitrary prescribed algebra

A commuting family generates a commutative algebra. More explicitly its joint eigenspaces group basis coordinates with identical joint eigenvalues, and the generated unital algebra is isomorphic to $\mathbb C^r$ for the number $r$ of distinct joint patterns. To justify that all pattern indicators lie in the algebra, for any two distinct patterns choose one projector on which they differ; products of that projector or its complement isolate each pattern. The resulting orthogonal idempotents span exactly all functions of the joint pattern. Our test fixes neither $r$ nor the multiplicities of these patterns, and cannot enforce a noncommutative matrix algebra. Tensoring all measurements with an identity preserves every sequential probability, so even exact Hilbert-space dimension cannot be determined from these transcripts.
