\section{A Cycle Whose Length Is a Power of Two}\label{1417}

{\small\sffamily UnsolvedMath ID 1417 · Graph Theory}

\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}Unless a section says otherwise, $\N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$,
$\Z$ is the ring of integers, $\Q,\R,\C$ are the rational, real, and complex
fields, $[N]=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor N\rfloor\}$, and $\log$ is the natural
logarithm. For real functions of a parameter tending to infinity,
$f\ll g$ and $f=O(g)$ mean $|f|\leq Cg$ eventually for some fixed $C$;
$f\gg g$ reverses this comparison; $f=o(g)$ means $f/g\to0$;
$f\sim g$ means $f/g\to1$; and $f\asymp g$ means both order bounds.
A subscript on $O$ or $\ll$ specifies allowed constant dependence.

Use finite simple graphs. The hypothesis is minimum degree at least three. A cycle is simple, so the possible power-of-two lengths start at $2^2=4$. The required exponent may depend on the graph; there is no proposed universal upper bound on that exponent. \sref{1417}{1} \sref{1417}{2}

\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
Does every graph with minimum degree 3 contain cycles of lengths that are powers of 2? \sref{1417}{1}

\paragraph{Residual task reported by the archive.}

The embedded review (2026-08-17) records: Settle the cubic/small-minimum-degree regime. \sref{1417}{1}

\subsection{Short English statement}

Must every finite graph of minimum degree at least three contain a cycle of length four, eight, sixteen, or another power of two?
