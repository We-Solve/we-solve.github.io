\section{Seymour's self-minor conjecture}\label{statement}

\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}$\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, and $\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$ denote the integers, rationals, reals and complex numbers. Any other conventions are specified locally.


For graphs $H$ and $G$, a minor model consists of pairwise disjoint nonempty connected vertex sets $B_v\subseteq V(G)$, one for each $v\in V(H)$, with an edge between $B_u$ and $B_v$ whenever $uv\in E(H)$. A proper minor model does not merely reproduce the whole graph with singleton branch sets and every edge: it deletes a vertex or edge, or contracts a nontrivial connected branch set. Thus a proper self-minor is a proper minor model of a graph isomorphic to $G$. This does not require the resulting graph to be nonisomorphic to $G$.
\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
Does every infinite graph have a proper minor isomorphic to itself? The source also isolates the countably infinite case of the same question after reporting that Oporowski's counterexample to the unrestricted assertion is uncountable. \sref{statement}{2}
\subsection{Short English statement}
Can every infinite graph be obtained, up to isomorphism, by a nontrivial minor operation on itself, and does this at least hold for countably infinite graphs?
