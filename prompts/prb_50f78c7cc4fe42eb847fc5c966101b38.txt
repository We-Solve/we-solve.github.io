\section{Bounded-Step Escape Through Gaussian Primes}\label{statement}


\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}Unless a section says otherwise, $\N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$,
$\Z$ is the ring of integers, $\Q,\R,\C$ are the rational, real, and complex
fields, $[N]=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor N\rfloor\}$, and $\log$ is the natural
logarithm. For real functions of a parameter tending to infinity,
$f\ll g$ and $f=O(g)$ mean $|f|\leq Cg$ eventually for some fixed $C$;
$f\gg g$ reverses this comparison; $f=o(g)$ means $f/g\to0$;
$f\sim g$ means $f/g\to1$; and $f\asymp g$ means both order bounds.
A subscript on $O$ or $\ll$ specifies allowed constant dependence.
The expression $n^{o(1)}$ in an upper bound means growth smaller than
$n^\epsilon$ for each fixed $\epsilon>0$. Local definitions govern any
exception, finite-field convention, or use of the same symbol for another
object. An asymptotic claim is not automatically an effective algorithm.



The Gaussian integers are $\Z[i]$. A Gaussian prime is a nonzero nonunit irreducible element of this ring; associates are allowed as different lattice points. The question asks for $M<\infty$ and an infinite sequence $z_j$ of Gaussian primes, starting within bounded distance of the origin, with $|z_{j+1}-z_j|\leq M$ and $|z_j|\to\infty$. The origin is the starting location, not itself a Gaussian prime. \sref{statement}{1} \sref{statement}{2}

\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
Can one walk from the origin to infinity using Gaussian primes as stepping stones and taking steps of bounded length? \sref{statement}{1}

\paragraph{Residual task reported by the archive.}

The embedded review (2026-08-17) records: Either drive the path exponent to zero with a uniform constant or prove arbitrarily wide Gaussian-prime moats. \sref{statement}{1}

\subsection{Short English statement}

Can one travel arbitrarily far through Gaussian-prime lattice points without ever needing an unbounded step?
