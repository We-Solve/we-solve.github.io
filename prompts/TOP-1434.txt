\section{Representation Number 3 Classification}\label{1434}
{\small\sffamily UnsolvedMath ID 1434 · Graph Theory}
\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}$\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, and $\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$ denote the integers, rationals, reals and complex numbers. Any other conventions are specified locally.


Let $G=(V,E)$ be a finite simple undirected graph. A word over $V$ is a finite sequence of vertices in which every vertex occurs. For distinct $x,y\in V$, let $w|_{\{x,y\}}$ be the word obtained by deleting every letter other than $x,y$. They alternate when this restriction has no equal consecutive letters, that is, it has the form $xyxy\cdots$ or $yxyx\cdots$, of either even or odd length. The word $w$ represents $G$ when, for every distinct $x,y$,
\[
 xy\in E\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x,y\text{ alternate in }w.
\]
Write $\mathrm{WR}(G)$ when such a word exists.

A word is $k$-uniform when every vertex occurs exactly $k$ times. For a word-representable graph its representation number is
\[
 \rho(G)=\min\{k\in\mathbb N:\text{some }k\text{-uniform word represents }G\}.
\]
Every word-representable graph admits a uniform representing word, so this minimum is defined. Uniform word length is $k|V|$; arbitrary repeated representing words are not used to measure the minimum.


\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
Classify, up to isomorphism, all finite graphs in
\[
 \mathcal R_3=\{G:\rho(G)=3\}.
\]
Equivalently, give a structural necessary-and-sufficient criterion for existence of a $3$-uniform representing word and nonexistence of both $1$-uniform and $2$-uniform representing words. The classification is unrestricted by graph subclass. \sref{1434}{2}
\subsection{Short English statement}
Describe exactly the graphs that can be represented using three copies of every vertex but cannot be represented using one or two copies.
