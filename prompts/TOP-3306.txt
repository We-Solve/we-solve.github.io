\section{End-Devouring Rays}\label{3306}
{\small\sffamily UnsolvedMath ID 3306 · Graph Theory}
\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}$\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, and $\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$ denote the integers, rationals, reals and complex numbers. Any other conventions are specified locally.


A ray is a one-way infinite simple path, equipped with its first (starting) vertex. Two rays belong to the same end of a graph $G$ if, after removing any finite set of vertices, their tails lie in the same component. An $\omega$-ray represents the end $\omega$. A family of rays devours $\omega$ if every $\omega$-ray intersects at least one family member. An end is countable in the convention of the source if some countable family of rays devours it; the graph itself need not be countable.
\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
For every graph $G$, every countable end $\omega$, and every infinite family $K$ of pairwise vertex-disjoint $\omega$-rays, does there exist a family $K'$ of pairwise vertex-disjoint $\omega$-rays which devours $\omega$ and whose set of starting vertices is exactly that of $K$? \sref{3306}{2} \sref{3306}{3}
\subsection{Short English statement}
Can any prescribed disjoint infinite family of rays in a countable end be replaced, retaining exactly its starting vertices, by disjoint rays meeting every ray in that end?
