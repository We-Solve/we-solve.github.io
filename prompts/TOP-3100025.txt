\section*{A graph G is said to be uniquely H-saturated if it contains no H, but adding any edge to G creates exactly one copy of H}\label{3100025}
{\small UnsolvedMath ID 3100025 · AMR-030-0025 · Combinatorics · AMR Open Problem Lists}
\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}
Fix an integer $r\ge3$. A finite simple graph $G$ is uniquely
$K_r$-saturated if it contains no copy of the complete graph $K_r$,
but adding any missing edge creates exactly one copy of $K_r$.
Copies are counted by their vertex sets, and every newly created
copy necessarily uses the added edge. A dominating vertex is one
adjacent to every other vertex.

Call a uniquely $K_r$-saturated graph sporadic here if it has no
dominating vertex. Cooper's finiteness question is whether, for
every fixed $r\ge3$, there are only finitely many isomorphism
classes of sporadic uniquely $K_r$-saturated graphs. Equivalently,
does there exist a bound $N(r)$ such that
\[
          |V(G)|\le N(r)
\]
for every such graph without a dominating vertex?

The bound may depend on $r$ but not on the graph. Requiring exactly
one newly formed clique is stronger than ordinary saturation,
which merely requires at least one. The absence of a dominating
vertex removes familiar infinite families and is part of the
question, not a cosmetic normalization. The restriction $r\ge3$
also matters: for $r=2$, edgeless graphs of arbitrary order already
make the analogous finiteness assertion false. Counting labeled
graphs rather than isomorphism types does not change finiteness
once their orders are bounded.
\subsection{Short English statement}
For each fixed r at least three, are there finitely many uniquely Kr-saturated graphs without a dominating vertex?
